# Motion and finishing touches: the Site consultant's reference

**Audience:** the Site consultant. Read this when you reach finishing touches, when you offer the
signature effect for the first draft, or whenever a client asks about a hero video, a living photo,
animation or movement on their site. It's the working part of the motion survey ([[DOC-72]]) and the
finishing-touches stage ([[DOC-71]] §12.9).

**The point of finishing touches:** a story-carrying effect is one of the main differences between a
cheap builder site and a premium build. Fade-ins are a commodity; a photo of the client's own fire,
steam or shopfront brought to life isn't. The failure mode is **overdoing it**.

## 1. How to offer it

**Baseline: one signature effect, plus entrance reveals.** Nothing more unless it passes the taste
tests below.

**The signature effect goes in the first draft,** but you discuss it with the client first. Make it
from their own photos where you can, and say what you'd do in plain words: "Your photo of the
counter: I could have the coffee steam gently and the bell catch the light. Want to see it?"
- **Cohort zero** (directed, new to websites): a short, enthusiastic suggestion, then go ahead unless
  they object.
- **Client-driven:** offer two or three options from the catalogue with your recommendation and why.

The hero is the most stable part of the page once the comparison board and story board are done, so
the clip can be made while the rest of the site is built.

**Everything else waits for the end:** secondary effects, and reveals tuned to the final layout.
Motion on sections that are still changing is wasted work.

## 2. The pattern catalogue

Use these names with the client; they're the vocabulary for talking about motion.

| Pattern | What it is | When to offer it | Watch for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Living photo** (cinemagraph) | A still where one element moves: water shimmering, steam rising, leaves stirring, a flame. A loch that barely moves under a hotel; steam off a barber's hot towel; a welder's fire. | The default signature for almost anyone with one strong photo. | The moving element needs room in the frame. Keep the motion subtle. |
| **Reveal, then rest** | One action that ends on the real photo and holds there. A pepper grinder twisting over a plate of steak and eggs. | Food, products, the moment something is finished. | A clean last frame; account for the end state (§5). |
| **Reveal, then gentle loop** | The reveal, followed by a quiet ongoing loop. The grinder, then the coffee steaming. | One image with both a moment and an ambient element. | The loop must be quieter than the reveal. |
| **Ping-pong place shot** | A slow camera move along the shopfront or room, forward then back. A barbershop front in black and white. | Businesses where the place is the brand: barbers, cafés, shops. | Wrong for anything that visibly runs backwards: fire, pouring, falling. |
| **Ambient band** | A shallow strip of motion with a call to action over it, not full-screen. A shopfront strip under "Visit our store". | Shops that want people to come in. | Keep it a strip; put the text over the calmest part. |
| **Craft clip** | Hands and tools in a tight crop, the best 4–8 seconds. Hands chiselling a chair leg; knotting a chain; a pour-over. | Makers, food, trades. | Wide lifestyle footage weakens a "handmade" claim. |
| **Scroll-scrubbed moment** | The visitor's scrolling plays one gesture forward. A lemon twist over a martini. | One premium moment, handled carefully. | Borders on hijacking the scroll. At most one per site. |
| **Drawn brand motion** | The logo or signature draws itself on; illustrated loops. A roaster's signature writing itself over the hero. | Only when the client brings the drawing, or asks for it knowing the limits in §6. Don't offer it. | Plays once. You can't build it reliably yet (§6). |
| **Hover-to-play** | Still until the pointer is over it. A grid of illustrated beer labels. | Grids of illustrations or products. | On a phone it plays once when it comes into view. |
| **Trailer loop + Play button** | A short silent loop, with the full film (with sound) one click away. A composer's teaser. | Musicians, venues, anyone with a real film. | Never the full film as the background. |
| **Animated page element** | Something already on the page, usually a background, brought to life by picturing it as a still and turning the still into a video (§5). A colour glow that becomes drifting smoke (the 1st Contact home page); a texture that shimmers; a backdrop or pattern that slowly moves. | Whenever the client wants something on the page to move, especially a background. Your first choice over drawn or coded animation. | Keep it slow and quiet behind text. It must fade to nothing well inside its edges, or a box shows. |
| **Entrance reveals** (baseline) | Headings and images fade or rise in as they appear. | Everyone, quietly. | On every heading it feels templated. |

Nobody in the welding, fabrication or pottery trades was found using motion at all. For those clients
a living photo of their own work is a genuine way to stand out.

## 3. The four taste tests

Every effect you propose must pass all four.

1. **The motion carries the story or the brand.** It shows the thing they make or do, or the look the
   brand is built on. A light show that shows no croissant, or a drone shot that sells the videographer,
   fails.
2. **Clearly real or clearly art, never the uncanny middle.**
   - Real must look **chosen**: graded, cropped, trimmed. A reposted phone clip isn't enough, and
     footage that barely moves is dull for its cost.
   - Drawn and illustrated motion is plainly art and avoids the uncanny problem, but it's the motion you're worst at making (§6).
   - An animated page element (a background, a texture, smoke or haze) is plainly atmosphere, not a claim about the client, so it passes.
   - Photoreal AI only of **the client's own subject**: their photo, animated believably for that real
     object. Never a generated place or person that isn't theirs.
3. **One motion language.** The baseline is one signature effect plus entrance reveals. A second
   motion is allowed only in the same language (the same grade, colour and pace) and away from the
   first, never competing loops in view at once. Protect the text: fading the clip into the page
   background under the text beats a flat dark overlay.
4. **It still works when the motion never plays.** On a phone in low-power mode, with reduced motion
   turned on, or on a slow connection, the visitor sees only the still. That still must be the real
   photo and tell the story on its own.

## 4. Don'ts

- Don't put a whole film in the background; cut the best 4–8 seconds and link the film.
- Don't use heavy clips; a hero clip is a few seconds, small and silent.
- Don't put an intro or splash video in front of the hours and the menu.
- Don't put motion in the navigation or on every section.
- Don't use photoreal AI of a place or person that isn't the client's.
- Don't use reposted social reels or screen recordings as hero footage.
- Don't slow clips to half speed or filter the colour away.
- Don't run motion on a stale page; it draws attention to the neglect.
- Don't hijack or drag the scroll for its own sake.
- Don't use sound.
- Don't strobe or glitch faster than three flashes a second.

## 5. Making the clip

- **A reveal that ends on the original photo, then quieter ongoing motion.** The strong action plays
  once; continuous motion stays gentle (steam, shimmer), because strong motion that keeps going wears
  out: "I couldn't leave that page open."
- **A clean last frame:** no unfinished action and no motion blur.
- **Account for the end state.** Generating from the finished photo adds to it: animating pepper being
  ground over eggs that were already peppered ends with twice the pepper. Plan the motion so the last
  frame is the photo the client gave you.
- **Room in the frame for the moving element.** Steam needs air above the cup; a flame needs space
  around it.
- **Strong motion plays once.** Only subtle motion loops.

### Animating something on the page: picture it, then animate the picture

**The general move:** when something on the page should move, especially a background, don't draw or code the motion. Make a still picture of that element on its own, turn the still into a video with AnimateImage, and put the video where the element was. Smoke is one example; the same works for a glow, a texture, a pattern, a backdrop, a sky, water or any surface. The operator suggested it, and on the 1st Contact home page it worked first time after several failed SVG attempts at the same smoke (2026-10-31).

1. **Picture the element alone.** Look at it on the page with `screenshot`. AnimateImage takes a Library picture, and a screenshot isn't one, so make the still with CreateImage (or EditImage from a Library picture) to match what you see. Put it on a **perfectly flat background in the page's exact background colour**, by hex (e.g. `#fbfaf7`), with a wide plain margin at every edge and nothing touching it, and no text in it. Keep it pale and low in contrast if text will sit on top. Look at the still before going on.
2. **Animate it with AnimateImage:** `mode: loop` for ongoing motion, `camera: locked`. Describe the movement in plain words ("slowly curls and drifts like smoke in still air", "the light shifts gently across the surface") and say the background stays flat and unchanged at every edge.
3. **Place the clip and its last frame** (as the poster) on the site, and have the builder put the video where the element was, behind the content.
4. **Fade the video out well before its edges.** A generated background is never exactly the page colour, so a clip that runs to its edges shows a faint box. Remove the element the clip replaces, so it isn't there twice.
5. **Tell the client you can't watch it play.** Your checks see still frames. Ask them to open the page in a new tab and watch a few loops, looking for a box edge, the strength and the kind of movement.

### A camera move around a still product: say only what the camera does

**The problem:** asked to pan the camera around a product photo, the generator tends to slide the product across its surface instead, and a ping-pong loop then plays that slide forwards and backwards so the pieces look like they're dancing. A single photo gives it no depth, so it guesses, and the more an object is described the more likely it is to move it.

**What failed (a jewellery hero, 2026-11-04):** two pingpong attempts whose motion text named the pieces in detail ("the camera drifts … around the two gold ear tunnels … the jewellery stays solid", then "Only the camera moves … the two gold earrings stay exactly where they rest on the granite, never sliding, rotating, lifting …"). Both slid the earrings across the stone. Telling it what the objects must *not* do still names them, and still made them move.

**What worked (third attempt, accepted by the client):** `mode: pingpong`, `camera: slow_orbit`, `duration: 5`, `resolution: 540p`, with this motion text:

> A still-life product photograph; the camera alone trucks very slowly to the right on a slider, a small distance, with gentle parallax. The scene is frozen like a sculpture; only the reflections of sunlight glide across the polished gold as the viewing angle changes.

The rules behind it:
1. **Call it a still-life product photograph.** That sets the expectation that nothing in the scene moves.
2. **Describe only the camera, in film terms:** it *trucks* (moves sideways) *on a slider*, *very slowly*, *a small distance*, *with gentle parallax*. Small and slow is what keeps it believable.
3. **Never name or describe the objects.** Refer to the whole scene ("frozen like a sculpture"), not to the pieces.
4. **Allow exactly one thing to change: the light.** Reflections gliding across the material as the angle changes are what make it look like a camera move, and are what the client liked.

**If it still slides the objects,** don't retry the same text a third time. Offer, in order: a slow push-in and pull-back (a move towards the subject; untested here, but the likelier camera move to hold the scene still), then `camera: locked` with only the light moving, then the still photo.

**Slower means longer, and length has a budget.** A client who wants it smoother and slower is asking for a longer clip. Under the 2 MB clip budget a pingpong at 540p fits about 6 s (BUG-347, until lagrange-framework REQ-237's lower-bitrate encode lands). Offer "slower" within that length, e.g. a smaller move over the same seconds, rather than a 10 s attempt that will be refused after several paid minutes.

**Not for lettering or logos.** Video redraws shapes frame by frame, so letters wobble, and the clip has to hand over to the real wordmark at the end. Animate a wordmark as one piece instead (§6).

## 6. What you can deliver reliably, and what you can't

**Video effects are your strength.** Living photos, reveals and animated page elements made with AnimateImage have consistently looked excellent. Lead with them.

**Drawn and animated SVG is not, yet.** You can't see motion, and an SVG effect depends on details you can't check: each device draws its own fonts, so letters positioned in your test browser land differently on the client's; an animation that hands over to the real element at the end jumps or changes weight; gradients and edges that look soft in one still band in another. In practice this has meant poor results and long, painful back-and-forth (1st Contact, 2026-10-31: drawn smoke wisps with hard edges; a letter-by-letter wordmark unfold that jumped at the end on the client's Mac).

- **Don't offer drawn or animated SVG effects** (drawn smoke or texture, a logo drawing itself on, illustrated loops, letters moving one by one). Your enthusiasm here runs ahead of what you can deliver.
- **For anything on the page that should move, offer the picture-then-animate video** (§5).
- **To animate a wordmark or heading, move the real element as one piece:** a fade and a small rise or settle, once. Never split it into letters or swap a drawn copy for the real one.
- **If the client asks for an SVG effect,** say plainly that it's the least reliable thing you make and you can't watch it play. Offer the video or whole-element version first. If they still want it, keep it simple and say what they'll need to check.
- **Static drawings are a different matter:** a simple wordmark or icon is fine. But a drawing is a poor share card, because many social and messaging apps don't show an SVG link preview. Use a PNG there.
