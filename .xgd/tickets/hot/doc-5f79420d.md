# Identity, Accounts & Entitlement

## What this covers

Who a person is, which account they operate, and what that account is allowed to
use. It is the model behind logging in to 1st Contact and behind every later
question about billing, trials, comped access and support.

It does **not** cover payments. [[DOC-40]] deliberately stops at "what access
does this account have, and why" — the money that produces some of those
answers is a separate concern, for the reason recorded in §5.

## 1. The gap this closes

Until now the builder served exactly one operator. `apps/control-app` names its
tenant in a deployment var (`TENANT_ID`), Cloudflare Access gates the hostname,
and the Worker re-verifies the Access JWT ([[REQ-147]], `access.ts`). That is a
complete design for **one** user and has no room for a second: two people
logging in would land in the same tenant and edit each other's sites.

Nothing persists about a person either. Access proves an email address on every
request and stores nothing — there is no record of who exists, when they joined,
or what they agreed to.

## 2. Four nouns

An earlier draft of this document said *"**Account** — a tenant"*, collapsing two
levels into one. They are separate, and the rest of §2 is what they are.

**Business** — the unit of isolation. Spelled `tenant` in the schema and
**Business** in the product. A business owns a website (v1: exactly one), a
customer list, a calendar, payments, marketing, monitoring, and the knowledge the
assistant accumulates. It is the hard information barrier ([[DOC-10]] §4.1) and
nothing reaches across it. Ids are **opaque** (`acct_<random>`), never derived
from a name: a tenant id appears in R2 keys (`t/<tenant>/blob/…`,
`chat/<tenant>/…`, `kb/<tenant>/…`) and is therefore permanent, and a readable id
is one rename request away from being a lie. The human label lives in
`tenants.name`, where it can change.

**Account** — the payer, and **not a tenant of its own**. One account may own
several businesses. Lagrange Foundry the account holds *Lagrange Foundry*,
*1st Contact* and *XGD*: three businesses, three websites, three customer lists,
and three assistants that know nothing of each other. Structurally an account is
a `users` row in the platform's own tenant — which is §2.1, and is where the
leverage is.

**User** — a person, identified by a verified email address, scoped to a tenant.
A builder user lives in the platform's own tenant (`1stcontact`); a site
visitor captured by a customer's contact form lives in *that customer's* tenant.

**Membership** — the join. `(user_id, account_id, role)` says this person may
operate that business. `account_id` holds a tenant id and always did; only the
word changed. Every account has at least one: `owner` of the business its invite
created. Support access is an extra row with an `expires_at`.

The separation is what makes an agency account possible later without a
migration: several memberships against one business, rather than an account
column on a user. It is also what makes an account's *second* business free —
one more `tenants` row and one more membership, and no schema change at all.

### 2.1 The platform is a 1st Contact business

**1st Contact is built as a 1st Contact site.** This is a leverage decision, not
an elegance one. Every customer-facing capability the product sells is one the
platform itself needs, and if the two are separate systems then each one gets
built twice.

| A customer's business does this | 1st Contact does the same thing |
| --- | --- |
| captures a visitor as a contact | a prospect lands on the 1st Contact site and becomes a `users` row in the platform tenant |
| sees that contact in their CRM | our pipeline *is* the platform tenant's CRM |
| invites a contact into a portal | the invite that provisions an account (§4) |
| authenticates portal logins | the same Access-verified identity the builder uses (§3) |
| bills a customer from that portal | our billing is the platform business's payments surface |
| books time with a customer | the same scheduling |

So the Account surface a 1st Contact customer sees — their plan, their invoices,
their details — is **not a builder feature**. It is the customer portal of the
1st Contact *site*, rendered by the code that will render the portal our
customers give their own customers. It appears in the builder chrome because
that is where a person looks for it, not because it belongs to the builder.

Two rules follow, and they are the ones a later hand will be tempted to break:

1. **Nothing is built platform-only.** If the platform needs it, it is a product
   capability that the platform happens to be the first tenant of. A bespoke
   admin billing page is the failure mode: it is the same page a customer needs,
   and building it twice forfeits the whole of this section.
2. **Our customers' customers never become tenants.** The recursion is exactly
   two levels deep, by construction. A contact on a customer's site is a `users`
   row in that customer's tenant, with a portal login — never a tenant of their
   own, never an account. Anything else makes tenancy unbounded and the barrier
   meaningless.

### 2.2 Why the boundary is the business and not the website

The alternative considered was the boundary at the *account*, with the business
as a filter over shared data. It was rejected on four counts, and the fourth is
the one that decided it.

- **A shared assistant is a contaminated assistant.** One knowledge base across
  1st Contact and XGD means positioning from one leaking into advice about the
  other, invisibly and unfalsifiably. A blank sheet plus deliberately uploaded
  background is the better product, not a worse one.
- **Shared contacts are the wrong default.** Two businesses that genuinely share
  a customer base are one business with two websites — which is §2.3's shape, not
  this one.
- **Membership wants to be per-business anyway.** "This employee may see the
  salon and not the studio" is a membership row under this model, and a new
  scope column under the other.
- **Splitting is harder than merging.** Merging two well-attributed sets is a
  union. Splitting one commingled set needs attribution that was never recorded,
  and the first call site that forgets its filter has produced a row that can
  never be assigned. Isolation *enforces* attribution; filtering merely relies
  on it.

### 2.3 Several websites in one business — v2, and already representable

`sites (tenant_id, slug)` is already keyed for many sites per tenant ([[DOC-12]]),
and v1 simply holds the invariant of one. That is deliberate, because it is where
the genuinely-shared cases land when they arrive: a business with two brands or
two locations that share one customer list, one calendar and one accumulated
knowledge base. The product ecosystem case — "which customers use product A and
product B" — is served *here*, by one business with several websites, and never
by machinery that crosses the tenant barrier.

Everything already scoped to a tenant therefore stays correct as written,
including [[DOC-10]] §4.1's rule that a client's second site should not start as
cold as their first, and [[DOC-38]] §7.7's one blob backing two sites. Those
statements now describe the inside of a business.

### Contacts are users

A contact captured by a customer's site is a `users` row in that customer's
tenant with no authentication fields set. Not a second table.

The argument is that the two converge anyway. A contact who is later invited to
a client portal must not become a *different* record with a duplicate email, and
a CRM that cannot say "this contact is also a logged-in user" has to answer that
question with a join across two tables that disagree about identity. The
tenant-scoped unique index on `(tenant_id, email)` is the one place identity is
decided, and it covers both.

## 3. Authentication: rented now, owned later

**Now: Cloudflare Access with the One-Time PIN provider.** Access is already
deployed on `app.1stcontact.io` and the Worker already verifies the JWT and
extracts a verified `email` claim. Its OTP provider is functionally the magic
link we would otherwise build, and it needs no email infrastructure of ours,
no sending domain, no token table and no session store. For the alpha cohort
this is the whole authentication system.

**The policy is set to identity-only** — OTP, any email — so the *authorisation*
decision belongs to this platform rather than to a list in the Cloudflare
dashboard. A verified email that holds no entitlement is refused by the Worker.

Two consequences accepted deliberately:

- The OTP email is Cloudflare-branded. Acceptable for an invited alpha, not for
  a public product.
- The Access edge no longer bounds *who may reach the Worker*, only *who may be
  identified*. Denial is server-side. At alpha volumes the lost cost boundary is
  not material; at public-signup volumes it is, and that is one of the triggers
  for the paragraph below.
- The Zero Trust free tier covers 50 seats.

**Later: our own magic link.** An `auth_tokens` and a `sessions` table, an email
provider, a verified sending domain. `users`, `memberships` and `entitlements`
do not change — which is the property this design is buying. The credential
layer is the only thing that is rented.

## 4. Provisioning: the invite does the work

Because the alpha is invite-only and the operator is creating the account
anyway, **everything is provisioned at invite time** and login is pure lookup:

| Invite (admin) | First login (user) |
| --- | --- |
| `users` row in the platform tenant — the account | verified email → user row |
| `tenants` row — their first **business**, opaque id | memberships → which businesses |
| `memberships` row, `owner` | one is selected; any others sit in the switcher |
| `entitlements` row, against the business | entitlement → active? |
| one site in that business | terms accepted? → else interstitial |
| | builder, scoped to the selected business |

An account's *second* business is the same rows minus the user: a `tenants` row,
a membership, an entitlement, a site. It is a self-serve action rather than an
invite, and it needs no schema it does not already have.

There is no just-in-time provisioning. It arrives with self-signup, as a second
entry point writing the same rows.

An invited-but-never-logged-in person is a `users` row with a null
`first_seen_at` — which is to say, a contact. §2 again.

## 5. Entitlement: grants, not flags

An entitlement is **a grant of a plan, for a period, from a source**. Not a
boolean on an account, and not a column that billing overwrites.

```
account_id | plan | source        | status | starts_at | ends_at
acct_x     | pro  | admin_grant   | active | 2026-09-01| 2026-12-01
```

`account_id` here is a **business** (§2), so the grant is per business and the
meter is per business: an account with three of them holds three grants and
receives one invoice, because invoicing rolls up by payer and the payer is the
account. Nothing in this table changes under §2's split — only the word does.

A business accumulates grants over its life — comped, then trial, then
subscription. Effective access is **the best active grant covering now**, not a
single-row lookup. That is the design choice that lets the four product states
land without touching the schema:

| | How it lands |
| --- | --- |
| Anyone can sign up | login's "no entitlement → deny" branch becomes "→ create account + `source='signup'` trial grant" |
| Trial tier | a `plan` value, plus a plan→capability map in code |
| Paid, incl. warning period | a `subscriptions` table (new table, no backfill) whose webhook writes `source='subscription'` rows and moves `status` between `active`/`warning`/`expired` |
| Admin comp, date-bounded | `source='admin_grant'` — **built first** |

`plan` and `status` are unconstrained TEXT. A CHECK constraint here would make
adding `'warning'` a schema migration, which is exactly the cost this model
exists to avoid.

### Access and money are separate

A comped grant is an entitlement with no subscription. A *discounted* one is a
subscription carrying a discount that also produces an entitlement. Putting a
`discount_pct` on the entitlement collapses the two and forces the access check
to understand pricing — the merge that would have to be undone later.

### Expiry must actually expire

A date-bounded grant whose expiry is never evaluated is worse than an open-ended
one, because it was promised as bounded. The check runs at login. For the alpha
an expired grant denies with a message; read-only access to one's own site is
the better product answer and the model supports it unchanged, so it waits for
the payments funnel that gives it a purpose.

## 6. The platform administrator

A `platform_admin` flag on the user row, plus a `PLATFORM_ADMINS` env var for
the console gate. The flag is ambient and that is the point: it works before any
membership row exists, and it cannot lock its holder out of the system that
grants it.

Time-boxed `support` membership rows are the auditable alternative and the
table exists for them from day one. They are not built now because during the
alpha the operator and the administrator are the same person, and an audit trail
of oneself is ceremony. The moment there is a second operator, they are the
answer, and they need no migration.

## 7. The tenant barrier, and what is parked behind it

Cross-tenant reach does not exist in this codebase and that is structural, not
incidental. `SiteStoreRoot` can do exactly one thing — `forTenant`. The ticket
store's scoped handle is *terminal*: `forTenant` on it throws. `BLOBS` is a
separate R2 bucket from `SITES` so that confidential client material sits behind
a binding boundary rather than a path convention.

A **platform operations assistant** — one chat that can correlate across
tenants, "is the bug in tenant X causing the complaint from tenant Y" — is
wanted and is **parked**. Its shape is settled even though it is not built,
because that shape decides what the rest of the system must leave room for.

### It switches tenants; it does not span them

The assistant holds **one ordinary scoped handle at a time** and has a tool that
changes which tenant that is. It does not get a wide handle.

This is strictly better than the platform-scope alternative it replaces, on
three counts:

- **No new query surface.** A wide scope means hand-rolled `WHERE tenant_id`
  predicates in new code, in the one place where a bug is a confidentiality
  breach. Switching calls `forTenant` again — the same terminal, already-tested
  handle every other path uses. `forTenant` is never widened, so no scoped handle
  anywhere becomes one bug away from being global.
- **Existing checks come free**, including `forTenant`'s refusal of an inactive
  tenant, which a second read path would have had to re-implement.
- **Better audit.** A switch is a discrete event with a stated reason. A wide
  scope would need the query layer instrumented to know which tenants a question
  even touched.

It also makes content moderation — the use case that pushes past operational
data into customer content — need no new read surface at all. After a switch the
assistant is an ordinary builder chat in that tenant.

### The barrier moves into the context window

What the design gives up is that the isolation is no longer on the data path.
Correlating X against Y means both in one context, which is the point — but it
means an assistant holding a **write** handle after a switch can write tenant X's
confidential material into tenant Y's site. A cross-tenant leak with zero
cross-tenant reads.

So **read-only is more load-bearing here, not less**. Under a wide read scope the
data could only escape through the transcript; under switching it can escape into
another customer's published site.

Three details that are easy to get wrong and expensive to discover:

1. **The transcript does not follow the switch.** The naive implementation
   archives to `chat/<current tenant>/…` and sprays cross-tenant content into
   customer tenants. The session pins its archive to the platform tenant at open;
   the switch moves the store handle only.
2. **The knowledge index does follow the switch.** `projectKnowledgeFor` derives
   its R2 sources from `indexPrefix(tenantId, …)`. Swapping the store and not the
   vectors scores the right tenant's tickets against the wrong tenant's
   embeddings — silent nonsense, not an error.
3. **Injection can drive navigation.** Customer-supplied material can instruct a
   switch. The defence cannot be *controlling* the switch, because the model will
   be talked into it and the human really is an administrator; the defence is that
   switching is *harmless*, which is to say read-only.

### Breadth needs one small primitive

Pure switching cannot answer "which tenants are seeing this?" without visiting
every tenant in turn — workable at thirty accounts, useless at three thousand.
Nor can it enumerate: neither store root offers `listTenants` today.

So the design pairs a **narrow cross-tenant index query** with the switch tool:
the query reads the ticket store only and returns `(tenant_id, ticket_id,
snippet)` — pointers, not content — and the assistant then switches into whichever
tenant is relevant and reads properly through the ordinary handle. Search wide,
read deep. That confines the only genuinely cross-tenant surface to one auditable
function over operational data.

### It stays a separate assistant

Not a mode on the builder chat. The builder's tool surface never gains a
tenant-switch verb at all, so injection in a client's uploaded PDF cannot reach
one even in principle.

### What this costs the rest of the system today

Nothing. Because the scope is always a single tenant, [[REQ-168]] declares no
platform variant — it only needs resolution to accept an explicit target and
authorise it, which an administrator operating a customer's builder ([[REQ-170]])
requires anyway. The switch tool, if built, calls the same function.

## 8. Erasure and retention

[[DOC-37]] treats erasure as identity severance. Two consequences land here:

1. A `users` row is the severance target. Deleting it must reach the memberships
   and entitlements that reference it, and the account itself if no other
   membership survives.
2. Once the parked operations assistant exists, its transcripts hold
   cross-tenant data in the platform tenant — exactly the kind of place data
   hides from a deletion sweep. Retention-bounding those transcripts is the
   cheapest mitigation and should be decided before it is built, not after.

## 9. Open items

1. **Read-only on expiry** and the payments funnel it belongs to (§5).
2. **The operations assistant** (§7) — parked; content moderation is the trigger.
3. **Seat model for agency accounts** — memberships support it; roles beyond
   `owner`/`support` are undefined.
3b. **Where the account's own record lives.** §2 makes an account a `users` row
   in the platform tenant, which is right for identity and unproven for billing:
   a billing entity name ("Lagrange Foundry Ltd") and a VAT number are not user
   attributes. The `fields` bag holds them until there is reason to do better;
   an `accounts` table is the alternative and would cost `memberships` a column.
4. **Self-signup abuse controls** — once anyone can create an account, the
   Access edge is no longer a cost boundary (§3).
5. **Plan → capability map** — a code constant now; a table if it ever needs to
   vary per account.

## Amendments — 2026-09-04, from [[DOC-42]]

[[DOC-42]] works out the account/business/membership/entitlement model against a
worked example, and in doing so amends four things stated above. **The sections
above are left as written**, deliberately: they are cited as rationale by tickets
whose implementations already reference them, and rewriting them in place would
make those citations point at text their authors never read. The amendments live
here and [[DOC-42]] holds the reasoning.

### To §2 and §2.1 — there is no platform tenant

This document says "the platform tenant" throughout. **It names a kind of tenant
that does not exist.** There is the **1st Contact business**: it owns the 1c
site, and its users are its customers — the same sentence a customer would say
about theirs.

The phrase matters because §2.1 rule 1 forbids building anything platform-only,
and vocabulary that encodes the special case is how the code acquires it anyway.
What is genuinely distinguished about that business is three facts about what it
*does*, none of which is a kind ([[DOC-42]] §8): the app is deployed against it
(`TENANT_ID`), it hosts the others, and its product is other businesses.

`TENANT_ID` is unaffected and stays — it is deployment configuration
([[REQ-180]] D5), not a model concept, and [[REQ-168]] deliberately leaves it two
readers.

### To §5 — the subject of a grant is the account, not the business

**Landed 2026-09-04 by [[REQ-184]]**, in
`db/migrations/0006_entitlement_subject.sql`. §5 below is left as written because
in-flight tickets cite it as rationale; this is what is actually in the schema.

§5 says *"`account_id` here is a business"*, and separates the subject from the
payer by joining memberships. [[DOC-42]] §6 corrects the shape: **an entitlement
grants an account access to a thing.** The subject is the account; the business
is the object. So the column named `account_id` was the one that was not, and the
name would have produced a bug — someone writes a user id into it because the
name says to, and it half-works.

The case that forces it is one level down: two members of one business, one
paying for gated content and one not. That is unrepresentable while the grant
*is* the business.

Per-business capacity and per-account access remain **different grants**, not one
generalised — "this business holds a pro plan" must not require re-granting every
member as they join. So the subject is an addition to the table, and
`account_id` was renamed `business_id` in the same migration rather than two.

Multi-user accounts stay cheap to leave open: the subject id happens to equal a
user id today, as long as nothing asserts that it *is* one.

**The shape, as shipped.** `entitlements` now carries both:

| column | holds | today |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `business_id` | the **object** — a tenant id | every grant names one |
| `account_id` | the **subject** — an account | `NULL` on every grant written so far |

`account_id IS NULL` is a first-class value, not a missing one: it means the
grant names no account, which is a per-business **capacity** grant. That is what
`provisionInvite` / `provisionBusiness` write, and naming a subject there would
make a business's plan the inviter's personally — invisible to the second member
the day one is added.

`bestActiveGrant` — the check that decides whether a business may be entered —
therefore reads `business_id = ? AND account_id IS NULL`. One column keeps the
two kinds apart in both directions: an account-subject grant cannot make a
business selectable for everyone holding a membership on it, and a capacity
grant can never match an `account_id = ?` lookup. There is no index on the
subject yet, because nothing reads it and an index without a reader is a guess
at a query nobody has written.

**`memberships.account_id` was renamed to `business_id` in the same migration**,
which amends §2's *"only the word changed"* by changing the word. That column
always held a business, and leaving it alone was right while `account_id` meant
*business* everywhere; the moment `entitlements.account_id` began meaning an
actual account, two adjacent tables carrying that name with opposite meanings
became strictly worse than the state being fixed. `tenant_id` is untouched, per
[[REQ-180]] §3, and so are the `acct_…` id VALUES — those are opaque, permanent
and present in R2 keys, so renaming them buys a data migration for nothing.

### To §5 — an expired grant must not deny at login

§5 says the expiry check runs at login and, for the alpha, *"an expired grant
denies with a message; read-only access to one's own site is the better product
answer... so it waits for the payments funnel that gives it a purpose."*

[[DOC-42]] §5 supplies a reason it cannot wait, and it is structural rather than
a product preference. **Membership means the person may log in; entitlement means
their account has been granted access to something.** The Portal — their payments,
their details, their delete button — is a view of the relationship itself, so it
is what membership *is* and needs no grant.

Denying at login therefore removes the remedy along with the access: a lapsed
account cannot see what it was charged, cannot reach the page where it would pay,
and cannot reach its delete button, which [[DOC-37]] makes an obligation rather
than a feature.

So `no_membership` stays a refusal and `no_entitlement` becomes a state inside an
admitted session. [[REQ-178]] and [[REQ-179]] were reopened to take this before
reconciling.

### To §6 — `platform_admin` carries two separable capabilities

§6 defends an ambient flag on bootstrapping grounds: *"it works before any
membership row exists, and it cannot lock its holder out of the system that
grants it."* That argument carries the **`PLATFORM_ADMINS` env var**, which is a
break-glass seed and should stay exactly as described.

It does not carry the **column**, which bundles two different things
([[DOC-42]] §10.3):

- **owner of the 1st Contact business** — not special at all. Every business has
  an owner, and it is `memberships.role = 'owner'`, which exists.
- **may enter a business without holding a membership** — `scope.ts:237`.
  Genuinely special, and special because 1st Contact *hosts* the others rather
  than because of any level.

Keeping them in one flag is §2.1 rule 1's shape: a platform-only marker standing
in for a capability every business owner needs. §6 already names the replacement
for the second half — time-boxed `support` membership rows, *"the auditable
alternative, and the table exists for them from day one"*. What is new is that
the first half should not be a flag at all.

### Also from [[DOC-42]], not an amendment

*Contacts are users* is unchanged and load-bearing. Its consequence is worth
stating because it was not: the CRM and the User tab are **two views of one
population**, differing by whether authentication fields are set, and the invite
is the verb that moves someone across ([[DOC-42]] §9). A User list and a CRM list
that can disagree about a person who is both is the failure the
`(tenant_id, email)` unique index exists to prevent.
