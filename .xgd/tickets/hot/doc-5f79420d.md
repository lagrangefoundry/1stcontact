# Identity, Accounts & Entitlement

## What this covers

Who a person is, which account they operate, and what that account is allowed to
use. It is the model behind logging in to 1st Contact and behind every later
question about billing, trials, comped access and support.

It does **not** cover payments. [[DOC-40]] deliberately stops at "what access
does this account have, and why" — the money that produces some of those
answers is a separate concern, for the reason recorded in §5.

## 1. The gap this closes

Until now the builder served exactly one operator. `apps/control-app` names its
tenant in a deployment var (`TENANT_ID`), Cloudflare Access gates the hostname,
and the Worker re-verifies the Access JWT ([[REQ-147]], `access.ts`). That is a
complete design for **one** user and has no room for a second: two people
logging in would land in the same tenant and edit each other's sites.

Nothing persists about a person either. Access proves an email address on every
request and stores nothing — there is no record of who exists, when they joined,
or what they agreed to.

## 2. Three nouns

**User** — a person, identified by a verified email address, scoped to a tenant.
A builder user lives in the platform's own tenant (`1stcontact`); a site
visitor captured by a customer's contact form lives in *that customer's* tenant.

**Account** — a tenant. The unit of isolation, the thing sites belong to, the
thing that will be billed. Ids are **opaque** (`acct_<random>`), never derived
from a name: a tenant id appears in R2 keys (`t/<tenant>/blob/…`,
`chat/<tenant>/…`, `kb/<tenant>/…`) and is therefore permanent, and a readable id
is one rename request away from being a lie. The human label lives in
`tenants.name`, where it can change.

**Membership** — the join. `(user_id, account_id, role)` says this person may
operate that account. Every user has at least one: `owner` of their own account.
Support access is an extra row with an `expires_at`.

The separation is what makes an agency account possible later without a
migration: several memberships against one account, rather than an account
column on a user.

### Contacts are users

A contact captured by a customer's site is a `users` row in that customer's
tenant with no authentication fields set. Not a second table.

The argument is that the two converge anyway. A contact who is later invited to
a client portal must not become a *different* record with a duplicate email, and
a CRM that cannot say "this contact is also a logged-in user" has to answer that
question with a join across two tables that disagree about identity. The
tenant-scoped unique index on `(tenant_id, email)` is the one place identity is
decided, and it covers both.

## 3. Authentication: rented now, owned later

**Now: Cloudflare Access with the One-Time PIN provider.** Access is already
deployed on `app.1stcontact.io` and the Worker already verifies the JWT and
extracts a verified `email` claim. Its OTP provider is functionally the magic
link we would otherwise build, and it needs no email infrastructure of ours,
no sending domain, no token table and no session store. For the alpha cohort
this is the whole authentication system.

**The policy is set to identity-only** — OTP, any email — so the *authorisation*
decision belongs to this platform rather than to a list in the Cloudflare
dashboard. A verified email that holds no entitlement is refused by the Worker.

Two consequences accepted deliberately:

- The OTP email is Cloudflare-branded. Acceptable for an invited alpha, not for
  a public product.
- The Access edge no longer bounds *who may reach the Worker*, only *who may be
  identified*. Denial is server-side. At alpha volumes the lost cost boundary is
  not material; at public-signup volumes it is, and that is one of the triggers
  for the paragraph below.
- The Zero Trust free tier covers 50 seats.

**Later: our own magic link.** An `auth_tokens` and a `sessions` table, an email
provider, a verified sending domain. `users`, `memberships` and `entitlements`
do not change — which is the property this design is buying. The credential
layer is the only thing that is rented.

## 4. Provisioning: the invite does the work

Because the alpha is invite-only and the operator is creating the account
anyway, **everything is provisioned at invite time** and login is pure lookup:

| Invite (admin) | First login (user) |
| --- | --- |
| `users` row, `invited_at` set, never logged in | verified email → user row |
| `tenants` row — the account, opaque id | membership → the account to serve |
| `memberships` row, `owner` | entitlement → active? |
| `entitlements` row | terms accepted? → else interstitial |
| a starter site in the account | builder, scoped to that account |

There is no just-in-time provisioning. It arrives with self-signup, as a second
entry point writing the same rows.

An invited-but-never-logged-in person is a `users` row with a null
`first_seen_at` — which is to say, a contact. §2 again.

## 5. Entitlement: grants, not flags

An entitlement is **a grant of a plan, for a period, from a source**. Not a
boolean on an account, and not a column that billing overwrites.

```
account_id | plan | source        | status | starts_at | ends_at
acct_x     | pro  | admin_grant   | active | 2026-09-01| 2026-12-01
```

An account accumulates grants over its life — comped, then trial, then
subscription. Effective access is **the best active grant covering now**, not a
single-row lookup. That is the design choice that lets the four product states
land without touching the schema:

| | How it lands |
| --- | --- |
| Anyone can sign up | login's "no entitlement → deny" branch becomes "→ create account + `source='signup'` trial grant" |
| Trial tier | a `plan` value, plus a plan→capability map in code |
| Paid, incl. warning period | a `subscriptions` table (new table, no backfill) whose webhook writes `source='subscription'` rows and moves `status` between `active`/`warning`/`expired` |
| Admin comp, date-bounded | `source='admin_grant'` — **built first** |

`plan` and `status` are unconstrained TEXT. A CHECK constraint here would make
adding `'warning'` a schema migration, which is exactly the cost this model
exists to avoid.

### Access and money are separate

A comped grant is an entitlement with no subscription. A *discounted* one is a
subscription carrying a discount that also produces an entitlement. Putting a
`discount_pct` on the entitlement collapses the two and forces the access check
to understand pricing — the merge that would have to be undone later.

### Expiry must actually expire

A date-bounded grant whose expiry is never evaluated is worse than an open-ended
one, because it was promised as bounded. The check runs at login. For the alpha
an expired grant denies with a message; read-only access to one's own site is
the better product answer and the model supports it unchanged, so it waits for
the payments funnel that gives it a purpose.

## 6. The platform administrator

A `platform_admin` flag on the user row, plus a `PLATFORM_ADMINS` env var for
the console gate. The flag is ambient and that is the point: it works before any
membership row exists, and it cannot lock its holder out of the system that
grants it.

Time-boxed `support` membership rows are the auditable alternative and the
table exists for them from day one. They are not built now because during the
alpha the operator and the administrator are the same person, and an audit trail
of oneself is ceremony. The moment there is a second operator, they are the
answer, and they need no migration.

## 7. The tenant barrier, and what is parked behind it

Cross-tenant reach does not exist in this codebase and that is structural, not
incidental. `SiteStoreRoot` can do exactly one thing — `forTenant`. The ticket
store's scoped handle is *terminal*: `forTenant` on it throws. `BLOBS` is a
separate R2 bucket from `SITES` so that confidential client material sits behind
a binding boundary rather than a path convention.

A **platform operations assistant** — one chat that can correlate across
tenants, "is the bug in tenant X causing the complaint from tenant Y" — is
wanted and is **parked**. Its shape is settled even though it is not built,
because that shape decides what the rest of the system must leave room for.

### It switches tenants; it does not span them

The assistant holds **one ordinary scoped handle at a time** and has a tool that
changes which tenant that is. It does not get a wide handle.

This is strictly better than the platform-scope alternative it replaces, on
three counts:

- **No new query surface.** A wide scope means hand-rolled `WHERE tenant_id`
  predicates in new code, in the one place where a bug is a confidentiality
  breach. Switching calls `forTenant` again — the same terminal, already-tested
  handle every other path uses. `forTenant` is never widened, so no scoped handle
  anywhere becomes one bug away from being global.
- **Existing checks come free**, including `forTenant`'s refusal of an inactive
  tenant, which a second read path would have had to re-implement.
- **Better audit.** A switch is a discrete event with a stated reason. A wide
  scope would need the query layer instrumented to know which tenants a question
  even touched.

It also makes content moderation — the use case that pushes past operational
data into customer content — need no new read surface at all. After a switch the
assistant is an ordinary builder chat in that tenant.

### The barrier moves into the context window

What the design gives up is that the isolation is no longer on the data path.
Correlating X against Y means both in one context, which is the point — but it
means an assistant holding a **write** handle after a switch can write tenant X's
confidential material into tenant Y's site. A cross-tenant leak with zero
cross-tenant reads.

So **read-only is more load-bearing here, not less**. Under a wide read scope the
data could only escape through the transcript; under switching it can escape into
another customer's published site.

Three details that are easy to get wrong and expensive to discover:

1. **The transcript does not follow the switch.** The naive implementation
   archives to `chat/<current tenant>/…` and sprays cross-tenant content into
   customer tenants. The session pins its archive to the platform tenant at open;
   the switch moves the store handle only.
2. **The knowledge index does follow the switch.** `projectKnowledgeFor` derives
   its R2 sources from `indexPrefix(tenantId, …)`. Swapping the store and not the
   vectors scores the right tenant's tickets against the wrong tenant's
   embeddings — silent nonsense, not an error.
3. **Injection can drive navigation.** Customer-supplied material can instruct a
   switch. The defence cannot be *controlling* the switch, because the model will
   be talked into it and the human really is an administrator; the defence is that
   switching is *harmless*, which is to say read-only.

### Breadth needs one small primitive

Pure switching cannot answer "which tenants are seeing this?" without visiting
every tenant in turn — workable at thirty accounts, useless at three thousand.
Nor can it enumerate: neither store root offers `listTenants` today.

So the design pairs a **narrow cross-tenant index query** with the switch tool:
the query reads the ticket store only and returns `(tenant_id, ticket_id,
snippet)` — pointers, not content — and the assistant then switches into whichever
tenant is relevant and reads properly through the ordinary handle. Search wide,
read deep. That confines the only genuinely cross-tenant surface to one auditable
function over operational data.

### It stays a separate assistant

Not a mode on the builder chat. The builder's tool surface never gains a
tenant-switch verb at all, so injection in a client's uploaded PDF cannot reach
one even in principle.

### What this costs the rest of the system today

Nothing. Because the scope is always a single tenant, [[REQ-168]] declares no
platform variant — it only needs resolution to accept an explicit target and
authorise it, which an administrator operating a customer's builder ([[REQ-170]])
requires anyway. The switch tool, if built, calls the same function.

## 8. Erasure and retention

[[DOC-37]] treats erasure as identity severance. Two consequences land here:

1. A `users` row is the severance target. Deleting it must reach the memberships
   and entitlements that reference it, and the account itself if no other
   membership survives.
2. Once the parked operations assistant exists, its transcripts hold
   cross-tenant data in the platform tenant — exactly the kind of place data
   hides from a deletion sweep. Retention-bounding those transcripts is the
   cheapest mitigation and should be decided before it is built, not after.
