# Data Deletion & Retention — erasure as identity severance

## 1. Purpose & scope

What 1st Contact must be able to destroy, at two levels, and the architecture that makes destruction
true rather than merely claimed.

**Status:** design capture from [[CHAT-21]]. The end-customer feature is intended **from day one** of
the user portal (§8 argues why that is cheaper than it sounds). The architectural decisions in §6 are
the ones that are nearly free now and a migration later.

**Amended 2026-11-03** ([[EPIC-9]], operator decisions): records of money moving are kept for every
party (§6.1), the retained minimum is name and billing address (§6.3), closing a business keeps the
business record and deletes its content (§4.2), and the whole thing runs as one automated lifecycle
(§7.1). Where this document and an epic disagree, this document is the reference.

**Scope:** the technical design — what is deleted, what is retained, who acts, and how the system
stays correct as limbs are added. The companion [[DOC-36]] covers what we collect; the two are one
design.

**Out of scope:** the legal instrument. This document uses controller/processor language because it
determines *where the button lives and whom it must satisfy*, not to substitute for advice.

---

## 2. The principle

Erasure is not *"remove the rows."* It is:

> **Sever the link between the rows and the human.**

The purpose of an erasure right is that a person becomes unidentifiable and unreachable — not that
history is rewritten. Once this framing is adopted, the retention exceptions in §5 stop looking like
loopholes and become coherent: **we delete the person and keep the accounting.**

Everything else in this document is a consequence of that sentence.

---

## 3. Two levels

| Level | Whose data | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| **Tenant** | A 1st Contact customer — Sarah, a shaman, a founder | Tenant deletion (§4) |
| **End customer** | *Their* customers — people who filled in Sarah's form, joined her list, paid her | Per-person erasure (§5) |

These are genuinely different problems. Level 1 is coarse and mostly solved by scoping discipline.
Level 2 is fine-grained, ongoing, and is a product feature.

---

## 4. Level 1 — tenant deletion

If everything is tenant-scoped, deleting a tenant is the whole story. So the only real question is:
**what have we deliberately let escape the tenant boundary?** — and per [[DOC-36]], the answer is
"quite a lot, on purpose": cross-customer benchmarks, refusal aggregates, spread statistics, any
transcript promoted to a few-shot exemplar.

The governing rule, shared with [[DOC-36]] §8:

> **Anything crossing the tenant boundary is either irreversibly aggregated at the moment it crosses,
> or it carries the tenant key so that it dies with the tenant. There is never a third category.**

### 4.1 Where tenant-nuke is not enough on its own
Two categories of storage cannot be rewritten:

- **Backups.** You cannot retroactively excise a row from last month's snapshot.
- **Append-only history.** If the consultation ledger and transcript live as a `chat` ticket body and
  `chat_transcript` comment in the git-backed ticketing store ([[DOC-33]] §3), then deletion means
  excising from git history — and append-only is precisely the property that store was chosen for.

For both, the practical answer is **crypto-shredding**: encrypt per tenant, and destroy the key. The
data becomes unreadable without rewriting history or restoring and rewriting backups.

Note this is **not an alternative** to tenant deletion. It is what makes tenant deletion *true* in
the presence of immutable storage, and it should be applied narrowly — to the stores whose contents
cannot be structurally guaranteed (§6.3) — rather than as a general mechanism.

### 4.2 Closing a business: the record stays, the content goes
*Operator, 2026-11-03.* "Nuke the tenant" is right for content and wrong for money, so a closing
business is not deleted outright:

- **The business record stays**, marked closed. It is the anchor for what must survive.
- **Its content is deleted:** sites and published builds, the library and its files, inventory,
  conversations, and its contacts with everything filed under them.
- **Its money records are kept** under §6.1: our invoices to it, and its own sales to its
  customers. They are offered to the business in an export before closure, and are not shown in any
  product view afterwards. Only the platform operator can read them, and only for a dispute.
- **Its plan with us stops renewing** and runs to the end of the term (§7.1 step 1). The deletion
  clock starts then.
- **Its domains are kept** until they are transferred out or lapse, because deleting a domain takes
  a website and its email off the internet ([[EPIC-6]]).

## 5. Level 2 — end-customer erasure

### 5.1 Who acts
Structurally, for end-customer data the tenant is the **controller** and 1st Contact is the
**processor**. But that allocates *responsibility*, not *labour* — and the customer should never have
to do the work.

Two mechanisms make that so:

**A standing instruction.** A processor acts on the controller's documented instructions; those
instructions may be configured once and executed automatically thereafter. The tenant sets the policy
at setup and never touches it again.

**The request arrives at us anyway.** If the tenant's site carries a *"delete my data"* link, that
link necessarily points at our infrastructure — we rendered the page and we hold the data. So the end
customer **self-serves**, our systems execute, and the tenant receives a notification and an audit
record.

Tenant involvement is therefore *being informed*. This is also the safer design: a tenant forgetting
to action a request is a liability we eliminate by never routing it through a human.

### 5.2 Compliance as a property of the substrate
Worth stating as a product position, not just an implementation note.

A sole proprietor running a site plus a mailing tool plus a payment processor has **three separate
erasure obligations and no coherent way to satisfy any of them.** In practice they satisfy none, and
mostly avoid thinking about it.

Because we own the whole chain, all three are satisfied by one action. That is the same structural
advantage that makes attribution a foreign key rather than a CDP problem ([[DOC-36]] §2), pointed at
*obligation* instead of *insight* — and it is a genuine selling point to anyone who has had the
thought and then decided not to have it again.

It also belongs to the frame in [[DOC-35]]: **the system holds the obligation the owner has no time
for.**

## 6. What is deleted, what survives, and why

### 6.1 The inventory
| Data | Outcome |
|---|---|
| **Email address, name, phone** | Genuinely destroyed. A **one-way hash** survives on the suppression list — an unusable fingerprint whose only job is to keep *not* mailing them |
| **Consent record** | The content goes; the **event** survives — pseudonymous key and timestamps — because it is the proof that we were permitted, and that we complied when asked |
| **Billing & payment history** | The **transaction** survives for 7 years from when the money moved (§7.1). The **person** is reduced to **name and billing address** (§6.3). This covers every record of money changing hands, whether the money went to us or to a business on 1st Contact: orders with a paid invoice, invoices, lines, credit notes, and our cache of Stripe payments, refunds and subscription invoices |
| **Enquiry content, list membership, attribution, session data, transcripts** | Actually deleted |

Three of these are retained *deliberately*, and in each case the retention exists to serve the person
rather than us:

- **Suppression hashes** prevent the exact harm they asked us to prevent. Delete the hash and the
  next list import re-adds them and mails them again — the single most common naive-implementation
  bug in this area.
- **Consent events** are the evidence trail. Deleting them destroys our ability to demonstrate that
  we were allowed to hold the data and that we honoured the request.
- **Transaction records** are a legal obligation that overrides erasure, and this is explicit in the
  relevant regimes rather than a grey area.

**Why we keep a business's own sales records too** (operator, 2026-11-03): we can be drawn into a
dispute between a business and its buyer. For those records we are the business's processor, so by
default we would delete or return them when the service ends. **Keeping a copy to defend a dispute
makes us the controller of that copy**, on our own legitimate interest in legal claims. That is
lawful only if the copy is minimised (§6.3), kept for a fixed period (§7.1), restricted to dispute
use, and **disclosed in our terms and privacy notice** ([[DOC-5]] policy 26). This is a design
position, not legal advice; the terms need a lawyer's review.

**Subscriptions on deletion:** every subscription the person holds is cancelled, and **cancellation
never ends service early**. It turns off renewal, the person keeps what they paid for until the end
of the term, and no refund is due. Once the last subscription has ended, their Stripe customer on
the business's connected account is deleted, so their email leaves Stripe too. That is §5.2's one
action covering the payment processor.

### 6.2 The promise must be accurate
It follows that **"delete all my data" is not a promise we can keep**, and offering it is worse than
offering the accurate thing. The feature is *"delete everything we are allowed to,"* with a plain
explanation of the three exceptions above. For most people that explanation increases trust rather
than reducing it, because it is obviously honest.

### 6.3 Architecture — separate identity from record
The design consequence of §2, and the decision that is cheap now and expensive later:

> **One identity store holds the person — email, name, phone. Every other store references an opaque
> `person_id` and holds no identity of its own.**

**At deletion the identity record is reduced, not replaced** (operator, 2026-11-03). If the person
has any record of money moving, it keeps only **name and billing address**: the minimum that ties a
money record to the real world. A record nobody can tie to a real person is worth nothing in a
dispute. Everything else is stripped, including email, phone and delivery addresses. With no money
records, the identity record is deleted whole. Every other store keeps its `person_id` and needs no
rewrite.

**Stripping must be trivial.** The identity store marks which fields are the kept minimum, so
reducing a record is one generic operation, not a per-field list someone maintains. **A new identity
field is stripped by default** unless it is explicitly marked as part of the minimum, so a field
added later cannot quietly survive erasure.

**"Store PII once" is a schema rule, not a habit.** An order's delivery address and a buyer's note
are identity: they live in the identity store, or are deleted, and never sit on the order. Free-text
fields on money records hold no identity.

The payoff is large: **most stores then need no deletion logic at all**, because they were never
identifying. The registry (§7) shrinks to the handful of stores that do real work; everything else
registers as *"references person_id, nothing to do"* and is anonymous by construction.

Two residuals, and they are the genuinely hard ones:

**Free text.** Transcripts, notes and enquiry bodies contain names and addresses inline no matter how
clean the schema is, and prose cannot be reliably scrubbed. **This is where crypto-shredding belongs**
— encrypt per person or per tenant, destroy the key. Narrowly, for the stores whose contents cannot
be structurally guaranteed.

**Accidental identity in structured fields** — a business name that is also a person's name, a slug
derived from an email address. Worth a periodic audit rather than a design.

## 7. The registry — making it survive new limbs

*"We will maintain deletion as we add new data"* is a promise that decays. Every new limb adds a
store, and eventually someone forgets.

The mechanism that does not decay: **erasure derives from a registry.** Every store holding
person-scoped data registers itself and declares its behaviour:

| Declaration | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `delete` | Rows are removed |
| `redact` | Rows survive, identity reduced to a declared minimum |
| `retain-with-reason` | Rows survive intact; the reason is recorded (§6.1) |
| `no-identity` | References `person_id` only; nothing to do |

Erasure iterates the registry. **Adding an unregistered store becomes a test failure rather than a
memory failure** — which is the same principle the project applies elsewhere: convert discipline into
mechanism.

Each declaration also carries its retention: `retain-with-reason` names the date its clock runs
from (for a money record, when the money moved). That way the lifecycle job (§7.1) needs nothing but
the registry. Two things fall out for free:

- The registry **is** an always-accurate data inventory, which is the tedious part of writing a
  privacy policy.
- The retention exceptions must be **declared explicitly** rather than discovered during an incident.

### 7.1 The lifecycle: one automated job
*Operator, 2026-11-03:* nobody deletes records by hand or tracks dates, so every rule here must be
something a scheduled job can apply. **One job runs daily**, reads the registry, and applies these
five rules:

1. **Deletion is requested** by a person erasing themselves, a business deleting a contact
   ([[DOC-54]] R5), or a business closing (§4.2). **The record is hidden immediately**: it leaves
   lists, is never mailed again, and its sessions end. Nothing is destroyed yet. If service is still
   running (a subscription term, a business's plan), renewal is turned off and the clock starts when
   the service ends.
2. **Everything is kept for 30 days**, then the job deletes it, reducing the identity record per
   §6.3. The 30 days is the undo window, and stays inside the law's roughly one-month expectation
   for erasure.
3. **Deletion can be deferred by hand.** Each deferral records a reason and lasts 30 to 180 days.
   Deferrals can be renewed indefinitely, each with its own reason, and the job deletes on its next
   run once one lapses. This is how a live dispute, chargeback or unpaid balance keeps a person's
   email, so email needs no special place in the minimum. The business can defer for its own
   contacts, and so can the platform operator. The same mechanism pauses rule 5.
4. **Records of money moving are kept**, joined to the reduced identity record (§6.1, §6.3).
5. **After 7 years everything is deleted**, including the reduced identity record. The 7 years runs
   from when the money moved, the record's own date, never from the deletion request. That way
   each record needs one date and no case tracking, and an active customer's oldest records age out
   too.

**Two exceptions:**
- **The suppression fingerprint is never deleted** (§6.1). If it aged out at 7 years, the next
  import would mail the person again.
- **Synthetic records** follow the test-data sweep's own schedule ([[DOC-54]] R4), not this one.

## 8. Why day one is cheaper than it looks

Because of the closed loop [[DOC-36]] §2 depends on. A single person record ends up fragmented across
**capture, list, billing, analytics, and post-attribution in the marketing calendar** — five limbs
holding pieces of one human.

Sweeping all of them is trivial when there is one limb and a project when there are five. Registry at
limb one means every subsequent limb pays a two-line tax instead of a retrofit — and the identity/
record separation in §6.3 costs nothing at all if it is the shape the first schema takes.

## 9. Open questions

- **Deletion latency and backups.** *Partly answered 2026-11-03:* live data goes 30 days after the
  request, or once any running service ends (§7.1). Still open: how long "deleted" may mean
  "unreadable in backups but present until rotation". Crypto-shredding makes this defensible; the
  backup window still needs choosing.
- **Notification design.** What the tenant is told when an end customer erases — informative without
  becoming a re-identification channel.
- ~~**Statutory minimum for billing redaction**~~ *Answered 2026-11-03:* one conservative answer
  everywhere, name and billing address (§6.3).
- **Whether transcripts are retained at all** once their signals are extracted ([[DOC-36]] §10). If
  emit-don't-mine works, short retention is both cheaper and the best privacy posture available.
- **Export before delete.** Erasure and portability usually arrive together; worth deciding whether
  the portal offers both from day one. *Partly answered 2026-11-03:* a closing business is offered an
  export of its money records before closure (§4.2).
