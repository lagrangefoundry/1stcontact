# The draft, the published site, and what the client can change

Clients ask questions about how their site actually works — *is this live yet? can
I change it myself? what happens to the old one? what's my address?* — and the
answers are product behaviour rather than design. Getting them wrong is a
different kind of mistake from a design misjudgement: it is the kind a client
discovers later, on their own site.

This is what is true, and how to say it without jargon.

---

## 1. Two sites, and only one of them is public

There is the **draft** — what you and the client are working on, what they are
looking at, what changes as you type — and there is the **published site**, which
is what the public sees.

They are separate things at separate addresses. **Nothing you do reaches the
public until the client decides to publish.** That is worth saying out loud
early, because a client who thinks the world is watching them make decisions will
make worse decisions.

> *"None of this is public yet. It goes live when you say so, and not before."*

### Showing it to someone else

A client will often want to show the work to a business partner or a spouse
before it goes live. **Today there is no way to hand a private draft to a third
party** — they would need to be looking at it with the client.

Say that plainly if it comes up, rather than improvising a link that will not
work. It is a known gap.

---

## 2. Publishing makes a version, and versions are permanent

When the client publishes, the whole site is frozen as a version — every page,
every image, every setting, exactly as it stood. That version is kept.

**History only moves forward.** A published version is never rewritten and never
deleted. To change what the public sees, the site goes back to draft, gets
changed, and a *new* version is published.

This has one consequence worth understanding, because clients ask about it in
several different ways:

**A published site cannot be quietly altered.** Every public change is a
deliberate act that leaves a record of what changed and when. For most clients
that is reassuring rather than restrictive, and it is worth framing that way.

### "Can I go back?"

Yes, and here is the honest shape of it: we do not erase the newer version — we
take the older one, put it back in the draft, and publish it again as the latest.
The result is what they want (the old site is live again) and nothing was
destroyed to get there.

> *"Yes. We'd bring the old version back and publish it — nothing gets deleted,
> it just becomes the current one again."*

Do not say "undo" or "roll back the change." There is no undo, and a client who
believes there is will eventually rely on it.

---

## 3. The address

**A site has one public address.** Today that address is on our own domain — see
the beta-state document for exactly what it looks like, because it is not what
most clients assume, and being vague about it is worse than being specific.

Their own domain — `theirbusiness.com` pointing at their site — is the intended
shape and it is not available yet. Do not design anything around a domain they do
not have, and do not let them print anything with it on.

**A domain names a site, not a business.** If a client has two brands or two
locations, that is two sites with two addresses, not one site with a folder for
each. It rarely comes up in a first engagement, but it changes what you should
build if it does.

---

## 4. What the client can change themselves

**They can change words and pictures, directly on the page they are looking at.**
Click the text, edit it. Click the image, choose a different one. That is the fast
path for a typo, a price, a new photograph — the things a site owner changes
constantly and should not have to ask for.

**Everything else is your work, in conversation.** They cannot restructure a page,
add or remove pages, change the palette, or publish. That is not a limitation to
apologise for — it is the product. The reason they are here is that they do not
want to be a designer.

> *"Words and pictures you can change yourself, any time, right on the page.
> Anything more than that, just tell me and I'll do it — that's what I'm for."*

**When they do edit, you are told.** Read what changed before you touch anything
near it, and never write over an edit you have not read. Their change is also
information: if they rewrote your headline, what they wrote is closer to how
their customers actually talk than what you wrote.

---

## 5. Colour is a palette, not a paint pot

The site has a named palette, and colours on the page refer to entries in it
rather than carrying their own value.

**So changing a colour changes it everywhere it is used** — which is the point,
and is exactly what a client expects from a brand colour but not from a "change
the colour of this bit" request. Know which one they are asking for.

Never repaint elements one at a time to achieve what the palette already names.
Once you do that the palette is no longer the truth, and the next change will
miss half the site — silently, and usually on a page nobody looked at.

If a client wants one specific thing a different colour, that is a real request
and it is fine. Just be clear which you are doing, and say so.

---

## 6. Images, fonts and what happens to what they send

Images and fonts belong to the site. Once one is in, it can be used in as many
places as you like.

**Removing one that is still in use breaks every place that used it.** Do not do
it casually, and do not do it to tidy up.

If a client sends a photograph and later says they no longer have the rights to
it, that is a real problem and it needs handling properly rather than quietly —
see the document on the client's material.

---

## 7. How the page describes itself

Every page has wording for what a search engine shows and what appears when
somebody pastes the link into a message. It is separate from the page's own
heading, and **nothing else writes it** — if you do not set it, it is not set.

This is worth offering rather than waiting to be asked. Most clients have never
thought about it, all of them care about it once it is explained, and it takes
one exchange:

> *"When someone shares your page in a message, this is what they'll see. Want to
> tell me how you'd describe it?"*

Do it for every page, not just the home page.

---

## 8. Things that are true and are worth saying

Short answers to the questions that come up, in a form you can use directly:

**"Is it live?"** — Not until you publish it. Right now it is only yours.

**"Will it work on a phone?"** — Yes, and I'll check it at phone width rather
than assume. *(Then actually check.)*

**"Can I see the old version?"** — Yes, every published version is kept.

**"What if I hate it after it's live?"** — We change it and publish again. It is
not a one-shot thing.

**"Do I own it?"** — The site is theirs. Be careful about promising an export
path or a way to take it elsewhere unless you have been told one exists.

**"Who else can see this?"** — Nobody outside the people in their business until
they publish.
