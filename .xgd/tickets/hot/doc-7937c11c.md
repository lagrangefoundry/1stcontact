# 1st Contact — Product Definition

## Vision

Build an AI that designs a small business's public face, and then runs it.

The goal is not to compete with enterprise marketing agencies or full-featured CRM suites. It is to
give solo founders and small service businesses something they currently cannot buy at any price
they can afford: a site that is genuinely *theirs* — designed in consultation, not selected from a
template — and the working machinery behind it that turns visitors into customers.

The platform also serves as a public demonstration of XGD architecture and autonomous software
stewardship principles.

---

## The Front Office

**We are the front office. We stop where the back office starts.**

The boundary is the whole product definition, and every scope decision falls out of it:

```text
   stranger  ──────────────────────────────────►  money changes hands
   │                                              │
   └────────────── 1st Contact ───────────────────┘

                                                  └──── not us ────►
                                                  accounting, payroll,
                                                  inventory, fulfilment,
                                                  ERP, tax
```

Everything between "a stranger might become a customer" and "money changes hands" is ours.
Everything after it belongs to someone else. This is the same line the product has always drawn
— *"stop at the point where money is collected"* — now named once instead of re-derived per feature.

Each capability is a customer-facing touchpoint:

| Capability | Its place in the front office |
|---|---|
| Website | Where they find you |
| Marketing | How they come to find you |
| Email lists | How you stay in front of them |
| Contact management | Who has reached you, and what happened next |
| Scheduling | How they book your time |
| Invoicing & payments | How they pay you |
| Monitoring | Making sure the front door is open |

The product name is the thesis: **1st Contact** is the moment a business meets a customer, and
everything that follows until they *are* one.

---

## What We Sell: Two Moments, One Relationship

**The engagement.** A real consultative design session — hours, not minutes — that ends with a site
the client could not otherwise have afforded. The AI asks the questions a good designer asks,
pushes back where a good designer pushes back, and builds while the client watches. The full
process is specified in [[DOC-33]].

**The operation.** The ongoing relationship: the front office runs. Enquiries land somewhere and are
chased. The list goes out. The booking is taken. The invoice is paid. The site stays up. The
marketing gets reviewed against what actually happened.

These are not two products. The engagement produces the **ledger** — the record of who the client
is, who they serve, what they offer, and what was decided and why — and the operation runs *from*
it. Stage 10 of the consultation is not a handover; it is the start of the ongoing tier
([[DOC-33]] §11).

## Why We Win — Three Theses

### 1. No expressive ceiling

Other website builders (Wix, Squarespace, Framer) have a **hard expressive ceiling**: you get their
widgets and a constrained editor, and you can never drop to arbitrary code without leaving the
product. Their ceiling is real and low.

Our architecture has no such ceiling, because:

- **Modules are arbitrary code.** The rendered output space is the entire space of what a browser can do — any layout, animation, canvas/WebGL, or interaction a hand-coded site can produce, a module can too.
- **Composition spans config → bespoke.** A site is structured data over modules; where the library covers a design it is pure config, and where it does not, a **bespoke module** is authored — and composition stays structured on top of it. The module is a *structured escape valve to arbitrary code that never leaves the model*.
- **AI + eyes reaches the top end.** With constraints removed, quality comes from the AI rendering its own output, seeing it, and iterating ([[DOC-13]]) — with human review as backstop — not from a narrowed editor.
- **The system grows its own language.** Capability gaps discovered while building sites are hardened into library primitives by the same dev system that builds the platform (XGD) — see [[DOC-14]]. The expressive ceiling rises without limit.

The consequence: we are a **strict superset of every template builder** — everything they can do,
plus everything they cannot — driven by an AI with eyes. The only real walls are **security** and
**reliability** ([[DOC-7]] §6), never taste.

### 2. Not a bundle — one thing, designed as one thing

The competitor is not Wix. It is the seven-tool stack the client is currently duct-taping together:
a site builder, a mail tool, a booking link, a payment processor, a contact spreadsheet, an uptime
checker — each with its own login, its own monthly fee, and its own visual identity crashing into
the page.

The difference is structural, not commercial: **our capabilities are designed into the page rather
than embedded onto it.** Payments and email capture are *in* the page — surfaces, states, flows,
legal copy — and are therefore stage-1 brief decisions, constraining architecture, copy and layout
like anything else ([[DOC-33]] §11). A booking surface is composed *with* the design. A third-party
embed is bolted through it.

A bundle is beaten by a better bundle. A coherent whole is not.

### 3. The machinery remembers the consultation

Mailchimp does not know why you chose your positioning. Calendly does not know who your audience is.
Every tool in the stack starts cold, every time.

The ledger is a **business record, not a site record** ([[DOC-33]] §11). Act I — business, audience,
offer, objectives, capabilities, brand — runs once per client, ever. Every later limb starts from a
document that already holds it. This is what makes the marketing advice specific, the follow-up
sequences informed, and a new capability a *follow-through* rather than an upsell:

> *"You told me in July you were losing enquiries in your inbox."*

This is the moat. It is also the thing a list of features hides completely, so it must be said
explicitly wherever we describe the product.

---

## Consults and Executes

The capability list mixes two kinds of thing, deliberately:

- **Consults** — the design session, marketing advice, what's working and what isn't, strategic pushback
- **Executes** — the site, contacts, lists, invoicing, scheduling, monitoring

Read as software, "marketing consultancy" is the odd one out. Read correctly — *an AI that consults,
plus machinery that executes what was decided* — it is the second instance of the shape [[DOC-33]]
already specifies for design: same acts, same ledger, different subject.

# Messaging

## Not

* Website hosting
* Website builder
* Website caretaker
* Marketing agency
* A bundle of small-business SaaS
* ERP or accounting
* **A template gallery, or an AI that picks from one**

## Is

**The front office for a small business** — designed, then run.

## Value proposition

> Your website, designed properly — and everything behind it that turns a visitor into a customer.

Secondary, for the ongoing tier:

> The front of your business runs itself.

## No Templates — the website-scoped position

We must not be reduced to a website provider; the product is the front office. But **when the
conversation is about the website, the position is: No Templates.**

Every competitor starts from a catalogue. Wix, Squarespace and Framer sell template galleries as a
primary marketing asset. The AI builders (Durable, Hostinger, Jimdo Dolphin, Wix ADI) market
themselves as AI-first, but the AI's job is *selection from a catalogue* — a short intake, then a
template chosen and populated. Their AI picks. Ours designs.

> They have templates. We don't have any to pick from.

Why this claim is strong:

* **It is falsifiable and checkable.** "Show me your templates" — "we don't have any" is a short, memorable exchange no competitor can repeat.
* **They structurally cannot follow.** The template catalogue is their product architecture, not a marketing choice. Removing it removes them.
* **It is our architecture stated in one phrase.** No fixed component catalogue, modules are arbitrary code, composition spans config → bespoke.
* **"Asks you questions" is already table stakes.** Every AI builder claims it. A two-to-five question intake is not a consultation, and *No Templates* is the difference the prospect can actually verify — [[DOC-33]] is why the difference is real.

Two obligations it creates, and neither is optional:

1. **The claim is about process, and prospects only see output.** [[DOC-31]] rec. 4 warns that a system with no fixed catalogue is *more* exposed to template-DNA failure than a hand-built site, if the generation model settles into a de facto fixed section order. If two of our sites share a fingerprint, the claim collapses in public. The Checklist B diagnostic — *strip the copy, swap the photography; is the architecture still generic?* — must run against our own output distribution before we say this loudly.
2. **"No templates" can read as "more work for me."** For a client who wants a site by Friday it can sound slower and harder. It must always be paired: *no templates, and nothing for you to design either.* The consultation is our labour, not theirs.

**Lead with:** custom, designed for you, no templates, nothing off the shelf, built from the conversation.

## Demonstration Over Claim

The strongest form of *No Templates* is not a sentence, it is watching a site come into being from a
conversation with no template moment in it.

**Hero concept: the conversation and the site, side by side, driven by scroll.** Chat on one side,
the site assembling on the other — the product's actual interface ([[DOC-4]] §2, Chat Window + Live
Preview), not a metaphor for it. Scroll-linked so the visitor controls the pace and can scrub back.
Reference: Lovable, the one comp whose own site is worth studying for craft.

This is doubly right, because [[DOC-31]] classifies continuous scroll-linked motion as a genuine
*technical ceiling* — something Wix, Squarespace and Framer structurally cannot produce. The hero is
therefore itself evidence for the expressive-ceiling thesis. The medium is the argument.

**The conversation we show is the whole decision.** If the chat reads *"make me a catering
website"* → beautiful site, we have demonstrated a prompt-to-site tool, which is Lovable and Durable,
and we have actively undermined the consultation thesis. The excerpt must contain a moment of
**pushback or a real question whose answer changes the site** — the thing [[DOC-32]] says people are
actually paying for. One beat of *"who's booking you — couples, or venues?"* separates us from every
prompt-to-site demo on the market.

Constraints:

* **Scroll-linked, never scroll-jacked.** Position maps to progress; the visitor can always leave.
* **Degrades to a static sequence.** No JS, no motion preference — still legible.
* **Cheap.** This is our own shop window; a heavy hero contradicts the JAMStack thesis.
* **Real, not fabricated.** Build it from an actual [[DOC-33]] session transcript. It is more credible, and it is the dogfooding the platform claims.

## Register

[[DOC-32]] finds that what people pay a designer for is direct access to whoever does the work, an
outcome rather than an artifact, and someone who will push back. The register we choose decides who
we are compared against, so:

**Lead with:** designed, in consultation, yours, custom, considered, we asked, we advised against, runs.
**Avoid:** maintain, caretake, look after, keep running, monitor, worry-free, hassle-free, hosting,
platform, dashboard, all-in-one, suite, template, theme, gallery, get started in minutes.

The word *caretaker* — the platform's original framing — is retired. It named one capability out of
eight (monitoring), described the making of the site as custodial when it is the moment the client
is actually paying attention, and anchored us against maintenance plans rather than designers. The
same applies to *"stop worrying about your website"*: anxiety relief is a weak, defensive promise
for work whose value is that it is good.

# Architectural Principles

## JAMStack Native

Customer websites are JAMStack sites.

Benefits:

* Low hosting cost
* High performance
* High reliability
* Strong security
* Cloudflare-friendly deployment

The management platform itself is also a JAMStack application backed by Cloudflare services.

## Configuration and Content Driven

Customer sites should not contain significant custom code.

A site should primarily consist of:

* Configuration
* Content
* Assets
* Theme settings

A shared rendering engine generates the final site.

### Desired Structure

```text
Site Definition
├── Configuration
├── Content
├── Assets
└── Theme

Shared Platform
├── Layouts
├── Components
├── Sections
├── Forms
├── SEO
└── Rendering Engine
```

## Structured Changes

AI should modify structured site definitions rather than arbitrary HTML/CSS.

Examples:

* Change color palette
* Add page
* Reorder sections
* Add contact form
* Add gallery

This reduces risk and improves reliability.

## Capabilities Land In The Page

In-page capabilities (payments, email capture, scheduling) are **stage-1 brief decisions, not
separate engagements** ([[DOC-33]] §11). A site designed without knowing a capability exists is a
site that capability cannot land in.

The **capability catalogue** is the interface between the capability set and the consultation: for
each capability, what it does, what it requires of the page, what it obliges, and what it cannot do.
It is projected from module declarations, never hand-written.

# Product Scope

## 1. Modular JAMStack Website Framework

Component-based architecture, reusable sections, theme system, responsive layouts, static
generation, Cloudflare deployment.

Target industries: caterers, consultants, coaches, tradespeople, photographers, other solo
businesses. Industry templates exist at the **business workflow** level rather than the visual
level — we do not ship visual templates.

## 2. Consultative Website Creation

The primary interface, and the primary act. Specified in full in [[DOC-33]].

Users interact via conversation against a live preview:

```text
Chat Window  +  Live Preview
```

Examples:

* "Create a catering website."
* "Move testimonials higher."
* "Add a wedding package."
* "Make the site feel more premium."

Onboarding is part website creation and part business consultation. Act I output is client
property, not site property, and persists into every later limb.

## 3. Contact Management (CRM Lite)

Track leads and customer interactions. Internally implemented using the XGD ticket model; the user
never sees tickets.

Lead states: New Inquiry, Contacted, Quote Needed, Quote Sent, Follow Up, Booked, Lost.

The user sees: Leads, Customers, Follow Ups, Conversations.

Relationship to the site: **behind the page** — a destination for what the page collects.

## 4. Email Lists

Capture, list management, and sending. Relationship to the site: **in the page** for capture (a form
and its destination), **off the site** for sending.

Scope: list building, segments a solo business can actually maintain, campaign sends, and the
calendar/reminder cadence that keeps them going out. Not marketing automation.

## 5. Scheduling

Calendly-light. Availability, booking, confirmation, reminders, cancellation.

Relationship to the site: **in the page** — a booking surface with states and a destination, the
same class of stage-1 decision as payments. *(Note: this limb postdates [[DOC-33]] §11 and should be
added to that table.)*

## 6. Invoicing, Billing and Payments

Quotes, invoices, Stripe payment links, payment tracking, and subscriptions where the client's
business is subscription-shaped.

Not accounting, payroll, inventory or ERP. We stop at the point where money is collected.

Relationship to the site: **in the page** — surface, states, flow, legal copy.

## 7. Marketing Consultancy

Ongoing advice rather than machinery: what to post and where, the email calendar, reminders,
content creation support, and — the part that makes it consultancy rather than a content generator
— **what is working and what is not**, read against the client's own objectives as recorded in the
ledger.

Relationship to the site: **off the site** — consumes the positioning from Act I.

## 8. Monitoring

Relationship to the site: **around it** — invisible to the design. Detail below.

## 9. User Portal and Subscriptions

Customer login, subscription management, invoice history, payment history, account management.
Expected to be shared with the broader XGD ecosystem.

# Monitoring MVP

Monitoring focuses on business outcomes rather than technical metrics.

Initial:

* Website availability
* SSL validity
* Domain health
* Contact form testing
* Lead capture verification
* Broken links
* Missing assets
* Basic SEO checks

Future:

* AI visibility monitoring
* Newsletter effectiveness
* Lead funnel monitoring
* SEM support

Monitoring is a trust and retention capability, not a headline. It is most valuable when it is
invisible, which is precisely why it makes a poor product framing.

---

# Deliberate Exclusions

These are back office, and therefore out of scope by definition rather than by prioritisation:

* ERP
* Inventory management
* Shipping and fulfilment
* Advanced accounting
* Payroll
* Enterprise CRM functionality
* Complex marketing automation

These may later be supported through integrations — the front office handing off to the back
office, which is the correct shape.

---

# Business Strategy

Primary customer:

* Solo founders
* Small service businesses
* Businesses that need a steady lead flow rather than sophisticated marketing operations

The comparison we want is a **good independent designer** who also happens to run your front office
— not a cheap agency and not a website builder. [[DOC-32]] sets out why: direct access, outcome over
artifact, and willingness to push back are what people are actually buying.

**Pricing is deliberately not settled here.** The commercial structure — what the engagement costs,
what the ongoing tier costs, how they relate — is a live question and is deferred. Earlier versions
of this document positioned the product on a price point (~$50/month, between DIY builder and
agency); that framing is withdrawn, because it selected the wrong comparison set and let pricing
drive positioning rather than the reverse. See [[DOC-32]] for benchmark data when the question
becomes live.
