# Consultant model: a great site as the goal, the process as a toolkit

**Status:** draft for operator review (2026-11-02). The inventory's calls were settled with the operator the same day (A.14). Nothing is built from this until it's agreed. Owner: EPIC-19.

## 1. Why this exists

The tools are good; the problem is that **the tools are running the show**. The consultant executes behaviours (stages, guides, holds, gates and refusals) instead of creating a solution with the client. Seen in a full test build (2026-11-02): it asked the client to operate the machinery ("I can only build the card from notes you've hearted"), rushed to a headline because a guide said one must be agreed before the first draft, pinned base facts because a guide said to pin as they talk, and missed the material that mattered (prices, classes, testimonials) until the client pointed at it. Patching bug by bug adds more rules and doesn't converge.

**And we must remember why the process exists.** Before it, the consultant was too passive: it could do great work but waited to be told, and client and consultant ended up waiting on each other. Backing out the declarative process must not bring that back. The answer is to replace the process with a goal, a plan the consultant owns, and one rule about initiative (§6), not to remove structure and hope.

## 2. What a great site is (our starting position, aligned with the client)

**This is our view going in, not a contract.** The consultant brings it to the client, and the client says whether that's what they want and what their priorities are: what matters most, what matters less, what they'd add. What comes out of that conversation is the agreed definition of done for this site, recorded as theirs, and it can change later.

Our starting view: a great small-business site passes these tests:

1. **Clear in five seconds.** A stranger on a phone knows what this is, who it's for, where, and what to do next, from the first screen.
2. **A reason to pick them.** One clear reason the right customer chooses this business, told specifically and in their voice: the owner's story, or what's distinctive about the product or service.
3. **Proof.** Real photos of real work, testimonials, credentials; nothing generic standing in for them.
4. **It answers what people ask before they get in touch:** what you do, prices or how pricing works, where, when, how to reach you; the next step is easy and works.
5. **It looks like them.** A coherent look from their world and their photos, at most one signature flourish; not a template.
6. **The basics are solid.** Fast, readable and accessible on a phone; no broken links; forms that deliver.
7. **It's true and it's theirs.** Every claim real and approved by them.

**The floor: 1, 4, 6, 7.** These are about the site working and being true, so we deliver them on every site; the client's priorities still set the order and the emphasis. **Shaped most by the client's view: 2, 3, 5** (§4).

## 3. The nine pieces, and when each is done

Each piece is **done when the client agrees it, or deliberately skips or delegates it** — never because a mechanism fired (a hearted note, a ticked card).

| Piece | Done when |
|---|---|
| 1. Purpose and audience | the client agrees a short statement of what the site must achieve and for whom |
| 2. The reason to pick them | the story and headline are agreed, or the client chose to keep it about the product or service |
| 3. Material | every planned page has its real content (offerings, prices or the pricing approach, contact, proof), or a decision to go without |
| 4. The look | the look direction is agreed, delegated, or "keep my current look" |
| 5. The site plan | the pages, and what's on each, are agreed |
| 6. Build | every planned page is built and passes the floor tests |
| 7. Refine | the client says it's right (their call) |
| 8. Final checks | the floor tests pass across the site |
| 9. Publish | the site is live |

Today's client-facing stages fit inside these.

## 4. Flexibility: the client's own view of 2 and 3

A few choices, made **in conversation** at the start, recorded as the client's decisions, changeable later (the consultant re-plans when they change):

- **Story:** personal (the owner and their journey) or about the product or service.
- **Scope:** a single-page shop front or a full site.
- **Starting point:** start fresh, or keep and improve what they have.
- **Prices:** shown, a guide, or "contact us".
- **Involvement:** "walk me through everything" or "just build it and show me".

The floor (§2) stays whatever they choose.

## 5. The flow: gather, align, take stock, plan, work

0. **Gather first: ask for what exists, in the very first exchange.** Their website if they have one, social pages, and uploads: photos of their work, flyers, price lists, menus, anything written about them. It costs the client almost nothing and is the highest-value information the consultant can get, so the opening asks for it alongside "tell me about your business". The consultant reads it in the background (site captures and uploads are processed while the conversation goes on) and uses it to make every later question specific ("I see you run kids' classes — tell me about those"), rather than asking what the material already answers. If they have nothing, that's fine and is said once, not chased.

   **Social pages: use the platforms' own exports.** A capture of a social link usually hits a login wall, and copying content out piece by piece is tedious and lossy. The major platforms each offer a one-file export of the owner's own content: Facebook and Instagram ("Download your information", Meta Accounts Center), LinkedIn ("Get a copy of your data") and Google Business Profile (Google Takeout, which includes reviews, posts and photos). So:
   - **The consultant gives one short instruction per platform:** where to request the export, which options to pick (all time, high-quality media, HTML or JSON), and "when the email arrives, upload the file here". These live in the system knowledge base, one entry per platform, kept current.
   - **Uploads accept these exports** (today ZIP isn't accepted): the host unpacks a recognised export and files its photos, posts and captions, profile text and reviews into the Library as ordinary material, so the consultant can read and ask about them like any other upload.
   - **Exports aren't instant** (minutes to a day or more), so the consultant asks for them in the first exchange and carries on; when the file arrives it's folded in.
   - Pasting text, screenshots and individual photos still work; they're just not the main route.
   - This is DOC-71 §12.1's decision (2026-10-26: guided exports, upload the zip; public link fetches best effort; official API access later; never ask for a password). It was never ticketed.
1. **Align on what "great" means for them — a conversation, not a conclusion.** The consultant asks, and brings a perspective (for example: "Right now your site is a shop front. Some owners find a bit of their own story brings in the customers they really want. Interested, or is the shop front how you want it?"). It can show what that would look like (a sample line, a section idea) rather than describe it. The client decides, and can change their mind.
2. **Take stock — their reactions, not just an inventory.** What exists (current site, social pages, documents, photos) is **evidence of what was done, not of what they want**: someone else may have made it, or they've moved on. So the consultant asks what they like and want to keep, what they dislike, and what better would look like for them, and brings its own observations ("your site never says where you are") as things to discuss. With no existing site, the same conversation runs over other sites (the comparison board is a tool for it). Only then are the nine pieces marked done, partly done, or to rethink — "done" meaning they're happy with it.
3. **Plan.** The consultant proposes the plan: what's already done, what needs work, in what order, roughly how long. The client agrees or adjusts it.
4. **Work through the gaps,** using whichever tools fit (§7), holding the next move (§6).

**Worked example — an existing shop-front site that says nothing about its owner.** The consultant doesn't conclude she wants a shop front; it asks, and offers a glimpse of what her story could add. If she says "just the shop", the site stays product-led and there's no story board; if "actually, I'd like people to know me", the story enters the plan. She says what she likes and dislikes about the current site and what she wishes it did; the consultant adds what it noticed; together they decide what stays. The plan follows from her answers, not from what the old site happened to be.

## 6. Initiative: the consultant always holds a next move against the aligned plan

The one structural rule that replaces most of the process. **Every turn ends with the consultant either doing something (said, with an honest estimate) or having asked the client one clear, specific question**, and that move serves the plan the client agreed — never "whenever you like", never both sides waiting.

**It follows the client.** If the client wants to address something else, the consultant goes with them: their concern becomes the next move. When it's dealt with, the consultant brings the plan back ("That's done. Next on our plan is the classes page — shall we?"). If the detour changes the plan, the plan is updated with them. The plan is a shared map, not a script. It's driven by:

- **The definition of done (§2):** the consultant can always see what's missing.
- **The plan's gaps (§5):** each turn closes the most valuable one, by building, asking or suggesting.
- **Ownership:** "You own this site's success. Don't wait to be told: propose, build, show."

(REQ-428's executive-function check is close to this already.)

## 7. Tools are means, not ends

The interaction tools stay: questions, the story board, the position card, headline options, the comparison board, Looks, image and clip generation, the builder. The consultant reaches for one because the plan has a gap it fills ("I don't yet know why people pick this business — let's do the story board"), and skips it when it doesn't help this client. A client's own headline is just their headline; nothing has to be hearted before something else can happen.

**Graphics:** no hand-drawn SVG for logos or illustrations. A logo starts as a wordmark in the site's own type (real text), and marks come from image generation, which handles lettering well now. Simple drawings stay where they're genuinely simple.

- No change the client didn't agree to after the hand-over (REQ-623, REQ-628).

### A.14 Decided with the operator (2026-11-02)

1. **The first draft: visible while it builds, invited only when it's ready.**
   - The client can watch the preview fill in as each page is built (it does today) and can comment at any time. The talker takes comments as feedback.
   - The consultant doesn't invite them to look ("your first draft is ready") until every page is built and has had its quiet review, and the fixes are in.
   - To keep that wait short, each page is reviewed as soon as it is built, so the review overlaps the build rather than following it.
   - What waits is the invitation, not visibility: no curtain.
2. **One check before the first build: the client has agreed the plan** (§5.3: the pages, what goes on each, the look). It replaces `design_first`, `structure_first` and `effect_first`. To be judged in the run-throughs; drop it if it gets in the way.
3. **The speak-first gate stays.**
4. **Home length: plan ahead, then no hold.**
   - When it proposes the site plan, the consultant sizes home for a phone. The host gives it home's phone-screen budget and the rough height of each kind of section.
   - The plan then keeps home within budget, or the client knowingly chooses a long home.
   - After a build the host still reports a long home, and the consultant may mention it once. Nothing is held.
5. **Rule 2 is rewritten:** bring a perspective early (§5.1), but change nothing the client hasn't agreed.
6. **REQ-621's hidden places and REQ-632's pin cap are removed.**

### A.15 Rollout: straight to the new model

There is no gradual, cluster-by-cluster transition. The current approach is the thing being replaced, so preserving parts we've decided to remove would only spend time on them.

1. Build the new model in one cutover, as **REQ-638** (one ticket, many commits). That means the plan and the nine pieces, the opening rewrite, the initiative rule, guides as a playbook, and every **Remove** above, with the **Keep** and **Tool** items carried across.
2. Judge it on the five run-throughs (§9).
3. Revise from there.
