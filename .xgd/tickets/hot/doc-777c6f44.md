## What this document is for

A client sits in a browser with a chat pane and a live preview of their own website.
On the other side are **two AIs with different costs, different speeds and different
jobs**, and behind those, cheap workers doing construction. This document is the
experience design for that session: who the participants are, what each one is for,
what the client sees while they work, what each AI can perceive and do, and what the
room they share actually is.

It covers both halves deliberately, because they are the same design:

- **UX** — what the client experiences. Nine silent minutes, a plan they can correct, a
  question they can answer without vocabulary, a consultant they can interrupt.
- **AX** — what an AI experiences. Whether it can see its own cost, whether anything can
  reach it while it works, whether it can read what it decided, whether it knows why its
  turn ended.

The two are inseparable because the client's experience of this product **is** the
agents' experience of it, one layer out. A consultant who cannot see its own context
pressure produces a client who waits nine minutes for a truncated reply.

**Scope boundary.** This document is the *what and why* of the session. The mechanism
that makes it possible — how a working turn is reached, how budgets are published, how a
cursor over a transcript works — is [[DOC-61]], which is the implementation half of §5,
§6 and §7 here. Commissioned construction (the consultant handing work to a cheap
worker) is [[DOC-60]]. The consultation method is [[DOC-33]]. This document is the frame
those three sit inside.

Finding 14 of [[EPIC-19]] is the conversation this came out of; §14.1–§14.17 carry the
measurements and the arguments. This document carries the design.

## 1. The participants

The working names from Finding 14 are **Alice** and **Bob**; the roles are what matter
and are what this document uses.

| participant | model / nature | what they are for | turn length |
|---|---|---|---|
| **the client** | human | says what they want, in their own words, badly and by degrees | whenever they like |
| **the consultant** | Opus-class (`backends.json` → `claude`, `claude-opus-5`) | judgement, taste, register, the design decisions, the client relationship | **2–10 minutes** |
| **the interrogator** | cheap/fast (`claude_builder` → `claude-haiku-4-5`) | plan and position, bookkeeping, answering immediately, **applying pressure against the brief** | **~1 second** |
| **workers** | same cheap model, transient | construction — L1 writes against a bounded brief; cannot converse, cannot see the client, report once ([[DOC-60]]) | one turn |
| **the surfaces** | not participants | the live preview, the plan panel, the cards, the change signal | — |

Two things follow from the turn-length column alone, and most of this document is
downstream of them.

**The cost asymmetry is the design constraint.** Measured in [[DOC-60]]: $0.54 per
element write by the consultant against $0.046 by a worker. The expensive participant
must spend its turns on judgement and nothing else.

**The latency asymmetry is the UX constraint.** A one-second participant and a
ten-minute participant in the same room cannot be given the same affordances. Everything
in §6 about badges, interrupts and queues is an answer to this one fact.

## 2. What the session is like today, on both sides

### What the client experiences

- They type, and either get a reply or watch the pane do nothing for nine minutes. **Nine
  silent minutes read as failure**, not as work.
- If they think of something mid-wait, they can type it and it is **thrown away**
  ([[BUG-122]]) — and if they reload to recover the text, it is gone.
- When the reply comes, it may be a truncated fragment of a plan, and nothing tells them
  so. They learn to poke the consultant until it finishes.
- They cannot see a plan, correct a plan, or tell whether the thing being built is the
  thing they asked for until they look at the preview themselves.
- They are expected to push back — *"do you really think this looks premium?"* — in a
  register nobody taught them. The operator's own words: *"I can't train the user to keep
  pushing like that."*

### What the consultant experiences

Its own account, recorded in [[REQ-284]]:

> **I get no warning.** This is the part I would most want you to know. I do not
> experience the cutoff. There is no signal, no error, no sense of pressure — the turn
> simply ends […] From the inside it is indistinguishable from having finished.
>
> Right now I am driving with no fuel gauge.

**The phenomenology is real; the diagnosis in it is not.** The consultant reached for the
gauge metaphor because a cutoff with no warning feels like running out of fuel, and Finding
5 accepted that reading. The silent stop is **iteration-cap exhaustion** in the tool loop,
with the wall-clock timeout as the other exit — neither is a context event. The fix is
therefore to publish the budget that actually binds, not to show the model a resource the
host already manages (a rolling window, two forms of summary, chunk-level search over what
falls out). [[DOC-61]] §F2 and [[REQ-344]] carry that argument; it matters here because
*"I get no warning"* is a true report of a misidentified cause, and the design must answer
the cause.

Four more, established in this epic's findings:

- **It cannot be reached while it works.** Ten minutes with no inbound channel, by
  assumption — an assumption [[DOC-61]] shows to be false.
- **It cannot read what it decided.** The engagement ledger is write-only ([[REQ-283]]):
  it records a decision and cannot see it next turn, so it re-derives state, and
  re-deriving state is what the screenshots were for.
- **It has no position.** Finding 13 diagnosed it as positionless — running the playbook's
  stages out of order, declaring things "locked" that did not survive the next page, and
  carrying process language in **2 of 102 turns** when coached to.
- **It does not know why its turn ended.** Exhaustion, timeout and completion are
  indistinguishable from the inside.

**These are the same list.** A participant that cannot see its budget, cannot be
interrupted, cannot read its own decisions and cannot tell why it stopped will produce
exactly the client experience above.

## 3. The division of labour

Marked by status, because this was argued over several sittings and not everything is
settled.

### Settled

**The client is never mediated.** *Filter the agents, never the client.* The consultant
receives the client's words verbatim — the hesitation, the phrasing, the self-correction
— because unmediated contact with how the client said it is the whole of its value. No
composition rule may ever compress that channel. (This is what kills any design where a
cheap model briefs an expensive one: the Wix-ADI failure shape, where the cheap
orchestrator decides what the expensive designer is allowed to know.)

**It is a group, not a pipeline.** All three on one stream; both agents address the
client directly; the interrogator's turns run concurrently with the consultant's rather
than in series. An earlier position had the interrogator out of the conversational path
entirely; it was withdrawn, because its objection was to a *relay* (client → interrogator
→ consultant → back) and does not apply to a room. The human precedent — a consultant and
an assistant both in the meeting — is real.

**The client can address the interrogator.** This is the retained gain from conceding the
group: correcting the plan becomes a cheap remark to the assistant rather than a challenge
to the consultant's judgement.

**A group chat in the UI need not be a group chat in the context.** The client sees three
participants. The consultant receives the client verbatim plus plan state as compact fact
— **not** the interrogator's transcript. Otherwise the clerical chatter lands in the
expensive context at full fidelity and the whole point is lost.

**Write authority splits by kind.** The consultant owns **decisions** — judgement. The
interrogator owns **task state and position** — bookkeeping. The interrogator must not be
able to open or settle a decision, or design calls land on the cheap model.

**One shared representation, not two.** The interrogator keeps no notes of its own. A
planner whose picture comes from a summary while the craftsperson's comes from the live
conversation will diverge, and the client will hear it.

**The interrogator's output is state, not prose.** It reconciles what happened against
the plan and writes task state; that state reaches the consultant as *fact* through the
per-turn channel already proven to work. The argument is measured: coaching prose was
carried in 2 of 102 turns, while the page digest is demonstrably worked from.

**The interrogator speaks only when it acts.** With one exception — the first time, to
teach the client that it is there and what it does. Silence from it is therefore
informative.

**The interrogator may narrate the wait.** The one place a second voice is unambiguously
additive: nine silent minutes read as failure, and a cheap fast agent saying what the
consultant is doing costs almost nothing. **Narrator of a wait, never broker of an
exchange.**

**Bound what the consultant may create.** Measured over one engagement: 61 standing-note
rewrites and 45 decisions. Given a task list it will generate forty items unless the
shape forbids it. Tasks are mostly instantiated from the plan, with a cap.

### The interrogator's real job: pressure

The operator's observation, which reframed this whole role:

> If I ask the consultant *"do you really think this looks like a premium site?"* I get
> some great answers — but I can't train the user to keep pushing like that. However, I
> can get Bob to do it.

A challenge is a **position-forcing device**: *"the brief says premium — does this page
read premium?"* leaves no way to answer without taking a position on the artifact. Finding
13 proposed a supply-side remedy (give the consultant state and expect leadership to
follow) and measured it failing at 2-in-102. This is the **demand-side** mechanism for the
same diagnosis, and it is the better bet for an evidenced reason: **the capability is
already observed** — the operator gets good answers when pushing — so the mechanism only
has to trigger it, not create it.

**Why a cheap model can do this.** Posing a good challenge does not require design
judgement; *answering* one does. A challenge is a scripted move against a stated goal and
an artifact. That asymmetry is what keeps taste on the expensive model while the pressure
comes from the cheap one.

**And it models the behaviour for the client.** A client watching the interrogator ask
*"does this read premium?"* learns that this is a thing one may ask. That is the answer to
*"I can't train the user"* — you do not train them, you show them. It addresses the
register problem socially rather than by inference.

**Two failure modes, both real:**

1. **Ritual.** A challenge that always fires becomes noise, and the consultant learns a
   ritual defence — both models pattern-match. Challenges must be anchored to an artifact
   and a stated goal, and must be able to **not fire**.
2. **Visible correction.** The client watches their consultant being pushed. Read one way
   that is rigour; read another it is a consultant who needed correcting. Which one it is
   depends entirely on the interrogator's register: it challenges **against the brief**,
   never against its own opinion of the site. It has no opinion of the site. That is not
   modesty, it is the rule that keeps taste off the cheap model.

### The interrogator may stop the consultant — on an imperative

*"Stop her"* fires. Anything weaker becomes a card with a button. This is safe to give the
cheap model for a reason that cuts the opposite way from suppression: **a wrong interrupt
is loud and cheap** (the consultant restarts), while a wrong suppression is silent and the
consultation simply never happened.

Style that card's button as the consultant's own activity badge, so the client learns the
badge is the direct route. **A card that teaches its own redundancy has done its job.**

### Open

- **Where the shared structure lives** — business, site, or conversation. See §8.
- **How much the interrogator narrates** before narration becomes chatter.
- **Whether the plan panel and the ledger are one object or two views.**

## 4. The room

### The four topologies, and the answer

A *session* is an LLM history; an *agent* is a model plus a session. The room is whatever
object holds what all three participants have said.

| | the room is | consequence |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | the consultant's session | every interrogator turn sits in the expensive context at full fidelity. Defeats the context goal unless views are composed. **Incrementally buildable and reversible** — switch the interrogator off and the product is exactly today's. |
| 2 | the interrogator's session | right economics, wrong epistemics: the consultant loses continuity and is *summoned with a brief* composed by the cheap model. The Wix-ADI failure shape. |
| 3 | **nobody's — a durable artifact all three participate in** | cleanest. Each agent's view is *already* a projection, so composing views is the normal mechanism rather than an exclusion list. Forces the addressing discipline to be designed explicitly. Most work. |
| 4 | — | not a topology: the reframe that a participant's view is *composed* rather than *filtered*. Applying it to 1 or 2 turns either into 3. |

**The answer is 3.** Four reasons, and the first is decisive:

1. **Compaction is a property of a context, so a room inside one inherits it.** A room
   held in an agent's conversation contains two things with different lifetimes — the
   shared record and that agent's private working state. When it compacts under its *own*
   context pressure, the shared record is summarised away by an operation that has nothing
   to do with the record's value.
2. **A restart truncates it silently.** A warm API conversation is bounded and a cold one
   re-seeded from a **window**, so a room-inside-a-context survives a process restart only
   as far back as the window reaches.
3. **Whoever owns the room owns the medium.** A chair holds no domain — and a chair that
   also *is* the transcript is a lossy relay by construction. Every question about what
   the client actually said becomes a question about its summarising.
4. **Read rates are wildly asymmetric, and a cursor absorbs that.** Ten minutes, one
   second, and a human. A cursor lets each read at its own rate — and lets the consultant
   read **mid-turn**. Inside someone's context there is no cursor to be at.

**The client is already a reader of this kind:** the browser scrolls a durable transcript
it does not own. Putting the room inside one agent's context would make the two halves of
the product disagree about what the room is.

**A correction to the record.** Finding 14.10 recorded "topology 2 conceded — the room is
the `chat_transcript` comment that already exists". The *substance* of that — the durable
transcript on the chat ticket — is topology **3**; only the label was wrong, because the
cheap model was described as "holding" it. Finding 14.7's own analysis favoured 3
independently. There is no disagreement to resolve, only a mislabel.

### Delivery is asymmetric even though ownership is nobody's

This is the part no general group-chat component does, and it falls straight out of §1's
latency table:

- **push** the delta to the cheap fast member — its context is small, the delta is small,
  and making it `pull` before answering would double a one-second turn;
- **notify and let it pull** for the expensive slow member, for whom reading is a real
  decision with a real price.

Two rules carried in from prior work: a **`pass` must not advance the pointer** (saying
"nothing from me" must cost the others nothing), and the feed is **derived** from the
record rather than written beside it — a written change-log was already rejected for being
rewritten on every append, a contention point, and unbounded growth in one body.

### Build order: start at 1, treat the room as an artifact from day one

Topology 1 is the only incrementally buildable and reversible option. The way to get 3
without a refactor is to build 1 **while treating the room as an artifact**, with the
consultant's session a *view* that currently happens to be complete. Then composing views
is a dial rather than a rewrite. Starting at 2 makes an architectural bet on the cheap
model's judgement that is hard to undo.

### The gate, and the hazard

**No standing frame, no room.** A member that only pulls deltas has no standing summary of
the engagement — the thing a member holding the whole conversation gets for free.
[[REQ-283]] item 2 decided that frame (`fields.frame` on the chat ticket, rewritten in
place, bounded). The room cannot ship in front of it.

**The loop hazard.** Two agents that can each wake the other have no natural stopping
point, and the interrogator's purpose is to *push*. That pressure is toward more exchanges,
not fewer. This needs a turn-taking rule at the product level and not merely a spend cap —
e.g. a client message opens a round, the interrogator may challenge once per consultant
turn, and the consultant's answer closes it. **Unbounded agent-to-agent chatter in front of
a waiting client is the failure the client actually sees.**

## 5. What the client sees

### The stream

Three participants, plainly distinguishable. The client's own messages, the consultant's
prose, and the interrogator's — rarer, and always attached to something it did.

**Queued messages are visible as queued.** Text typed while a turn is running gets a
pending bubble, is durable immediately, and is coalesced: everything typed during one turn
arrives as one thing. The affordance already exists upstream in `webui-chat`; what was
missing here was the transport and the delivery point.

**And it reaches the consultant *before* her report.** Upstream delivers a queued batch
after the turn, as a new turn — which would have the consultant announce a finished
homepage into a conversation that abandoned it four minutes ago. Mid-turn delivery is the
design; after-the-turn is the fallback, and reads as one.

### Activity, and the one control that means something

**Both workers get an activity indicator**, so the client can see there are two. Only the
consultant's is worth interrupting: a one-second turn's indicator flickers, and offering a
stop control there teaches the client that the control does nothing.

The consultant's indicator carries **what she is doing**, sourced from her own milestone
posts rather than a spinner. A milestone is a write to task state, so the label and the
plan agree without a second surface.

A turn that ends at its budget must leave the indicator reading **stopped**, not *still
working*. A stuck progress bar is the silent-truncation failure wearing a new costume.

### The plan is a panel, not a message

A stream is chronological; a plan is current. A to-do list pasted into history is stale
misinformation six turns later — and a stream widget demands a response where a panel can
be glanced at and ignored, which is what makes it structure rather than hoops.

**A card is a projection of the shared structure, not a message.** This is what makes the
plan and the widgets one feature rather than two: a card can be mutated in place, so it
updates live; and transcript replay is markdown-only, so a card that *were* a message would
vanish on reload. Both properties follow from "projection", and neither is available to a
one-shot stream event.

### Widgets are for moments, and never forms

- **A widget's value is that it is answerable without vocabulary.** Two rendered pages and
  *"which of these?"* needs no shared language — which is the register problem solved by
  construction rather than by instruction.
- **It must never be a form.** The client can always ignore it and type instead.
- **Questionnaires only for facts only the client has** — hours, phone number, service
  list. **Never for taste.** Taste is settled by choosing between rendered artifacts, not
  by answering questions about them.
- **A feature checklist is *what we build first*, not a catalogue.** A checkbox list gives
  every item equal visual weight, implying equal cost, and "contact form" and "recurring
  payments" are not the same size.

## 6. What each AI experiences

The AX half, stated as capabilities the participants either have or lack.

| the participant can… | today | designed |
|---|---|---|
| know what the call it is about to make will cost | yes — priced at the point of the call ([[REQ-284]]) | unchanged. **Not** a context-occupancy gauge: the window is host-managed and a percentage buys the model no decision ([[DOC-61]] §F2, [[REQ-344]] abandoned) |
| be reached while it works | no | at every tool call — up to fifty per turn ([[DOC-61]]) |
| know what it is spending and how much is left | no | warnings at 75% / 90%, countdown from 95%, in three currencies |
| know why its turn ended | no — exhaustion is indistinguishable from completion | exhaustion arrives as an **instruction to write the report** |
| read what it decided | no — the ledger is write-only | ledger read plus a bounded standing frame ([[REQ-283]]) |
| learn that something arrived without paying to read it | no | a notification carrying **a count and a cursor**, never the content |
| read another participant's room | no | the same cursor operation, pointed at a different record |
| say it has reached a milestone | no | a write to task state, which the plan panel and the activity badge both read |
| hand construction to something cheaper | yes, and it almost never does | [[DOC-60]] — commissioned rather than performed |

Two principles run through that table.

**Arrive unasked.** The framework states the rule in its own gauge's design note: *"It is a
priming entry and never an operation: the failure being fixed is that cost is invisible at
the moment of choosing, and a session that thought to check how full it was would not have
needed to."* The rule is right even where that particular gauge was wrong — it governs queue
notifications, budget warnings and the price of a call. **A capability the participant has to
remember to use is a capability it will not use.**

**Meter what actually binds.** The corollary, learned the hard way: a signal is only worth a
packet if the participant can *act* on it. Iterations and wall-clock bind absolutely — at the
last call the turn ends, and only the host knows the count. Context does not bind, because the
host rolls the window, keeps two summaries and leaves the rest searchable. Showing a
non-binding resource does not produce care, it produces false economy.

**Notify with a count, charge for the content.** The complement of arriving unasked: a
notification must be small enough to arrive on every request, so it carries a number and a
cursor and nothing else. Spending context on the content is then the participant's own
decision, made with the gauge in front of it.

**And a standing caution.** Every packet in that table is one the participant may read and
ignore — except the budget, which the host enforces. Finding 13's 2-in-102 is the reason to
build the host-enforced half first and to distrust any design whose safety depends on the
model behaving well.

## 7. The shared structure

The plan, the task list, the decisions and the position. Today, partially:

| what | where | state |
|---|---|---|
| engagement ledger (`### Decision N`) | the chat ticket's **body** — because the knowledge component indexes title and body and **not** comments | exists, write-only |
| standing frame | `fields.frame` on the same chat ticket | decided, [[REQ-283]] |
| the transcript | `chat_transcript` comment on the chat ticket | exists, durable |
| **a plan, a task list, a position, state on a decision** | — | **does not exist** |

The ledger is 47 flat entries with no section, no open/parked/superseded, no ordering.
Finding 13.9 saw the same gap from the method side.

**The unsettled question, and it is load-bearing:** *what does the structure belong to* —
the business, the site, or the conversation? The identity matters because [[DOC-33]] says
Act I *"runs once per client, ever"*, while a real engagement spans days, many sittings and
several lost sessions. **A plan keyed on the conversation dies with the conversation** —
and this epic has watched that ticket brick twice and be repaired by hand once. Analysis
favours **the business**: it is the only identity that matches "once per client, ever" and
survives a lost conversation. Settle this before building the structure.

## 8. The method, and what the interrogator is really fixing

[[DOC-33]] is the consultation playbook. Finding 13 measured one engagement against it and
found **the playbook and the session did not run the same process**: the order was
inverted, two stages with no artifact never happened at all, "locked" did not survive
contact with the next page, and the method is written where the consultant cannot reach it.

The remedy is not more prose. Coaching language reached 2 of 102 turns. The remedy is
structural and social:

- **structural** — the position lives in the shared structure, written by the participant
  whose job is bookkeeping, and arrives as fact;
- **social** — the pressure comes from the interrogator, in front of the client, in the
  client's hearing, against the brief.

The second is the part that no amount of priming achieves, and it is the reason this design
has a second agent at all.

## 9. What this design is not

- **Not a broker.** The interrogator never stands between the client and the consultant.
- **Not a second opinion on design.** It has no view of the site. Challenges are made
  against the brief.
- **Not nested delegation.** Workers do not delegate; one level, enforced in the grant
  ([[DOC-60]]).
- **Not interruption mid-thought.** The consultant can be reached at its next tool call.
  A long delegation with no intervening call is unreachable, and fifty iterations is a
  ceiling ([[DOC-61]]).
- **Not a group-chat component adoption.** The existing one generalises turn-order,
  nomination, membership and rounds over an arbitrary roster; this is a fixed roster of
  three with a human in it and asymmetric delivery. Take its conclusions, not its code.
- **Not a new voice for its own sake.** The interrogator earns its turns by acting. If it
  is talking without acting, it is chatter and the design has failed.
- **Not a questionnaire product.** Taste is settled by choosing between rendered artifacts.

## 10. How we will know it worked

**Client-side**

- Minutes of unexplained silence per session — today up to nine at a stretch.
- Words typed during a turn that never reach the session — today all of them.
- Sessions where the client corrects the plan — today impossible; a plan nobody ever
  corrects is a plan nobody reads.
- Client-visible agent-to-agent exchanges per client message — the loop hazard made
  countable. A room that raises this has failed however elegant its cursor is.
- Turns the operator (or client) has to poke to completion — the habit is the measurement.

**Agent-side**

- Turns ending at a cap without a report — today unobservable, target zero.
- Screenshots per turn against reported occupancy — the whole claim of the gauge is that a
  visible cost changes the choice at the moment of choosing. If it does not, the gauge is
  not the instrument we thought it was.
- Share of element writes performed by workers — today 27% ([[DOC-60]]).
- Challenges that fired and were answered with a *changed* artifact, against challenges
  answered with a defence. A challenge that never changes anything is ritual.
- Turns carrying process/position language — today 2 of 102, and the reason this design is
  structural rather than prose.
