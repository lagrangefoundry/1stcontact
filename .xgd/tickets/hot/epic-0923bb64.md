## Why this epic exists

A single hour of design work in the builder chat cost **$20** (2026-09-08, site-1stcontact). Claude was costing more than minimum wage to draw one hero segment, and the session felt slow as well as expensive. This epic holds what we learned, what was fixed, and what is still owed.

The whole diagnosis, the arithmetic behind every number below, and the design argument for delegation are in the chat transcript on **CHAT-47** (`chat-5011da11`) — 2026-09-09 to 2026-09-10. Read that for the reasoning; this body is the standing summary.

## What was actually wrong

Not the model. The request shape.

Every request is one prefix — `[tools][system][messages]` — and Anthropic's cache matches it from the start. Two consequences govern everything:

- a change invalidates everything **after** it, never before it;

- therefore anything that changes often must sit as **late** in the prefix as possible.

A tool-using turn is not one request. After every tool result the backend calls the API again carrying the whole conversation. The measured 2026-09-08 session was **15 exchanges but 117 round trips** — one turn alone ran 41 tool calls.

`cache_control` was applied to the **system assembly only**. The message history — including every screenshot — was re-sent uncached on all 117 round trips. Cost was quadratic in turn count. Measured breakdown of the ~70k of accumulated context: screenshots 33%, knowledge search 16%, `get_l1` 14%, `set_l1` 11% — but screenshots were only ~2% of tokens _billed_, because the expense was never their size, it was being re-sent 117 times.

Two aggravating facts found alongside it:

- **No token telemetry.** `usage` on the `message_start` frame was read and discarded, so spend could only be modelled, never observed.

- `max_tokens`** hardcoded at 4096.** A large `set_l1` payload hit the ceiling, the stream ended with no `content_block_stop`, and the tool was dispatched with an **empty input object** — silently. Observed twelve consecutive times in one session (2026-09-09, Lagrange Foundry site), 34% of all tool calls in that session failing, and the model retrying blind each time.

## The fix, and one thing we got wrong first

Caching the message history was the change with the best ratio, and it landed. But the first version only helped **within** a turn, not across turns — because the per-turn reminder (which carries "the site moved under you") sat at the end of `system`, and `system` precedes `messages`. Every turn the reminder changed, it invalidated the entire history behind it. 1stcontact's reminders change on nearly every turn by design.

That gap was ours: the original acceptance criterion said "within a turn" and never mentioned the second _turn_. The follow-up moved the volatile and reminder tiers **past** the history, appended to the final user message at request-build time and never written back into stored history. The principle was generalised, not reversed: stable first, volatile last — applied to the whole request rather than to one segment of it, because the request is the unit the cache matches on. Recorded as **DOC-22 Amendment X**.

Observed in production after the fixes landed: **$1.67 for a 38-minute session** — and 12 of that session's 48 round trips were wasted on the truncation bug, so the useful work cost less still.

## What is live in 1stcontact today

- `claude-opus-5`**, **`max_tokens`** 64000**, set in `tools/generate/src/cli/ai/backends.json` (BUG-67). The model is now this repository's decision, not a framework release — that seam is the part of BUG-49 we asked for.

- Message-history caching and per-request token telemetry are in the installed shared store and active on every session.

- The truncation failure that produced twelve empty `set_l1` calls is fixed on both halves: the ceiling is roomy, and an unfinished tool block is refused rather than dispatched.

Note the consequence of the Opus move: **the front face is now the expensive model.** Opus 5 is $5/$25 against Sonnet's $3/$15, and thinking is on by default. That is the right trade for judgement — what the thing is, what it means, which direction to take — and the wrong one for the mechanical majority of the work, where the payload is large and the decisions are small. Which is what the remaining work is about.

## What is still owed

1. **Re-measure, from telemetry rather than a model.** Every number above for the current configuration is an estimate; REQ-143/145 mean real `cache_read_input_tokens` and per-request spend are now recorded. Confirm cross-turn reads are non-zero and scale with history, and establish a fresh per-session baseline on Opus 5. The CHAT-47 figures predate the Opus move and are no longer comparable.

2. **Effort levels — the cheap lever, still not taken.** The backend sends no `output_config` at all. Effort does not touch the cache prefix, so it is a per-turn dial with no architectural cost: low for mechanical construction turns, high for strategy. Not implemented anywhere in the framework yet. Worth doing **before** delegation, and this epic should not be used to skip it.

3. **Adopt the delegation surface.** REQ-148 shipped and `delegation_surface.json` is installed in the shared store, but 1stcontact grants nothing from it — `instances.json` declares one role, `consultant`, and no worker. Adoption means: a builder role with a narrower grant, a backend configured for that role's workers, `DelegateWork` granted to the consultant and `ReportDelegatedWork` to the builder.

The shape the framework enforces, and which we should not fight:

- the brief is **prose** (intent cannot be enumerated), but authority comes from the **role's declared grant** — no phrasing of a goal widens it;

- the worker's conversation never enters the caller's, so the construction transcript stops being billed at the consultant's rate;

- **no nesting**, and worker spend **rolls up** into the caller's ledger, so this cannot make cost invisible again;

- `accept` checks are the load-bearing part. If Opus has to screenshot and re-inspect everything the worker did, the tokens have been moved to the more expensive side rather than saved.

The likely split, from the existing capability groups: builder keeps ReadSite, AuthorPages, ManageComponents, MeasureDrawings, DrawImages, SeeSite; consultant keeps ManagePages, WriteConfig and ManagePalette — brand colours, site structure and config are the decisions worth Opus.

4. **Choose the worker's model from measurement, not from estimate.** On the 2026-09-08 workload the bulk of spend was construction (21 screenshots, 21 `write_image`, 18 `set_l1`, 15 `get_l1`). Haiku 4.5 at $1/$5 would do that roughly 3× cheaper than Sonnet does today, with the strategic layer on Opus small enough that its rate barely matters. That is an argument for trying, not a sizing — item 1 supplies the sizing.

## Explicitly not the approach

**Per-turn model switching within one conversation** — Opus for strategy turns, Sonnet for construction turns, same session. Prompt caches are keyed per model. Alternating A/B/A/B roughly halves the hit rate and pays the writes twice, undoing most of what REQ-143/144 bought. Delegation exists precisely because each side then keeps its own clean cache lineage.

## Measurement: the Lagrange Foundry build (2026-09-09 → 2026-09-21)

Reconstructed 2026-09-21 from the session's own records — the `tool_transcript`
and `chat_transcript` comments on **CHAT-3** (`chat-50932534`, in the
control-app's ticket store, not the xgd one) and the 768 R2 audit objects under
`audit/biz_5b10…/`. Audit and transcript align call-for-call, 0 mismatches over
758 calls, so counts, order and true result sizes are **measured**.

**The dollar figures are calibrated, not derived.** Two observations from the
operator's account pin them: **$166.50 of API spend across 2026-09-01 → 09-21**,
and **at least $75 on 09-20 alone** (seven $10–11 top-ups). A first pass
modelled $252 for the build and a second $55; neither survived contact with
those numbers. What the calibration fixes is the one thing the records cannot
show — how long the Worker isolate holding `messages[]` survived between turns —
and the answer is **about an hour**, which is what a long-running local
`wrangler dev` process gives you rather than the seconds a production isolate
gets.

**This is why item 1 matters more than anything else in this epic.** REQ-143/145
capture `usage` and put `{usage, requests}` on the turn's terminal `done` event.
**1stcontact persists none of it.** `apps/control-app/src/ai.ts` flushes an
audit record per *tool call* and nothing per *request*;
`cache_read_input_tokens` appears nowhere in this repository or in either
transcript. Two rounds of this analysis were wrong by 4.5× and 2.4× in opposite
directions, and both errors were about an unobservable the API already reports.
One write beside the audit flush ends the guessing.

The shape is now clear: **cost is dominated by long tool-using turns replaying

the 56th carries everything the first 55 produced. That is the same quadratic
the 09-08 diagnosis found, surviving the caching fix because a cache read still
costs 10% and 10% of a very large number 56 times is not small.

### The levers, measured

Each line includes the ones above it. They are not additive — each makes the
tokens the next would have saved cheaper.

| | 09-20 | $/hour | whole build |
|---|---|---|---|
| As it ran | **$75** | **$29** | $107 |
| Window 40 → 10 exchanges | $53 | $20 | $81 |
| + tool definitions deferred (~13k → ~2k tokens/request) | $50 | $19 | $74 |
| + cache TTL 1h → 5min (write 1.25× not 2×) — **config total** | **$39** | **$15** | $61 |
| + a window trim that preserves the cache prefix (framework code) | $30 | $11 | $50 |
| + low effort on construction turns | $25 | $10 | $42 |

The first three are settings. They roughly **halve** the cost and need no new
code in either repository.

Two notes on the table. **Window 20 is worse than window 40** (~$111): a smaller
window slides more often, and every slide drops the oldest exchange off the
*front* of `messages[]`, which invalidates the byte-prefix the cache matches on.
Only at 10 does the smaller payload outrun the extra invalidation. And the
fourth line is the same effect attacked directly — `boundDialogue`'s front-drop
and `ageImages`' in-place redaction each rewrite the prefix, and at the observed
isolate lifetime that is worth ~$9 on 09-20.

### Cost per hour

Charging the operator's turns at a typing rate rather than at the wall-clock gap
— the session was multitasked, and the gaps are not work. On 09-20: 1.58 h of
measured machine time plus 1.03 h of typing at 40 wpm = **2.60 h engaged**.
Whole build: 3.18 h machine + 2.18 h typing = 5.36 h.

**$29/hour as it ran; $15/hour on settings alone; $4/hour with delegation.**
Against the $20/hour measured on 2026-09-08, the caching work already landed —
09-20 was a far heavier session per hour than that one — and the remaining
headroom is large and mostly cheap.

### What this changes about the path forward

1. **Persist the turn's `usage` at the host.** One write beside the audit flush
   in `ai.ts`. Nothing else here can be confirmed without it.
2. **Dial the window down to 10** — REQ-126's own stated experiment, never run,
   the largest single configuration lever at ~29%.
3. **Defer the tool definitions.** ~13k tokens of schema on every round trip for
   a grant of ~60 operations, most unused in any turn.
4. **Reconsider the 1h TTL.** Chosen for sessions paced by a person thinking;
   with a prefix invalidated by the window and by image ageing far more often
   than by an idle gap, the doubled write is paid many more times than the long
   lifetime is used.
5. **Make the window trim and image ageing cache-preserving** (framework). Trim
   at a breakpoint boundary, or in blocks, rather than one exchange per turn.
6. Effort levels and delegation are unchanged and still correct. Delegation is
   the largest single lever — over half — and it stacks with everything above.

worktree (not committed).

## Metrics first: what to measure, before optimising anything

Three passes of the analysis above landed on $252, $55 and $107 for the same
build. The first two were wrong by 4.5× and 2.4×, in opposite directions, and
both errors were about numbers the API already reports and this repository
already throws away. **That error band is wider than the margin of the business**
— at $20/engaged-hour a $50/month plan sells two and a half hours; at $4 it
sells twelve. Nothing downstream of this section is worth doing until the
measurement exists.

Two instruments are needed, and they answer different questions.

### Instrument 1 — per-turn spend, from the API's own counters

**What the API reports.** Four counters per request: `input_tokens`,
`output_tokens`, `cache_read_input_tokens`, `cache_creation_input_tokens`. They
partition the input side, and they are the only way to know what a turn cost,
because a cached token and a fresh one differ by 20× in price and are
indistinguishable from anything this repository currently records.

**What upstream already does.** REQ-143 captures `usage` off `message_start` and
`message_delta`; `turnUsage` folds the turn's requests into `{usage, requests}`;
`turnSpend` puts it on the terminal `done` event; REQ-148 §6 adds `sessionUsage`
and `attributed`, so a delegated worker's spend rolls up into its caller. **All
of it is built. None of it is written down here.**

**Where it hooks.** `streamPrompt` in `tools/generate/src/cli/ai/host-core.ts`
already reads the terminal event — `if (event.kind === DONE) outcome =
turnOutcome(event.meta)` — and already has a `finally` that calls
`closePending(deps, sessionId, outcome)`. The spend record is the same shape of
change in the same two places: fold `turnSpend(event.meta)` beside `outcome`,
and write it in the `finally`.

**Through a port, not a table.** `deps.recordTurnSpend(record)`, following
`LedgerDeps`: the Worker implements it against D1, the `1c` CLI appends to its
own file or declines, and neither learns the other's storage. That is the same
argument `ledger-core.ts` makes for being a third surface.

**What a record holds.** Tenant, session, turn id, start and end, role, model,
outcome, request count, the four raw counters, `attributed` spend from any
delegated worker, and a **cost in micros computed at write time**. Both, not
either: the raw counters so a past period can be re-priced when rates change or
when a model swaps, and the settled cost so a bill does not depend on a price
table that has since moved. The price table is four rates per model — input,
output, cache read, cache write — and it belongs beside `backends.json`, as
this repository's configuration, for the reason BUG-49 gives.

**The first thing it must confirm** is that `cache_read_input_tokens` is
non-zero and grows with the conversation. Every number in the section above
assumes the caching is working; nobody has ever seen it work.

### Instrument 2 — engaged time, the unit the customer can understand

Token cost is not sellable. Consulting hours are. The meter has to be something
a client would accept as fair without understanding any of the above.

**The definition, chosen from the data rather than assumed:** a turn's *engaged
time* is the wall clock from the client's message to the last thing the turn
did, plus the gap until the next turn **capped at five minutes**. The clock
pauses after five minutes of silence and resumes when they come back. This is
how every time tracker works, it is measurable server-side with no assumption
about the client, and it cannot be gamed by typing slowly.

Measured against the candidates on 09-20 — 38 turns, 6.68 h elapsed:

| definition | 09-20 | implied $/hour |
|---|---|---|
| machine time only | 1.58 h | $48 |
| + idle gaps capped at 2 min | 2.67 h | $28 |
| **+ idle gaps capped at 5 min** | **3.69 h** | **$20** |
| + idle gaps capped at 10 min | 4.64 h | $16 |
| raw elapsed wall clock | 6.68 h | $11 |

Five minutes is the honest reading: the median inter-turn gap that evening was
4.2 minutes, so a two-minute cap would stop the clock during ordinary thinking
and undercount real work, while ten minutes bills multitasking as consulting.

### The finding that shapes the product: an hour is not a unit of cost

Cost per engaged hour varied **twenty-fold within a single evening**, and it
climbed monotonically as the session went on:

| clock hour (09-20) | engaged | cost | $/engaged-hour |
|---|---|---|---|
| 12:00 | 0.34 h | $0.71 | **$2** |
| 14:00 | 0.65 h | $8.06 | $12 |
| 15:00 | 0.82 h | $16.80 | $20 |
| **17:00** | 0.66 h | $28.16 | **$42** |

A turn with no tool calls averages $0.57; a turn with fifteen or more averages
$2.77, and the most expensive single turn was $8.90. The driver is context: a
long tool-using turn replays its own accumulated results on every round trip,
and a late turn starts from a fuller window than an early one.

So **hours are the right meter and the wrong cap.** The product should sell and
display hours, and enforce a **dollar ceiling underneath them**, or a client
doing an afternoon of heavy construction exhausts a month's budget in one hour
while another spends the same hour talking and costs nothing. The two numbers
are both in Instrument 1's record, so this is a reporting decision rather than a
second mechanism.

It also means the optimisation work has a second purpose beyond the headline
rate: the levers that cut cost — a smaller window, deferred tool definitions,
delegation — all cut it hardest on exactly the expensive turns, which
**flattens** this curve. A predictable $/hour is worth as much to the pricing
model as a low one.

### What it has to be worth: the viability arithmetic

At a $50/month plan and a target of 20–30% of revenue going to inference:

| | $/engaged-hour | hours at $10/mo COGS | hours at $15/mo COGS |
|---|---|---|---|
| As it runs today | $20 | 0.5 h | 0.75 h |
| Configuration levers only | $11 | 0.9 h | 1.4 h |
| All levers incl. delegation | $3.3 | 3.0 h | 4.5 h |

**"One hour of AI consulting a month" is what today's cost supports, and it is
not a product. "Four hours a month for $50" needs the whole stack**, and is. The
gap between those two sentences is the whole of this epic, and the measurement
is what tells us which one we are actually in.

### Order of work

1. **Instrument 1.** The `done`-event spend record, the port, the D1 table, the
   price table. Confirms or refutes every number above.
2. **Instrument 2.** Engaged time on the same record — it is two timestamps and
   a capped gap, and it costs nothing to add while the record is being designed.
3. **One week of real sessions**, then re-read this section against measurement
   rather than against a model.
4. Only then the levers, in the order the measurement says — the ranking above
   is a prediction, and predictions from this analysis have a poor record.

## The metrics plan — two REQs, and what they are not

### What is actually missing, precisely

Narrower than "no telemetry". Tracing it end to end:

- `ClaudeAPIBackend` captures `usage` per request and puts `{usage, requests}`
  on its terminal event (REQ-143).
- `SessionManager` folds it and **already writes it into the junction log** on
  the `turn_end` record — `record.usage`, `record.requests`, `record.cost_usd` —
  and `manager.usage(sessionId)` reads it back, including a delegated worker's
  `attributed` spend (REQ-148 §6).
- 1stcontact's junction is `memoryJunctions()` (REQ-103's Cloudflare packaging).
  **It dies with the isolate.** The `ArchiveSyncer` persists the transcript to
  the chat ticket and carries no `usage` with it — confirmed: neither the
  `chat_transcript` nor the `tool_transcript` comment on CHAT-3 contains a
  single token count.
- Nothing in this repository calls `manager.usage()`, and `streamPrompt` reads
  the terminal event for `outcome` and discards the rest of `meta`.

So the per-turn numbers exist, in memory, for the life of an isolate, and are
then dropped. **Nothing needs to be built upstream.** This is entirely a
1stcontact gap, and it is a write and a read.

### Provider portability is a design constraint, not a later migration

Open-weights inference is roughly 7× cheaper and not yet at the level this
product needs for judgement — but the gap is closing faster than we will reach
scale, so **nothing built here may assume Anthropic.** Three consequences, all
cheap now and expensive later:

- **The counter vocabulary is already neutral.** `usage.js` uses the Anthropic
  names deliberately — *"the four questions worth asking of any cached
  endpoint"* — and the ChatGPT adapter already reports into them, with
  `cache_creation_input_tokens` pinned at zero because that provider caches
  automatically and never bills a write. So a record holding those four
  counters is portable by construction. Do not add a fifth.
- **The price table is keyed by `(backend, model)`, never by model alone**, and
  it is configuration beside `backends.json` — the seam BUG-49 built.
- **Store raw counters *and* settled cost.** Raw so a period can be re-priced
  against another provider; settled so a bill does not move when the table does.

One honest limit to record now: **token counts are not portable across
tokenizers.** Re-pricing a stored month against an open-weights model gives an
estimate, not an answer. A true comparison needs the same workload replayed
through the other backend. That replay harness is real work and is **explicitly
not** in these two tickets — but the records below are what make it possible, so
they must keep enough per-turn shape to drive one.

### REQ (1) — the turn spend record

**Behaviour.** Every turn that reports spend leaves one durable record, per
tenant, readable after the isolate that produced it is gone.

- `streamPrompt` in `host-core.ts` already reads the terminal event for
  `outcome`; it folds `turnSpend(event.meta)` beside it and writes the record in
  the `finally` that already calls `closePending`.
- The write goes through a **port** — `deps.recordTurnSpend(record)` — following
  `LedgerDeps`: the Worker implements it against D1, the `1c` CLI implements it
  against a file or declines, and neither learns the other's storage.
- The record holds: tenant, session, turn id, `started_at` / `ended_at`, role,
  backend, model, outcome, request count, the four raw counters, `attributed`
  spend from any delegated worker, and **cost in micros settled at write time**
  from the price table.
- A turn that measured nothing writes no record — the framework's own "nothing,
  never zero" rule, because a spend of zero and an unmeasured turn are different
  facts and a meter must not confuse them.

**Acceptance must include the thing nobody has seen.** `cache_read_input_tokens`
non-zero, and growing with the conversation, across two consecutive turns. Every
figure in this epic assumes the caching works; that assumption has never been
tested against the wire.

**Not in scope:** any enforcement, any UI, engaged time.

#### Where the record goes: a new D1 table

**`turn_spend`, in D1, by migration `0019_turn_spend.sql`** — the next number
after `0018_activity_log.sql`.

The handle is not currently reachable from the AI host: `WorkerAiEnv` declares
`SITES: R2Bucket` and the API key and nothing else. So the D1 binding is
**assembled by `router.ts` and passed in as a parameter**, and `null` is an
ordinary state — the same shape `fidelity` and `images` already have on
`workerHost`, and for the same stated reason. A deployment with no meter still
opens the session and still takes the turn.

The write itself lands in a path already held open: `streamTurn` wraps the
turn's `finally` in `ctx.waitUntil` (BUG-46), so the record survives the
response closing without a new lifecycle mechanism and without delaying the last
frame the client sees.

The four alternatives, and why not:

- **`log_records`.** Tempting — it exists, it already has `business`,
  `duration_ms`, `outcome` and a JSON `data` column, and it needs no migration.
  Disqualified: `pruneRecords()` DELETEs it on a band and `log_floor` records
  how far that has reached. **A meter you bill from must not be deletable by an
  ops job.** Nothing has been pruned yet — 6,165 rows, floor at 0 — but the
  mechanism exists and is meant to run.
- **R2, beside the audit** (`spend/<tenant>/<session>/<turn>.json`). Durable,
  and the flush path already exists. Disqualified on the read: a month's report
  becomes a prefix listing and thousands of GETs. The audit gets away with that
  because nothing reads it routinely; a meter is read on every dashboard load
  and every billing run.
- **`counters`** `(tenant_id, type, value)`. An aggregate only — it cannot say
  which turns, cannot split by model or role, and cannot be re-priced, which is
  the whole of the portability argument. It is the right *second* structure: a
  cap check must not scan a month of rows. **Rows for truth, counters for
  speed** — but the rollup arrives with the cap, which is not this ticket.
- **The chat ticket, via the ledger surface.** Wrong on principle, by
  `ledger-core.ts`'s own argument: the ledger is *the engagement's* record and
  spend is not part of the engagement. It would also put the client's bill in a
  document the consultant can read and the knowledge base indexes.

**Retention is keep, not prune.** The raw counters are the asset that answers
the open-weights question a year from now; deleting them forecloses it. Rows
roll into a period aggregate when they age out of usefulness — named here so it
is a decision rather than an omission, and built when there is enough data for
it to matter.


### REQ (2) — reading it back, in hours and in dollars

**Behaviour.** A tenant's spend for a period, in both units, from the records
REQ (1) writes.

- **Engaged time is computed at read, not stored.** A turn's share is its own
  duration plus the gap to the *next* turn capped at five minutes — and the next
  turn does not exist when the first one ends. Storing it would mean a
  retroactive update on every turn; deriving it from two timestamps costs
  nothing and cannot drift.
- Five minutes is chosen from measurement, not taste: the median inter-turn gap
  on 09-20 was 4.2 minutes, so two minutes stops the clock during ordinary
  thinking and ten bills multitasking as consulting.
- The report answers, per tenant per period: engaged hours, settled cost, cost
  per engaged hour, and the same three split by role and by model — the split is
  what makes a delegation experiment readable, and what makes a second provider
  comparable.

**Not in scope:** the cap. Hours are the right meter and the wrong cap — cost per
engaged hour varied twenty-fold in one evening — so enforcement needs a dollar
ceiling underneath the hours, and neither the plan nor the ceiling is decided.
It is a separate ticket once a week of real records exists to set it from.

### Sequence, and what gates what

1. **REQ (1)**, alone, then **one week of ordinary sessions**.
2. Re-read the Measurement section above against records rather than against a
   model. The lever ranking there is a prediction, and the predictions in this
   epic have been wrong by 4.5× and 2.4× in opposite directions.
3. **REQ (2)** can be built in parallel with that week — it only needs the
   record's shape, not its contents.
4. Only then the levers, and only in the order measurement puts them.
