# Why People Pay For Design — Independent-Designer Economics & AI-Website-Builder Pricing Benchmarks

## 1. Executive Summary

People do not pay independent designers for pixels — they pay to offload risk, time, and strategic judgment they don't trust themselves (or a template) to handle well, and they pay a *premium* for direct, undiluted access to the person actually doing the work. Across agency marketing pages, freelancer pricing surveys, and forum-summary sourcing, the same value drivers recur regardless of price point: opportunity cost of the owner's own time, escape from templated "sameness," measurable performance/SEO/conversion outcomes, credibility in trust-sensitive verticals, and — specifically for boutique/independent providers — direct access with no account-manager hand-off.

On the supply side, the market has already converged on **flat/tiered package pricing over hourly billing** (82% of freelance designers price this way), with a strong project-fee center of gravity at **$2,500–$5,000** for a real small-business site, a **sub-$1,000 "template-adjacent" tier**, and **maintenance retainers in the $100–$3,000/month range**. AI website builders (Wix, Squarespace, Framer, Durable, 10Web, Dorik, Hostinger) have separately converged on **tiered flat subscriptions ($10–$40/month) metered by an internal AI-credit allowance**, with free/low-cost tiers functioning as the trial mechanism. **B12 is the one product in the set that already combines a live AI-assisted flow with human consultation and a one-time paid "done-for-you" fee ($1,999)** — the closest existing analog to 1stcontact's model, though priced well above 1stcontact's target band.

1stcontact's planned structure — free live 15-minute consult/demo, $200–$1,000 one-time paid build session, $30–$80/month ongoing — sits in a **genuinely underserved gap**: priced above commodity self-serve AI builders (which top out around $30–40/mo with no live human involvement) but below the independent-designer project-fee sweet spot ($2,500+), justified by AI-compressed delivery cost rather than lower quality. The recommendation in Section 4 is to **tier the $200–$1,000 band explicitly by scope** (mirroring the dominant flat-package pattern), **meter the $30–$80/mo tier by usage/credits** (mirroring the AI-builder convention that protects provider margin), and **use the boutique "direct access, no hand-off" narrative** as the premium-positioning lever rather than competing on price against Wix/Durable. Hitting the specific 90% net-cost-positive target, however, is a unit-economics question this report cannot resolve — see the explicit caveat in Section 4.

## 2. Value-Proposition Thesis: What Is Actually Being Purchased

Synthesizing across the "why hire a designer" sourcing, ten distinct (but overlapping) purchase motives recur. They cluster into four categories:

### A. Buying back the owner's own time and risk
The most consistently cited driver is **opportunity cost**, not sticker price. One analysis quantifies it directly: ~30 hours of DIY effort at $50/hr = $1,500 in owner time, plus ~$540/year in platform fees — "nearly professional pricing without superior results" (Zentus). The recurring framing across sources is a reallocation question — *"is your time best used learning how to build websites, or running your business?"* (Matt Chase Designs) — not a pure cost comparison. This is the strongest and most universal motive, present regardless of industry or business size.

### B. Buying an outcome, not an artifact
Multiple sources are explicit that DIY sites are rarely built with conversion, SEO, or business objectives in mind (Olly Olly), and cite concrete before/after numbers: a custom-built site ranking #1–2 in 9 cities across 2 states and driving 100+ customers from organic search (Hello Web Designs); a boutique case study citing 719 initial customers, 343 repeat buyers, 226.82% ROI, and an 88% lift in organic keywords (Forge and Smith). The purchase, in other words, is framed as a *revenue instrument*, and the decision heuristic several sources converge on independently is the clearest articulation of this: **if the site's job is to exist, a builder is fine; if the site's job is to win customers, a professionally built site "almost always pays for itself."** This heuristic is directly usable in 1stcontact's own positioning language.

### C. Buying differentiation and trust signaling
- **Anti-genericness**: "Wix templates start to look like Wix templates" — a documented sameness problem that undermines brand differentiation (BleylDev, Hello Web Designs). Designers build "around your specific services" rather than fitting the business into a template.
- **Credibility in trust-sensitive verticals** (law, medicine, real estate, luxury retail): an amateurish DIY site is framed as an active liability — "one lost high-value client because of a DIY-looking website costs more than hiring a designer would have" (Zentus).
- **Total cost of ownership**: DIY wins on year-one sticker price but loses by year two or three once premium app add-ons stack up ($500–$1,500 one-time frequently undercuts multi-year DIY subscription costs — Crearewebsolutions).
- **Ownership/portability**: DIY-platform sites are effectively unexportable — "leaving the platform means rebuilding from scratch" (BleylDev) — versus a professionally built asset the client actually owns.

### D. Buying access, attention, and expertise — the boutique-specific layer
This is the category most directly relevant to 1stcontact, because it describes *why a small, independent provider* beats both DIY tools *and* larger agencies:
- **No hand-off**: "the team that pitches your ideas is the team that actually works on your project" (Nora Kramer Designs) — avoiding the account-manager-to-junior-designer pipeline of bigger firms.
- **Selective attention**: boutiques take fewer simultaneous projects so work doesn't "get lost in the shuffle."
- **Strategic/marketing expertise beyond visual production**: designers "know all about digital marketing" and can validate whether design choices actually serve business goals — something a site builder categorically cannot assess (Simply Built).
- **Ongoing accountability**: "with a DIY builder, you're on your own... if something breaks, it's your problem" (Olly Olly) — the value of a human who remains reachable after launch.

**Synthesis for 1stcontact**: 1stcontact's proposed format — a live, conversational, story-first discovery call that produces a working site *in front of the client* — is structurally positioned to deliver categories B, C, and D simultaneously in a single session, which none of the AI-builder competitors do (they are all fully self-service) and which a typical freelancer cannot do at this price point (freelance delivery is asynchronous, over days/weeks). The live, single-session, no-hand-off format *is* the boutique "direct access" value driver (D), made possible at AI-compressed cost, applied to real-time SEO/conversion-literate site generation (B) that isn't visibly templated (C). That combination — not the AI itself — is the actual product being sold, and should anchor the pricing narrative rather than "AI website builder," a category people already associate with $0–$40/month DIY tools.

**Cross-cutting patterns:**
1. Tiered flat subscription is the near-universal AI-builder structure; none use hourly/consultant billing.
2. AI usage itself is usually metered as a secondary axis (credits) *inside* a flat tier — this is the mechanism the market uses to cap the provider's variable cost exposure while still offering unlimited-feeling access.
3. Free tier vs. time-boxed trial split roughly evenly; a permanent free/branded tier is the more common trial substitute.
4. **B12 is the only product offering anything resembling 1stcontact's live, human-paired AI flow** — and it prices that add-on at $1,999, roughly 2–10x above 1stcontact's planned $200–$1,000 band, which confirms 1stcontact is deliberately positioning below the one directly comparable existing benchmark, not inventing an unprecedented price point.

## 4. Recommended Pricing Framework for 1stcontact

### 4.1 Where 1stcontact sits on the map

Plotting 1stcontact's three components against the benchmarks:

| Component | 1stcontact's plan | Nearest benchmark(s) | Positioning read |
|---|---|---|---|
| Free 15-min consult | Free, dual-purpose (value + pitch) | Majority (~85%) of designers use free discovery calls; Wix/Durable/Framer free tiers as trial mechanism | Consistent with dominant market norm on both sides — not a differentiator by itself, but table stakes done well |
| $200–$1,000 one-time build session | Flat, live, session-based | Below designer sweet spot ($2,500–$5,000); above template-tier freelance work ($490–$1,000); well below B12's $1,999 done-for-you add-on | Deliberately positioned as **premium-to-DIY, budget-to-agency** — justified by AI-compressed delivery cost, not lower quality |
| $30–$80/mo ongoing | Flat monthly | Squarely inside AI-builder subscription range (Wix $29, Squarespace $29, Framer $30, Durable $22–$41, B12 $49) | Priced like a **SaaS subscription**, well *below* even bare-bones human maintenance retainers ($100–$500/mo) |

This is a coherent position, but it has one structural implication that must be made explicit: **the $30–$80/mo tier is priced at AI-builder-subscription levels, not at agency-retainer levels — so it cannot carry material human time without breaking margin.** More on this below.

### 4.2 Structural recommendations

**1. Keep the free consult free, but treat it as underwriting the paid conversion — not as a standalone cost center.**
The data supports free discovery as the market norm; charging for it would suppress top-of-funnel volume and conflicts with the "give real value + demo the live-build experience" dual purpose that is 1stcontact's actual differentiator against pure self-serve AI builders (none of which offer a live human-paired demo at all). The cost of the free call should be modeled as a **customer-acquisition cost amortized across paid conversions**, not as a line item expected to be cost-positive on its own.

**2. Sub-tier the $200–$1,000 paid session by explicit scope, not by time spent.**
The dominant pattern among independent designers is flat/package pricing tied to scope (82% of freelancers), not hourly billing — and 1stcontact's live-session format makes hourly billing especially awkward to justify to a consumer audience. Recommend three explicit scope bands within the range, mirroring the WebFX/small-agency tiering pattern:
- **~$200–$350** — single-page/landing-page scope (mirrors the low end of the "template-adjacent" freelance band, $490–$1,000, but priced lower because AI compresses build time).
- **~$400–$650** — small multi-page business site (the segment where independent designers' $750–$1,000 low tier and 1stcontact's offer directly compete on outcome, not price).
- **~$700–$1,000** — added complexity (booking, e-commerce basics, integrations) — still well under the $2,500+ where independent-designer custom work begins.

Scoping this at intake during the free call (rather than after the paid session starts) is the single highest-leverage lever for protecting margin on the lowest tier: the paid-discovery data point (designers who charge for discovery land 2x more $5,000+ deals) suggests structured, disciplined scoping conversations correlate with better-matched, more profitable engagements — 1stcontact can capture that discipline without charging for the call, simply by using it rigorously as a scoping/qualification instrument.

**3. Meter the $30–$80/mo tier by usage/credits, not "unlimited updates."**
Every AI-builder competitor in this price band (Squarespace, Framer, 10Web, Hostinger, B12) protects margin at a flat subscription price by metering the AI-driven component with a monthly credit allowance, rather than promising unlimited AI usage or unlimited human-assisted edits. This is the single clearest convergent structural lesson from the benchmarking: **flat price + metered usage is how this exact market segment already solves the "stay net-positive per customer" problem 1stcontact is asking about.** Recommend:
- $30/mo tier: a capped number of AI-assisted edit/update credits per month, self-serve.
- $80/mo tier: a larger credit allowance and/or a bounded amount of human-reviewed changes (e.g., a capped number of "designer-reviewed" edits per quarter, not unlimited).
- No tier should include open-ended human time; any human-touch component must be capped and costed the same way a maintenance retainer would be (Jim.com's bare-bones benchmark, $100–$500/mo, is a useful sanity check for what unmetered human involvement actually costs to deliver — 1stcontact's $30–$80/mo band is priced well under that, which only works if human time in this tier is minimal-to-none).

**4. Use the "no hand-off, direct access" boutique narrative as the pricing justification, not "AI-built site."**
Leading with "AI website builder" invites direct price comparison to Wix/Durable/Squarespace, where 1stcontact's $200–$1,000 one-time fee will look expensive relative to their $0–$40/mo entry points. Leading with the boutique-designer value cluster (Section 2D) — live, one-session, no-hand-off, story-first discovery with the person who actually builds it — positions the comparison instead against the $2,500–$5,000 independent-designer sweet spot, where 1stcontact's offer reads as a *steep discount for comparable personal attention*, not an expensive AI tool. This reframing is what allows 1stcontact to avoid "racing to the cheapest possible price": the anchor category matters as much as the number.

### 4.3 Explicit caveat on the 90% net-cost-positive target

Everything above addresses **market positioning and structure** — where 1stcontact's price points sit relative to comparable designer and AI-builder offers, and what structural mechanisms (scope-tiering, usage-metering, capped human time) the market has already converged on to protect margin at a given price point.

**It does not, and cannot, tell 1stcontact whether $200 (or $350, or $1,000) actually clears their own cost floor in 90% of sessions.** That requires an internal unit-economics model this report has no visibility into, specifically:
- Per-session compute/token cost of the live conversational build (variance by conversation length, model calls, revision cycles).
- Any human oversight/QA time per session and its fully-loaded cost.
- Infrastructure and hosting cost amortized per paid customer and per free-tier customer.
- Free-to-paid conversion rate (since the free call's cost must be amortized across paid conversions, not treated as free).
- Distribution of actual client requests across the proposed scope tiers (a $200 tier only stays cost-positive if scope creep into $1,000-tier work is actively prevented at intake, per Recommendation 2).
- Marginal cost of servicing the $30–$80/mo tier over a multi-month customer lifetime, including support load and credit redemption rates.

1stcontact should build that internal model (ideally session-level, not averaged) before finalizing exact price points inside the recommended bands, and should treat the tiering/metering structure above as the mechanism for keeping that model's assumptions defensible — not as a substitute for building it.
