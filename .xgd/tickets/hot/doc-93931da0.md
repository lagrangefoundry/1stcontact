**Status: design. Not scheduled, no construction tickets cut.** Written
2026-09-22 from the EPIC-20 discussion. Captured now because the reasoning is
fresh and the build is not next.

## The question this answers

EPIC-20 keeps arriving at a decision it cannot currently state: **is a cheaper
model good enough to put in front of a paying customer?** REQ-292/293 settled
the cost half — a run's dollars, tokens and engaged hours are now exact. The
quality half has no instrument at all, so "Fable at 40% of the cost" is a
sentence we can write the second clause of and not the first.

This is that instrument. It is not a statistical test and does not try to be. It
is a small number of **fixed client sessions**, run against each candidate model,
read by two people. The claim is narrow and sufficient: a model that cannot hold
a design conversation will fail these visibly, and open-weight candidates will
betray themselves within three turns.

## Rejected: an AI interrogator

The first proposal was a model playing the client — an adaptive interrogator that
reacts to what the builder says. **Rejected, and the reason generalises.**

An interrogator must *decide what to say next*, and that decision is the thing
under test. A weak interrogator asks weak follow-ups, never presses a thin
recommendation, and accepts a poor hero section — so a strong model is never
stressed and scores well for it. Worse, a cheap interrogator fails in the same
directions as the cheap candidates, so it flatters precisely the models this
exists to eliminate. What gets measured is how well the interrogator drives, not
how well the builder builds.

Nothing about script design was the problem. A fixed script is the correct
instrument; the earlier objection to scripts was really an objection to *badly
written* scripts.

## The script

Ordered, human-authored client turns. Identical on every run against every model.
The conversation asks for **recommendations** and accepts them:

1. Assets uploaded — the business, its people, its van, its logo, its USP, its
   services, its pricing.
2. *"Please read the attached assets and suggest appropriate messaging for this
   business. Give me a strong recommendation."*
3. *"That's great, let's go with your recommendation on messaging. Now what do
   you suggest for the hero section? Give me a strong recommendation."*
4. *"I love that, please create it. Now what would you put in the next section?"*

— a few rounds of this, then one or two open questions on styling, fonts and
layout, agreed to and implemented.

### "Give me a strong recommendation" is load-bearing

It is not filler and must appear in every script, because it is what makes a
fixed script *fair*.

Consider a hedging model. It replies *"great — who's your target customer? what's
your budget?"*, and the script, which cannot see that, says **"I love that,
please create it."** The model must now build from nothing, the transcript reads
as incoherent, and that is the **correct** score — it was told to recommend and
it didn't. Without the instruction the same trap would unfairly punish a
reasonable clarifying question. With it, hedging is disobedience and the script
punishes it with no grader involved.

The same applies to a bad idea: the script agrees, the site comes out bad, and
the bad site is the signal. **A run that goes incoherent is a result, not a
broken run.**

### Three scripts, differing deliberately

- **One with scripted pushback.** Every agreeable script measures only *can the
  model generate and execute good ideas unprompted*. Real clients disagree
  constantly, and taking correction is a separate capability. Scripted, not
  adaptive — at a fixed turn: *"Actually no — my customers are mostly over 60 and
  that reads far too young. Try again."* Determinism intact. Models that re-skin
  the same idea and call it new show up immediately.
- **One with text-only assets.** Photographs make a script a vision test as much
  as a judgment test, and candidates vary from decent to none there. One
  text-only fixture (written USP, service list, pricing) means that when a model
  fails, we can tell *which* thing failed.
- **One full-fat**, photographs and all — the real product.

**2-3 runs per (script, model)**, to separate "this model is bad" from "that run
was unlucky."

## Fixtures: harvested, then de-identified

Invented businesses are too generic to discriminate — a weak model succeeds on
platitudes when the brief is vague. So fixtures are **captured from real business
websites and then made unrecognisable.**

What is harvested is **structure and specificity**: that a plumber has four
service tiers and an awkward call-out fee, that the USP is "we clean up after
ourselves," that the pricing page carries a strange exception. That texture is
what invented briefs never have, and it is nobody's property.

**The rule: facts transplanted, identity regenerated.** Text details are
perturbed. Name, faces, logo and town are replaced outright — not edited. You
cannot anonymise a photograph of a real person's face by renaming the business,
and these artefacts are destined to be shown to prospects (below), which makes a
real tradesperson's team photo on our marketing site a different category of
problem from a benchmark artefact.

### The photo pipeline

Describe, then regenerate. The real photograph goes through the Awareness
`describe` seam; the resulting description drives the `ai-imagegen` plugin
([[REQ-208]], plain `fetch`, Worker-safe). Both halves already exist in-repo —
this is wiring, not new capability.

**The description is the anonymiser, and its lossiness is the guarantee.** Text
cannot encode a likeness. What survives is composition, framing and mood — "a man
in his fifties in a polo shirt beside a white transit" — and nothing traceable to
a person. That is a stronger property than editing a photograph.

**Text-in-image is regenerated, not redacted.** Van livery, signage and name
badges can carry a real phone number or business name through the description. The
generated image carries the **new fictional identity** instead, which yields a
consistent fictional world rather than a blanked-out one.

## Where it runs: inside the product, one tenant per test

**Not an external harness.** A runner driving the HTTP API from outside tests the
API and steps around most of the pipeline. The scripted turns are inserted at the
**back end**, into a real session, so the run is indistinguishable from a
customer's — the draft site appearing in the preview pane beside the transcript,
exactly as a client would see it.

**A tenant per test.** `POST /api/admin/businesses`. This is cleaner than it
first appears: `tenant_id` is already the scope column on `turn_spend`, so a
tenant per run makes that run's cost, tokens and engaged hours a single scoped
query. **No run tagging is needed** — an earlier proposal to stamp sessions with a
`script_id`/`run_id` is obsoleted by this and should not be built. It also
retires the question of how to snapshot the finished site: the site is the
tenant's own state and persists.

**Live observation is free.** `/api/ai/reattach` ([[BUG-46]]) takes the cursor
`/api/ai/session` hands out and resumes the tail at the fold offset. Its own note
records the property that matters here — the turn "is driven by whoever started
it and is entirely unaffected either way." So a browser opened on the test
tenant's chat joins a turn the runner started, paints the site as it is built,
and does not perturb the run. No new code.

### What a run yields

A tenant holding: the uploaded material, the full transcript
(`GET /api/chats/export`), the finished site at its own revision, and the exact
cost (`GET /api/admin/spend?business=...`, principal and delegated split per
REQ-292). Screenshots for later side-by-side comparison are a convenience, not an
architectural requirement.

## Second use: example sites

These runs also produce the example sites the product needs, which turns a cost
we were going to pay into something we wanted anyway. The **Opus runs** are the
source for that, being the ones worth showing.

**A benchmark run is never edited.** This rule exists because the second use pulls
against the first: once a run is destined for marketing we will want to fix the
weak headline and re-prompt the thin section, and the moment we do, the transcript
no longer measures what the model did unaided. Score the run as it came out. To
ship one as an example, **fork it after scoring and polish the fork.** Costs
nothing; protects both uses.

## Scoring is deliberately undesigned

Two people read the transcript and look at the site, and score it. Not a rubric
in code, no schema, no UI. The axes we will be asking about are known — quality
of the ideas, quality of the site, quality of the conversation — and whether they
deserve formalising is a decision to make *after* watching three runs, not
before. If models do badly here it will be obvious.

The one thing worth noting in advance: unstated criteria drift silently, and the
purpose is comparison across months. So whatever we settle on after the first
runs should be written down here then.

## Open questions

1. **How does the runner authenticate as the test tenant?** `/api/ai/prompt`
   resolves through `requireScope()` on the request. If an existing operator or
   service path resolves scope for a named business, this whole thing is glue. If
   not, that is a small new surface — and it is the only identified unknown that
   changes the size of the job. **Check this first.**
2. **Who authors the fixtures?** Generated from captured sites and then edited by
   hand, most likely. They must stay specific; a vague brief cannot discriminate.
3. **How many candidates in the first pass?** Opus 5 is the incumbent. Fable 5
   and Sonnet 5 are the immediate comparisons; open-weight candidates are the
   actual question behind the epic.
