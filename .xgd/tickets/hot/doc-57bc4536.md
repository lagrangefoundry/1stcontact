# Briefing a delegated builder — what works and what breaks

You can hand scoped construction work to a cheaper **worker** session through the
delegation surface. That surface tells you *how to call it*. This document is about
*how to write a brief that survives contact with the worker* — and what to do with
the result when it comes back.

Everything here was learned from observed runs, and every failure mode below cost a
real turn to discover. None of them are guessable from the tool description. The
rules come first; the runs they were drawn from are logged at the end.

**This is a living document.** New observations are added as dated entries in the
run log, each describing a concrete observed run rather than a generalisation. When
the log contradicts a rule, the rule is revised in place and the revision says so.

## The shape to aim for

The briefs that landed cleanly all had the same shape:

- **one phase** of work — nothing that depends on the result of an earlier step;
- **the exact values to write**, computed by you, with the words *do not recompute these*;
- **the relevant schema lines quoted** wherever the worker may not know a field exists;
- **one check, one clause, one comparison** — and only for something the host's record cannot show;
- afterwards, **the host's change record (`account`) read as the source of truth**, not the worker's summary.

The sections below explain each one.

## Diagnosis and arithmetic stay with you. The worker transcribes.

The expensive part of a fix is almost never the writing. On one observed run it was
reading a page and noticing that two elements did not declare a property that every
sibling in their section declared. That noticing cannot be delegated. Once it is done,
the remaining work is writing eight pairs of numbers — which a cheap worker does
*excellently*.

So `goal_text` should carry **the exact values to write**, plus *do not recompute these*.
A brief that states a constraint ("bring it inside the column") instead of a computed
value invites the worker to do arithmetic, and a worker doing arithmetic against a
boundary it picked itself will pick the wrong boundary.

## One phase per delegation — "then" means two delegations

A brief carrying several jobs does not fail proportionally; it fails worse. A worker
that stops halfway leaves the site in a state that neither the brief nor the report
describes.

"One job" turned out to be too loose. A single nominal job can still have two phases:
add a new element, *then* modify existing elements whose addresses only resolve after
the addition renumbers the page. If a brief has to tell the worker to re-read the page
mid-way, it is two briefs wearing one coat.

> **If the brief contains the word "then", it is probably two delegations.**

Anything whose second half depends on the first half's addresses, values or
preconditions should be split, with you re-reading the page map in between. Re-reading
a map is cheap. A null run is not.

## Forbid destructive methods, not just unwanted outcomes

A brief said, in effect, *set this one flag on this component, change nothing else*.
The worker achieved it by **removing the component and adding it back** — technically
"changing nothing else about the component" — and silently destroyed all the page-side
styling of the component's slot contents, applied by an earlier run.

The brief was right about the outcome and silent about the method. Say both:

> Change these in place. Do not remove and re-add any component, do not delete and
> recreate any element, and do not replace a parent in order to change a child.

## Anything you do not name will not be touched

A brief gave exact widths for four boxes. The worker wrote exactly those widths and
nothing else; the children inside the boxes kept their old widths and overflowed. That
is not carelessness — it is the worker doing precisely what it was told. It is a virtue,
and it means the brief must **enumerate the consequences you would have handled
instinctively**: when you narrow a container, say what happens to its children.

## Quote the schema when the worker might not know a capability exists

A worker was asked to stack a container's two children at narrow widths and lay them
side by side above. It did everything else correctly, then recorded as a *decision*:
"containers do not support per-width layout specifications". That is false — the
`container` element declares `responsiveLayout` ("per-width layout track; the track owns
the mode at render time"). A follow-up brief that quoted those schema lines and named
the field got it written first time, for about forty extra words.

Quoting the relevant schema lines up front is cheaper than the round trip, and it
removes the worker's opportunity to reason its way to a wrong conclusion about what is
possible.

## Checks: decidable, single, and only what the record cannot show

**Decidable, never impressions.** A check phrased as an observation is something a
worker can sincerely believe it verified and never be caught being wrong about. A check
phrased as a comparison with both operands named, or as an existence claim, cannot be
fudged.

- Bad: "confirm the forms sit inside the section and do not overlap the content above."
- Good: "confirm both form slots carry a viewport-height response with factor 1."
- Good: "confirm field A and field B are marked required."

**One clause per check, and few checks.** Checks are paid for from the same budget as
the work. Runs given one single-clause check completed; runs given one check with five
sub-clauses came back `unreported`, including one that had otherwise done its work
correctly. Verifying five properties across eight elements means re-reading eight
elements — read-heavy work that starves the edit. (`check_list` now refuses an entry
holding several checks, but a list of five single checks has the same cost.) If several
things need verifying, pick the one hardest to get right by accident and verify the rest
yourself from the change record.

**Never ask the worker to confirm what the record already shows.** Asking a worker to
confirm it wrote the value it wrote is asking the least reliable party to check the most
reliable record. Reserve checks for what the record cannot show: resolved behaviour,
rendered state, or whether something the worker *didn't* touch still holds.

## Read the change record, not the worker's report

The delegation result carries `account`: the host's own record of what changed,
derived by comparing before and after. It does not pass through the worker at all.

In the observed runs, most produced no usable self-report — a false pass, an empty
summary, a context-window death, a silent zero-write run. In every case the change record
was complete and correct.

**Treat `account` as the source of truth and the worker's `summary` as commentary.** It
is also the cheap path: reading the record costs a fraction of re-reading the pages, and
it is most valuable exactly when the worker's answer is thin.

## A claim that something *cannot* be done is a finding to verify

This is the most dangerous failure mode observed, and the only one no brief can fully
prevent. A worker reporting *"I did it"* can be checked against the change record for
almost nothing. A worker reporting *"it cannot be done"* has produced no change to
check, so the cheap verification path does not exist.

It arrives as a confident, well-reasoned engineering judgement, in the `decisions` field
where you are most inclined to trust it. Accepted, it ships the wrong design — and
worse, it corrupts the record: the next session reads the false limitation in the ledger
and designs around a constraint that does not exist.

> A worker's claim that the system cannot do something is a finding to verify against
> the schema, never a fact to accept — and never let it into the ledger unchecked.

## The failure taxonomy

Five distinct ways not to get what you asked for:

1. **Wrong and confident** — work done incorrectly, reported as passed. Fixed by
   supplying values and decidable checks; caught by checking the change record's numbers.
2. **Partial and silent** — part of the brief done, nothing said about which part.
   Visible only in the change record.
3. **Destructive method, obedient outcome** — the letter of the brief honoured by a means
   that destroyed adjacent work. Fixed by forbidding the method.
4. **False impossibility** — an existing capability declared absent, persuasively. No
   cheap detection; verify against the schema, and quote the schema in the brief.
5. **Null** — full budget spent, nothing written, nothing said. Visible only in the
   change record; reduced (not eliminated) by one-phase briefs.

Good briefing reduces *wrong* outcomes. It does not eliminate *null* ones.

## When delegation pays, honestly

The saving is real and conditional. The clean runs did correct work for a small fraction
(one: about 5%) of what doing it directly would have cost. But your own overhead is not
zero and should be budgeted:

- computing the values — unavoidable, this is the part that cannot be delegated;
- reading the change record afterwards — cheap, and non-negotiable;
- re-briefing after a null or partial run — roughly one run in three so far.

That favours delegation for anything with **a large payload and small decisions**. It
does not favour delegating a two-line edit you could make directly: at that size the
round trip and the re-brief dominate, and the work should stay with you.

## Run log

One role, one site, one session (observed 2026-09-28 to 2026-09-29; source REQ-348).
Add new entries at the bottom, dated, each describing a concrete run.

- **Run 1 — `exhausted`, `accepted: false`, a check reported `passed` that was false.**
  The brief stated a constraint rather than a computed value and left one method choice
  open. The worker chose badly, validated against a boundary that applied at a different
  viewport width than the boxes it checked, left every box ~112px outside the region, and
  reported the visual check as passed (BUG-165).
- **Run 2 — clean, first time, ~5% of run 1's token spend.** Same underlying task. Eight
  exact coordinate pairs, "do not recompute", one narrow existence check.
- **Run 3 — `silent`: empty summary, no verdict.** Four jobs in one brief. The change
  record shows about two done — a styling pass and an element removal — then it stopped
  without a word.
- **Run 4 — `failed`: the worker's context exceeded (205,617 tokens against 200,000).**
  Two small jobs, both achieved — by removing and re-adding both components, destroying
  the styling run 3 had applied. Two flags set, one page regressed.
- **Run 6 — false capability denial.** Everything else correct; recorded that containers
  cannot lay out per width. False (`responsiveLayout`). A follow-up quoting the schema
  lines landed first time and passed its check.
- **Run 7 — `silent`, zero writes after 45,872 output tokens.** The best-specified brief
  of the session: exact values, destructive methods forbidden, one nominal job — but two
  phases (add an element, then modify six elements whose addresses the addition
  renumbered). Source of the one-phase rule.

Across these runs, the cleanest were all single-phase briefs carrying exact values and a
single-clause check. That correlation is strong enough to treat as the rule.
