## What the client asked for

> "Part of our contract to the user has to be that their site is guaranteed to be
> working. That means not just that the webpages are accessible, but that the
> email is delivering and receiving and the web forms are working, including
> payments. To this end one of our important requirements is basic flow
> monitoring (BFM). This epic is related to [[EPIC-8]] — [[EPIC-8]] is the front
> end and visibility to the user, it will contain things like Google analytics and
> possibly SEO/SEM statistics. This ticket is the technical implementation of the
> site functional tests that will be running continuously.
>
> One of the core goals here is to design test guttering into the product from the
> outset (now) so that testing doesn't become an add-on later. Examples of
> guttering are that we want to test the whitepaper sign-up flow. However, we want
> to do it in a way that the system recognises the traffic as test traffic and does
> not add it to the Contacts page, list it in the matrix etc.
>
> BFM needs to be thought of as:
> - platform tests — we can have a small set of example sites that demonstrate all
>   the features of the platform
> - per business tests — fewer, less frequent
> - continuous monitoring and to be run after a deployment
>
> In that chat session I did talk about tracing email all the way to a third-party
> inbox, but that might be overkill — we will have fairly sophisticated email
> routing capabilities, it may be enough to send the email out into the world and
> have it come back into our system.
>
> For these tests we will clearly need a cloud setup — maybe it should be, or some
> of it should be, outside of Cloudflare — for obvious outage reasons."

Designed against [[CHAT-54]] ("Email receipt"), whose BFM half is the source for
several decisions recorded below.

## The principle

**A flow is proved by manufacturing traffic that travels the whole of it, and
the traffic is recognisable as ours everywhere it lands.**

Both halves are load-bearing, and the second is the one that has to exist first.

The first half is what separates this from every check the platform has today.
Reachability is an *observation* — ask the origin a question, read the answer,
nothing changed. A flow is a *transaction*: a form is genuinely submitted, an
email is genuinely sent, a Checkout session is genuinely created. The only test
that can tell you a form works is one that fills it in. That is also the only
kind of test that can lie to the customer's own record, which is why the second
half is not an implementation detail.

The second half is the client's guttering, and the reason it is urgent now rather
than later: **a marker retrofitted onto records that already exist is a marker
with a hole in it.** Every read written before the flag existed reads unfiltered
and nobody notices, because a bot lead looks exactly like a lead.

## The honest version of the promise, which is still a strong one

A note on the framing, and then it is not relitigated: we cannot guarantee a
customer's site is working. Cloudflare can have an outage, a registrar can fumble
a renewal, Gmail can start junking a domain overnight. What is defensible —
and what [[DOC-46]] §5's "what you must not claim" requires we say instead — is:

> **If your site stops working, we know before you do, and we can show you the
> evidence either way.**

That is a real differentiator against every competitor in this market, it is
checkable, and it survives contact with an outage. It is also the shape this
project already has an opinion about: a claim of correctness is only worth what
its evidence is worth. Probe results are that evidence, which makes retention and
presentation of probe history part of the product rather than an operational log.

The distinction has a concrete consequence for [[EPIC-8]]: what a customer sees is
not a green light, it is a green light *with a timestamp and a history behind it*.

## What exists today

Close to nothing, and one thing better than expected.

- **`bin/smoke` (`tools/generate/bin/smoke.mjs`, [[REQ-144]]/[[BUG-57]]) is the
  real precursor.** Post-deploy assertions against a **live origin** — apex
  resolves, the trailing-slash 301 holds, every referenced asset 200s with the
  right content type, cache policy is the published policy, an unknown site key
  404s indistinguishably, the control app is private to an unauthenticated
  caller. It is exactly the right idea, executed once, invoked by hand: `bin/deploy`
  ends by printing *"Now prove it serves: bin/smoke"*. It proves bytes serve. It
  proves nothing transacts.
- **`resolver.ts` ([[REQ-257]]/[[EPIC-5]]) reads live DNS from outside**, following
  the domain's current delegation rather than reading our own zone back. MX, SPF
  includes, DKIM selectors probed by name. This is a checker already, and the
  per-business DNS half of BFM largely consumes it rather than rebuilding it.
- **One cron exists**, `17 4 * * *` on the control app, and it purges sessions
  (`index.ts`'s `scheduled`). There is no scheduler, no verdict store, no
  notification path, no probe.
- **No `synthetic` marker exists anywhere in the product data model.** Not on
  `users`, not on `contact_events`, not on message tickets. This is the gap the
  client is naming, and it is the one that gets more expensive every week.
- **Nothing runs outside Cloudflare.** Every scheduled thing we have shares fate
  with everything it would be watching.

And the motivating anecdote is our own: [[EPIC-7]] cites [[TODO-5]] — on our *own*
domain, two separate mail failures were live and nothing anywhere reported them.
That is the failure BFM exists to make impossible, observed on the one domain we
were best placed to notice it on.

## Scope

**The standing rule the rest of this section serves: the product is built for
testability out of the gate.** Not "testable once someone adds tests" — every surface
ships with the affordances a test needs, at the time the surface is built. The client's
words: *"the urgency is to build it and insert it as a requirement before it becomes
an afterthought."*

Two consequences that read as constraints on other epics rather than as work here, and
both are cheaper now than at any later date. A new write path arrives markable. A new
read path arrives filtered. Neither is a retrofit if neither is deferred.

### 1. The gutter — a constraint on the whole product, and the part that is urgent

This is not a section of BFM. It is a contract BFM defines and **other epics
honour**, and it has to be settled before those epics build the surfaces that
would have to be retrofitted. [[EPIC-13]] §5 already carries an early form of it;
this epic is where it becomes normative.

**The client's specification, and it is the normative form of this section.** Four
mechanisms, each standard and each used by every surface rather than reinvented per
feature:

> 1. A standard mechanism to **mark test traffic as test, in flight**.
> 2. A standard mechanism to **mark the data that traffic produces as test**, and code
>    that adds that mark when writing the row, ticket or blob.
> 3. Standard rules that **exclude test data from all standard production queries**.
> 4. **Garbage collection** that cleans test data up once the test is complete and its
>    results are checked — perhaps at the same time: check the data and delete it.

The rest of this section is what each of the four requires in this codebase. The
shape to copy for all four is `tickets.ts`'s `forTenant`, which stamps
`WHERE tenant_id = ?` on every read and write and returns a handle that is
*terminal* — no call site is given the chance to forget. Every one of these four
fails in the same way if it is a convention rather than a mechanism: thirty call
sites, twenty-nine of which remember.

#### (1) Marking traffic in flight

- **A signed, unforgeable marker.** HMAC with a platform secret, time-bound,
  verified server-side, never trusted from a payload alone. The attack this closes
  is the important direction: a caller who could mark *real* traffic synthetic could
  make a competitor's leads silently vanish from their own dashboard. It is now also
  load-bearing for money, since it is the only permitted input to the payments
  credential selection discussed below.
- **It rides the request, not the route.** Any entry point can carry it — a form
  post, an inbound email, a webhook — because a separate test route would be a
  second code path and would test itself rather than the product.
- **It carries a run id — `newId('run')`, the existing opaque minter ([[REQ-190]]),
  never a second shorter format. This is a finding rather than a refinement.** Mechanism (4) says *check the data and delete it*, which is impossible
  unless the probe can find the rows it produced. It cannot: the public capture
  endpoint answers with *"the one frozen acknowledgement whatever it says"*
  ([[REQ-223]] §2), deliberately, so no identifier crosses back. Every record a run
  writes therefore carries that run's id, and verification and collection are both
  *"the rows stamped `run`"*. A boolean alone makes collection a TTL sweep and makes
  verification a guess.
- **The reserved address can be the run id's carrier for email-keyed flows** —
  `bfm+<run>@<reserved domain>` — so the capture path needs no extra wire field, while
  flows that mint no contact carry it explicitly. Worth settling in the design doc;
  the requirement is the run id, not its transport.
- **It lands in request context, from which everything downstream derives.** Each
  handler asking "am I a test?" and remembering to pass a boolean onward is the
  convention-not-mechanism failure in its most likely form.
- **The probe runs the full real path.** Short-circuiting on the marker would stop
  testing the thing the test exists to test. The marker changes what is *written*
  and what is *shown*, never what is *executed*.
- **Reserved identity namespace.** A reserved domain for probe addresses and a
  reserved contact shape, so a real customer cannot collide with one by accident and
  a probe cannot be mistaken for a person.
- **The marker stays a boolean. An initiator class was proposed and withdrawn** —
  see the decision below; the case that seemed to require it does not.

#### (2) Marking the data it produces

- **`synthetic` is a column**, on every record a probe can create — contacts,
  `contact_events`, message tickets, and whatever payments and bookings add. Not a
  field inside a JSON bag: D1 cannot index into one, and this predicate is on every
  read.
- **The write layer stamps it from context; call sites never supply it.** Exactly as
  `contact_events.business_id` is *"derived from the contact and never supplied"* —
  every insert is `INSERT ... SELECT`, so an event cannot be filed under the wrong
  business. Same mechanism, same reason: a flag a caller passes is a flag a caller
  omits.
- **Asynchronous continuations have no request context, so the flag propagates by
  derivation.** This is the non-obvious half and the place a naive implementation
  leaks. A delivery webhook arriving thirty seconds later is a different request
  carrying no marker; it knows it is synthetic because the message it attaches to is.
  The rule: *in-flight marker where there is one, parent's flag where there is not,
  and never a default of false.*
- **Blobs have no `WHERE` clause**, so the mark has to be in the R2 key — a reserved
  prefix rather than object metadata. A prefix makes the exclusion visible in the
  key, makes the GC sweep a prefix listing, and makes an accidental exposure
  greppable.

#### (3) Excluding it from production queries

- **Default-deny at the read layer, not opt-in at the call site.** The ordinary
  handle excludes synthetic rows structurally. Seeing them requires asking for a
  different, explicitly-named handle.
- **Every aggregate, count, export, metric and AI context is filtered**, not just the
  list views. A bot that opens and clicks 100% of thirty messages a month will wreck
  a reported open rate, and the number is wrong in the flattering direction.
- **Customer notifications are suppressed**, in one place — [[EPIC-14]]'s audience
  resolver — because the declaration carries the flag and the resolver drops it. A
  daily bot enquiry that emails Alice every morning is worse than no monitoring.
- **The escape hatch has exactly one legitimate caller: the verifier in (4).** That
  is a useful property rather than a footnote — it means any other consumer of the
  synthetic-visible handle is a bug detectable by inspection, which is the strongest
  guarantee available for a rule that is otherwise enforced by discipline.

#### (4) Garbage collection

**The client's refinement is the better primitive and it replaces the TTL-first
design this epic previously carried: verification and collection are the same step.**
The probe's assertion is a read of the rows it produced; the delete follows
immediately on success. Steady-state synthetic volume becomes *the number of probes
in flight* rather than *days of accumulated debris*, which is a different order of
magnitude and removes the bloat question rather than managing it.

It does not remove the sweeper, and the two cases where it does not are the ones to
design for:

- **On failure, retain deliberately.** The partial record is the diagnostic — how far
  the flow got before it stopped is most of what makes *"your contact form is broken"*
  actionable. So: **delete on pass, retain on fail**, and failure debris is what the
  TTL then bounds.
- **On no verdict at all, the sweeper is the only thing left.** A probe whose runner
  died, or whose email never arrived, wrote rows that nobody will ever read or
  delete. Orphans are the TTL's actual job, and it is a backstop rather than the
  mechanism.

##### The periodic sweep, and why it must be ignorant of run ids

Collection-on-verify is bookkeeping, and **all bookkeeping eventually has a hole in
it**. So there is a second, dumber pass whose entire value is that it depends on none
of the first one's state:

- **It keys on the row, never on a run registry.** `synthetic = 1 AND created_at <
  horizon`, and nothing else. The moment the sweep joins to a table of known runs, a
  lost run row means orphans that live forever — which is precisely the failure it
  exists to catch. It must be able to collect rows from a run nobody remembers, a
  probe that has since been deleted, a run id in a format we no longer write, or a
  code path that stamped `synthetic` and forgot the run id entirely.
- **Its horizon is a floor above every per-class TTL**, generously so — the
  human-confirmed probes measure their lifecycle in days, and a sweep that outran them
  would delete the evidence a probe was still waiting on. It is not lifecycle
  management; it is leak collection, and it can afford to be slow.
- **It takes failure scaffolding too, once the horizon passes.** A month-old failure's
  rows will not be debugged. The *verdict* is what survives — small, bounded, and the
  thing [[EPIC-8]] renders — so nothing of evidential value is lost. Consistent with
  *write real, reap hard*.

**The reframe that makes it worth more than hygiene: a non-empty sweep is a bug
report.** If verify-and-delete is working, this pass takes nothing, every time. Every
row it does take is evidence that a run leaked — a probe that crashed between writing
and verifying, a path that stamped without a run id, a verification that never ran. So
the count is a health metric for the gutter itself and belongs somewhere visible, not
only in a log line. **The expected harvest is zero**, and that is the falsifier.

**The discipline already exists and should be copied rather than reinvented.** The one
cron we have (`index.ts`'s `scheduled`, [[REQ-231]]) sweeps sessions, and its docstring
states both halves of what this needs: it *"reports rather than returns"*, because the
invocation log is *"where a sweep that has quietly been taking nothing for a month is
visible"*; and it *"does not swallow a failure"*, because a throw marks the invocation
failed and makes a broken sweep visible in the dashboard. A garbage collector that
silently stopped looks exactly like a system with no garbage — this is the one place
that recursion is already solved here.

**Safety, because this is a scheduled `DELETE` holding production credentials** — the
hazard §"Gaps" already names, arriving in its most literal form:

- **`synthetic` is `NOT NULL DEFAULT 0`.** A nullable flag makes an unstamped row
  ambiguous under three-valued logic, and ambiguity in a delete predicate is how a real
  contact gets collected.
- **Bounded per invocation.** A predicate bug should be a slow leak of damage, not an
  instant one.
- **It refuses an implausible harvest.** Because the expected count is zero-to-a-handful,
  a sweep about to take ten thousand contacts has a broken predicate rather than a large
  backlog. Stopping and shouting is the correct response, and it is cheaper to write
  than to recover from.

**And the honest limit, so the sweep is not over-trusted: it collects rows that were
marked and not reaped. It cannot see rows that were never marked.** A write path that
stamped neither flag nor run id produced something indistinguishable from real data,
and no sweep will ever find it. That failure is mechanism (2)'s to prevent by being
structural — derived from context and parent, never passed by a caller — which is why
(2) is the one that cannot be a convention.

- **Collection is one statement, because the schema already cascades.**
  `user_acceptances`, `asset_grants` and `contact_events` all declare
  `FOREIGN KEY (contact_id) REFERENCES users (id) ON DELETE CASCADE`, and the baseline
  deliberately leaves `DELETE` alone on the event spine so erasure can reach it
  ([[DOC-37]]). So collecting a synthetic contact is deleting the contact, and the
  whole chain goes with it. This is the same statement the delete-contact affordance
  in §1a needs, which is why they are one piece of work.
- **A probe waiting on a human cannot be collected on a machine's schedule.** The
  forwarding test above is verified by someone clicking a button — in ten minutes, or
  tomorrow, or never. Its records have a lifecycle measured in days where a scheduled
  probe's is measured in seconds. So the TTL is **a property of the probe class, not
  a global constant**, and *"no answer yet"* is a third verdict state rather than a
  failure: rendering an unread inbox as red trains everyone to ignore the indicator,
  and the indicator is the whole product in that case.

So the shape is **lifecycle-driven collection with a time-based backstop**, and the
falsifier is precise: a synthetic row older than **its probe class's** timeout, whose
probe reached a terminal verdict, is a bug.

**Falsifiers, stated so the rule is checkable rather than aspirational:** a count,
view, export or metric a customer can reach that includes a synthetic record; a
probe that takes a shortcut past a real code path; a request that can assert
`synthetic` on a record without holding the platform secret; a notification
delivered on synthetic traffic to anyone but us.

#### Why the probe writes, and why the rows do not survive

The obvious economy is to not store synthetic records at all — validate arrival some
other way and keep the real tables clean. It is the right question and the answer is
no, for a reason that is about coverage rather than tidiness:

**You cannot test a write path without writing.** The flow under test is not "does
the handler return 200", it is "does a form submission become a durable contact
record in this business's tenant". A probe that stops short of the INSERT skips the
constraint checks, the tenancy stamping, the event-spine append, the acceptance
write and whatever the last migration changed about all four — which is most of what
actually breaks, and all of what breaks *silently*. A test that covers the flow right
up to the part most likely to fail is the "testing as an add-on" outcome this epic
exists to prevent, arriving in the disguise of thoroughness.

**And "validated some other way" is a second write path.** To know the probe arrived
we must record that it arrived — in a probe table, a log, a KV entry. So the choice
is never store-versus-not-store; it is *which* table the write lands in. Choosing a
different one leaves the real one unexercised and creates the second implementation
this epic already objects to twice.

**The distinction that does hold — and it is the client's point, correctly aimed — is
between the write and the permanence.** Two things get conflated under "store":

- **The transactional write**, which proves the path. Must happen, against the real
  table, under the real constraints.
- **The durable historical record**, which is what bloats. Must not happen.

So: **write real, reap hard.** Synthetic rows are scaffolding with a TTL in days.
What is kept long-term is the *verdict* — passed/failed, when, how long it took —
which is small, bounded, and is the thing [[EPIC-8]] renders and the uptime claim
rests on. Deleting the scaffolding costs no evidence.

**Most of the volume never needs the flag at all, because tenancy already gutters
it.** The canary tier is a separate business with its own `tenant_id`, and
`forTenant` is terminal — a customer's reads are structurally incapable of returning
a canary row. Since the canary tier is deliberately where the *high-frequency*
traffic lives (§3), the flagged-and-filtered path only ever carries the thin
per-business tier: roughly one probe per business per day. That is the shape worth
designing to, and it reduces the bloat surface by whatever ratio the two tiers end
up at. The column is still wanted — platform-level aggregates *do* cross tenants and
would otherwise count canary traffic as product usage — but the customer-facing
filter, which is the one with thirty call sites and thirty chances to be forgotten,
comes free.

**Sizing, so the concern is settled with a number rather than an assurance.** A
whitepaper probe writes roughly one contact, six events and one message ticket. At
one per business per day with a seven-day TTL, a thousand businesses hold about 56k
synthetic rows in steady state — noise against D1. The bloat that actually deserves
watching is not row count:

- **Message bodies in R2.** Bytes, not rows, and they accumulate per probe email.
- **The embedding corpus.** `knowledge.ts` embeds tickets, so a synthetic message
  ticket joining the corpus costs money per message *and* pollutes retrieval — a
  support answer citing a bot's enquiry. [[EPIC-13]] already requires corpus
  membership default *out*; this is the same rule and the same reason.
- **Erasure surface** ([[DOC-37]]). Fewer rows is genuinely better, and the TTL is
  not a substitute for erasure reaching them.

**Where not writing *is* correct: irreversible flows.** A probe must never send mail
to a real person, move real money on a real card, or delete anything. The answer
there is not to skip the write but to point the flow at a target where the write is
harmless — which is the canary tier again, and is the second independent argument
for keeping it fat and the per-business tier thin.

#### The contract does not invert, and the marker stays a boolean

An earlier draft carried an exception here. [[EPIC-5]]'s closing turn asked for a
customer-initiated forwarding test — Alice presses a button, a message travels her
real mail path, she confirms she received it — and that read as synthetic traffic
whose delivery to the business was the entire point, forcing the marker to carry an
initiator class so the exception could key on it. **Withdrawn.** The client's
counter-analysis is correct and simpler:

**The site owner is not a contact of her own site.** Alice is a contact of 1st
Contact; she is not a lead on `alicesplumbing.com`. The test message is addressed *to*
her business from a sender matching no contact, so it lands where [[EPIC-13]] already
sends inbound mail that matches nobody: *"a pending/unidentified state in the contact
list rather than a mail surface, where triage is promote-to-contact"*. It creates no
contact, no timeline entry and no metric — so there is nothing for the rule to
suppress, and the rule never engages.

**And landing there is arguably the feature.** Seeing the test arrive in the product,
having travelled the real path, is exactly the end-to-end confidence the button exists
to give. (It should be dismissible on confirmation, or the queue accumulates one
message per configuration change and becomes a queue nobody reads.)

**The general rule this yields is better than the exception it replaces: suppression
keys on the record, not on the traffic.** A notification fires because a contact event
happened; if that event is synthetic, it is suppressed. Traffic that produces no
contact event has nothing to decide. That is one rule instead of two classes, and it
sits where the notification is declared rather than in the marker.

**What would bring the initiator class back**, recorded so the reasoning is not
re-derived: a *"test my contact form"* feature. That one genuinely does create a lead
on Alice's own site, and she plausibly should receive the "new enquiry" notification,
because the notification is most of what she is testing. Nothing on the board asks for
it today. If it lands, the distinction returns — and it will be obvious why.

### 1a. Delete contact — the affordance that replaces being clever

The client's call, and it resolves the *"test my contact form"* case recorded above
without a mechanism: **give the business a delete-contact feature and let the test lead
be an ordinary lead.** They see everything that happened and clean up themselves.

It is the better trade for three reasons beyond simplicity:

- **It is a feature customers need regardless** — mistyped addresses, duplicates,
  someone who asks to be removed. [[DOC-37]]'s erasure right needs a hand on it
  eventually, and this is that hand.
- **Transparency beats concealment here.** A customer-initiated test that quietly
  leaves no trace is a test she has to take on faith. One that lands visibly and is
  hers to remove is evidence, and removing it is a second thing proven working.
- **It is the same code as mechanism (4).** The cascade above means collection and
  deletion are one statement against `users`. Building the customer-facing affordance
  builds the probe's collector, and a collector exercised by hand daily is a collector
  that works when the scheduler needs it.

The surface belongs to Contacts ([[EPIC-10]]/[[EPIC-11]]); it is named here because
testability is why it is being pulled forward, and because [[EPIC-15]] is the epic that
would otherwise have paid for the cleverness it replaces.

What it must not become: a delete that leaves the spine intact. A contact removed from
the list whose events survive is the *"we deleted them but kept the history"* mistake
the baseline schema explicitly warns about.

### 2. Probes — what actually gets exercised

A probe is a named flow with a declared expected outcome, a schedule, and a
verdict. The set, grouped by what breaks:

**Serving** — the `bin/smoke` set, generalised from one origin to every published
site: apex and www resolve, TLS valid with expiry runway, expected content marker
present, referenced assets 200, redirect grammar holds, an unpublished key 404s.

**Forms** — a real submission through the public path, with the signed marker,
landing as a flagged record: contact form, whitepaper/asset request, and each
form instance type as it lands. The client's named worked example — the whitepaper
sign-up — is the right first probe because it is the longest chain in the product
today: submit → contact created → acceptance recorded → asset granted → email sent
→ delivery webhook. Six components, one assertion.

**Email out** — composition, ESP acceptance, delivery webhook, and arrival. §3
and the decision section below settle how far the round trip has to go.

**Email in** — a message sent to an address on the domain under test, travelling
our real `MX` and Email Routing and Worker and forwarding rules, confirmed to
arrive. [[EPIC-13]] already specifies this as its forwarding test and it is the
same probe.

**Payments** — a full synthetic path through the production code, plus the cheap
account-state checks. How the money side of that is made testable is an open design
question, treated below rather than settled here.

**Platform plumbing** — control app reachable and Access-challenging, ESP API
answering, Cloudflare Email Routing accepting, D1/KV/R2 answering, our own DNS
resolving, the publish pipeline completing.

### 3. The three tiers, and the reason they are three

The client's split is right, and [[CHAT-54]] supplies the argument that makes it
cheap: **code paths are shared and break for everyone at once; configuration is
per-customer and breaks one at a time.**

| Tier | Runs against | Frequency | Proves |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Platform** | canary sites we own, in the platform tenant | high | every flow, every path variant, full matrix |
| **Per-business** | the real customer's real site | low — one representative path daily | *their* DNS, *their* sending domain, *their* connected account, *their* config |
| **Post-deploy** | the deploy target | once, as a gate | that the change that just landed did not break a flow |

The canary tier carries the volume because synthetic data costs nothing there —
there is no customer record to pollute and no reputation to spend. The per-business
tier is deliberately thin, and the constraint is specific to this market: a plumber
sends 200 real emails a month, and twelve unengaged test sends a week is 20% test
mail that nobody opens. Monitoring that degrades the reputation it is monitoring is
not monitoring. So: **continuous on the checks that cost nothing** (DNS, TLS, HTTP,
Stripe account state, DKIM resolving) and **thin and event-triggered on the ones
that consume sending volume**.

**Post-deploy is an invocation mode, not a fourth probe set.** One definition,
three ways to call it: scheduled, as a deploy gate, and on demand. `bin/smoke`
becomes the deploy-gate invocation of the serving probes rather than a parallel
implementation of them. Two implementations of "is the site serving" would
eventually disagree and the customer would believe the wrong one.

#### What the automated-deployment epic will want from this, and the trap in it

Noted for the epic the client is about to open: it consumes probe definitions and
must not author its own, on the same "two implementations eventually disagree"
grounds as `bin/smoke` above. Three things this epic owes it, and one warning.

**The environment seam already exists; the environment does not.** `bin/deploy`
takes `--env`, defaults to `production`, and documents `--env staging` in its own
usage — but only `[env.production]` is actually defined in either `wrangler.toml`.
So a second environment is a configuration exercise rather than an architectural
one, which is the cheap half.

**The trap is assuming probes in a staging environment can be the deploy gate.**
They cannot, and the reason is specific rather than general: staging has different
DNS, no real `MX`, test ESP credentials, no real connected accounts and no real
customer config. The flows BFM exists for are *precisely the ones that differ
between the environments*. Staging probes catch code regressions — genuinely
valuable, and the fastest feedback available — and are structurally incapable of
catching the config and integration failures that are this epic's whole subject.
A deployment epic will be tempted to conflate them because it is much cheaper, and
the failure is silent: a green gate that never tested the thing that breaks.

**Which means the canary tier is not a staging environment.** It is real production,
on real DNS, with real credentials, against sites we happen to own. That is what
makes synthetic traffic there both safe and meaningful, and it is why §3 puts the
volume there rather than in a sandbox.

**So the gate is two-phase, and only the second phase needs the rollback story**
the gaps section flags: staging probes before the deploy, canary probes in
production after it, with progressive rollout the mechanism that lets the second be
a gate rather than a postmortem.

**A fourth invocation mode is worth naming now because it is product, not ops:**
on-demand debug. Alice says "my emails aren't arriving"; instead of a support
thread she gets a real probe run and a specific cause within minutes. That is the
moment the AI panel earns its place, and it costs nothing extra once the probes
exist.

### 4. Where it runs — decomposed, because "outside Cloudflare" is three different
requirements with three different prices

The client's instinct is correct and the reason is exact: *a monitor on the
infrastructure it monitors goes quiet precisely when it should shout.* But moving
everything outside is expensive (no bindings, no service RPC, secrets duplicated
into a second cloud) and most of it is unnecessary. The requirement decomposes:

1. **The bulk of probe execution can stay on Cloudflare.** It needs the bindings,
   the service bindings, the platform secret and the ESP credentials, and it is
   testing Cloudflare-hosted things anyway. If Cloudflare is down, everything is
   down and a probe from anywhere says the same thing.
2. **The alarm path must not be on Cloudflare.** The failure being designed against
   is not "the probe returns red", it is "the probe returns nothing and nothing
   notices". An outage that takes the alarm with it is indistinguishable from
   all-green.
3. **Outside-in HTTP must be genuinely outside.** A Worker fetching an origin it is
   co-located with can be satisfied by internal routing that a real visitor's packet
   would never take. At least one outside-in check per site has to come from a
   network we do not run.
4. **The monitor needs its own liveness.** A dead-man's switch: the runner
   heartbeats outward on a schedule, and *the absence of a heartbeat* is the alarm.
   This is the single cheapest thing in the epic and the one that makes the rest
   trustworthy.

**On third-party uptime services (the client's "ping" question): buy the outermost
layer, build the flows.** They are good at exactly the thing we cannot do
ourselves — being somewhere else — and for £10–20/month that is a bargain for
(2), (3) and (4). They are useless for everything else this epic cares about: they
cannot hold our auth, cannot drive a six-component form chain, cannot read a
mailbox, cannot ask Stripe about a connected account. So the recommendation is a
small external service as dead-man's switch and naive outside-in prober, and
everything with a flow in it built here. That is not a compromise position; it is
each tool doing the only thing it is good at.

### 5. Evidence and retention

Probe runs are the evidence behind the promise in the second section, so they are
records, not logs: what ran, against what, when, with what verdict, and what
changed since the last run. Retention long enough to answer "was my site up last
Tuesday" — which is the question a customer asks after they hear about an outage
from someone else. Verdict *history* is what makes flapping detectable and what
makes an uptime figure computable; a current-state-only store can say green and
nothing more.

#### The code map, and the two mechanisms that already exist in shape

**Entry — and mechanism (1) needs no new concept.** `apps/public-site/src/lead.ts`
already has `RESERVED_FIELDS`: wire-level machine artefacts — a trap, a Turnstile
token, the form handle — stripped before anything reaches the record, on the stated
reasoning that *"storing them would put three machine artefacts in a contact's
provenance beside the words a person typed."* The synthetic marker is exactly that
kind of artefact and belongs in exactly that list. What is new is only the signature
check, not the plumbing.

**The one interface change.** `LeadIntakeBinding.captureLead(spec)` — the RPC seam
between `public-site` and `control-app` — gains one field. [[REQ-223]] §3.2 made this
an RPC entrypoint with no URL that reaches it, so the marker cannot be injected from
outside the seam.

**Writes to stamp:** `addContact` (`people.ts` → `users`), `recordAcceptance`
(`acceptances.ts` → `user_acceptances`), `openGrant` (`people.ts`/`grants.ts` →
`asset_grants`), `recordEvent` (`events.ts` → `contact_events`), `sendRecordedEmail`
(`messages.ts` → message tickets, and R2 blobs behind them).

**Mechanism (2)'s derivation rule is already implemented, structurally.**
`recordEvent` inserts `SELECT ?, u.id, u.tenant_id, … FROM users u` — `business_id`
is *derived from the contact and never supplied*, which the baseline schema states as
the reason an event cannot be filed under the wrong business. `synthetic` rides the
same `SELECT` as `u.synthetic`. So the async-continuation case — a delivery webhook
arriving thirty seconds later in a request carrying no marker — is answered by a
pattern already in the code rather than by a new one.

**Reads to filter, and they are few:** `peopleOf`, `personOf`, `personDetail`,
`contactChangeHead` and `contactsChangedSince` in `people.ts`, reached from three
call sites in `router.ts` (the people list, the contact detail, the change poller).
Every one already takes `Scope`, which is a one-field interface (`{ businessId }`)
threaded everywhere. **The gutter rides `Scope`** — the `forTenant` property obtained
without inventing a second handle, and the reason (3) is a small change rather than a
sweep.

**The matrix the client named** is `builder/people-axes.js` ([[DOC-44]] §3,
[[REQ-188]]) — the Lead/Member axes. It renders from `peopleOf`'s output, so
filtering at `peopleOf` should cover it; worth confirming by test rather than by
reading, since that is precisely the assumption this epic distrusts.

**Migration `0013` is next**, and four tables want the column: `users`,
`contact_events`, `user_acceptances`, `asset_grants`.

#### The minimal first build

The client's scoping for now: **place the guttering, prove it works, do not build a
suite.** That resolves to the four mechanisms at their smallest honest size, plus
five assertions — chosen because each one falsifies a different mechanism, and
between them nothing in §1 is taken on trust:

1. A marked submission **runs the full path** and produces the records — falsifies a
   short-circuit, which is mechanism (1)'s failure.
2. Those records **appear in no customer read** — list, detail, change feed, axes —
   falsifying (3).
3. An event written on a synthetic contact **is synthetic without any caller passing
   a flag** — falsifying (2)'s derivation, the leak-prone half.
4. A **forged or unsigned** marker is refused and the record is written **real** —
   falsifying the attack that matters, which is marking real traffic as test.
5. Collection **removes the synthetic records and leaves the real ones** — falsifying
   (4).

Everything else in this epic — probes, tiers, external execution, verdict storage —
waits. The gutter is the only part that gets more expensive by being deferred, which
is the whole of why it goes first.

## Decisions taken, and why

**BFM is a checker family inside [[EPIC-7]]'s engine, not a second engine.**
[[EPIC-7]] already specifies target → checker → verdict → notification generically,
explicitly instructs that it be built generic with one checker, and names "HTTP
reachability and mail deliverability" as the same shape. Building a second
scheduler and a second verdict store here would be the exact mistake [[EPIC-8]]
names: two places computing the same verdict, disagreeing, and the customer
believing the wrong one.

**But BFM places three demands on that engine that must land before it is built,
or it gets rewritten.** This is the most time-sensitive finding in the epic:

1. **A checker may be two-phase and asynchronous.** Send an email at T; the verdict
   arrives when it lands in a mailbox at T+4m, or fails to by T+15m. A synchronous
   observe-and-return interface cannot express this, and every interesting probe in
   §2 has this shape.
2. **A checker may write to production.** That makes rate, ordering and blast
   radius the engine's concern rather than each checker's — it must be able to say
   "not more than once an hour for this business" and mean it.
3. **A checker may execute somewhere other than a Worker.** The interface must not
   assume the runtime, per §4.

**The email round trip stops inside our own system — with one boundary crossing.**
Taking the client's question directly: yes, out-into-the-world-and-back is enough,
and tracing to a third-party inbox per customer is overkill. Our own routing is the
thing under test, and a loop that leaves via the ESP and returns via our `MX`,
Email Routing, Worker and forwarding rules exercises every component we own.

The one caveat that costs almost nothing to close: **a loop that both starts and
ends inside our infrastructure shares fate with our infrastructure**, and it also
cannot see the last hop. So at least one destination is a mailbox on a provider we
do not run — a single bot account, per [[CHAT-54]], not a seed panel. That one
account makes the loop cross a boundary and gives a free lower-bound spam signal:
if mail lands in spam in a mailbox that opens, clicks and rescues everything, the
problem is severe and real.

What the round trip still **cannot** prove is that mail reached *Alice's own*
inbox — we cannot read her mailbox. The available proxy, from [[CHAT-54]], is a
reserved `bfm-@` address on her domain routed to our bot mailbox: identical path,
proves routing and Worker and forward, and silence-plus-acceptance is decent
evidence for the final hop since a failure there bounces back to us.

**Functional monitoring and inbox placement are different jobs and this epic owns
only the first.** "Did it arrive at all" is BFM and is a pass/fail alarm. "Inbox
or spam" is a slow-moving gauge, measured by a third party, on an event-triggered
cadence, and it belongs to [[EPIC-13]]'s deliverability scope. Conflating them
produces a monitor that is both a bad alarm and a bad gauge. The two even have
opposite disciplines — a measurement seed must never engage with test mail or the
measurement becomes circular; a functional canary must engage hard, because a
well-fed account is what makes it a reliable canary.

**Payments must have a full synthetic path through the production code. How, is a
design input to [[EPIC-9]] and is deliberately left open here.**

The requirement is settled and is the client's: *"we need a full synthetic test path
available."* Nothing about payments is built yet, so the useful output of this epic
is not a chosen mechanism but a constraint that reaches [[EPIC-9]] before it designs
credential handling — because that is the decision which is cheap now and structural
later.

**One fact that constrains every option:** Stripe test mode is a genuinely separate
environment, not a sandbox overlay on live. Test keys never touch live data, test
and live objects occupy different ID spaces and cannot reference each other, and the
two modes deliver webhooks with *different signing secrets*. So there is no
single-credential arrangement in which the same account transacts both ways, and any
design has to say which mode a given request is in before it can even verify the
webhook.

Two viable shapes, and they are complementary rather than alternatives:

**A — test credentials loaded in production, selected by the synthetic marker.** This
is the client's proposal and it buys the most: the deployed production code path runs
end to end — routing, session creation, redirect, webhook receipt, signature
verification, the DB write, the event append, notification suppression — on real
deployed code with nothing stubbed. It is the only option that exercises *capture*
and the full webhook round trip at no cost, so it can run at canary frequency.

What it costs, stated plainly because these are the failure possibilities the client
anticipated:

- **Credential selection becomes a code path with a correctness requirement**, and
  its dangerous failure is silent: picking the test key for a real customer means the
  money never moves while our record says a payment succeeded. The marker must be the
  *only* input to that choice and it must be unforgeable — which is the same
  requirement §1 already imposes, now load-bearing for money rather than for tidiness.
- **Two webhook signing secrets** means either two endpoints or one endpoint that
  knows the mode before verifying. Worth deciding rather than discovering.
- **It can only reach the canary tier.** Test-mode objects cannot reference a live
  connected account, so this proves the shared code path — which is the half that
  breaks for everyone at once — and proves nothing about Alice's own account.

**B — a real card on live keys, authorised and voided.** Auth-and-void is materially
better than the charge-and-refund [[CHAT-54]] reached for: a voided authorisation
moves no money and, unlike a refund, does not leave the processing fee behind. It is
the only shape that can run against a **real connected account**, so it is the
per-business tier's answer — proving Alice's account genuinely accepts money today,
which is the thing she is paying us to be sure of. Caveats to carry into [[EPIC-9]]:
cards support auth/capture separation and several local payment methods do not; an
authorisation still places a visible hold; repeated identical small auths may
eventually interest Radar; and a void proves less about Connect's money *movement*
than a capture does, since transfers and application fees resolve at capture.

**So the likely resolution maps onto the tier split this epic already has** — A for
the canary tier at high frequency, B for the per-business tier at low frequency — but
that is a recommendation for [[EPIC-9]] to test, not a decision taken here.

**Independently, and available whatever is chosen**, three checks cost nothing and
should exist regardless:

- **`charges_enabled` and `payouts_enabled` per connected account** — the highest-value
  check on the list, because it catches the actual failure mode: Stripe disables a
  Connect account when verification requirements change, and the business finds out
  when the money stops.
- **`requirements.currently_due` and `past_due`** — and warning Alice *before* the
  deadline is a product feature, not monitoring. *"Stripe needs a document from you
  by the 14th or your payments stop"* is exactly what a one-van business misses.
- **Checkout session creation and webhook signature verification**, which exercise
  most of the integration without charging anything.

**Staging probes must be able to carry an identity, and the probe interface has to
know that before it settles.** [[EPIC-16]] §5 keeps staging private behind Cloudflare
Access on both hostnames, which is right — a public staging deployment runs weaker
config by design and its forms are real forms posting into real intake code. The cost
lands here: a probe aimed at staging needs an auth seam for a service token, and
[[BUG-59]]'s `SERVICE_TOKEN_IDENTITIES` already maps a service token's `common_name`
to a person, so the mechanism exists. Recorded now because retrofitting auth into a
probe interface after it has settled is the same class of mistake as retrofitting the
marker.

**Canary sites live in the platform tenant.** `TENANT_ID` already names the
platform's own business (`wrangler.toml`, [[REQ-168]]/[[REQ-190]]), and the
platform is a business like any other in this model. The canaries are a small set
of example sites that between them exercise every feature — which also makes them
the demo set and the regression corpus, so they earn their upkeep three ways.

**Do not allowlist our own senders in any monitoring mailbox's admin console.** The
natural reflex when a monitor's mail starts going to spam is to allowlist it so the
monitor keeps working — and that silently destroys the only placement signal the
system has. Recorded here because it is a 2am decision and the person making it
will not be reading [[EPIC-13]].

## Boundaries

- **The surface anyone looks at** → [[EPIC-8]]. This epic produces verdicts and
  evidence; it does not draw. Health verdicts are rendered there, not recomputed.
- **The scheduler, verdict store and notification routing** → [[EPIC-7]]. This epic
  contributes checkers and the three requirements above; it does not build a second
  engine.
- **Notification delivery, channels, and opt-out** → [[EPIC-14]]. A probe declares
  that something happened to an audience; it never learns what a channel is.
- **Inbox placement measurement** → [[EPIC-13]].
- **The email surfaces the probes exercise** → [[EPIC-13]]. The gutter contract
  §5 of that epic carries is the same contract, defined here.
- **Forms** → [[EPIC-10]]; **activity log and the event spine** → [[EPIC-11]];
  **payments and Connect** → [[EPIC-9]]. Each owns a `synthetic` column and a
  default-excluding read; this epic owns the rule they honour.
- **Deploying, rolling back, progressive rollout, the staging environment and its
  fixtures** → [[EPIC-16]]. This one supplies the probes it gates on, §3's two-phase
  finding and the staging warning; it does not own the pipeline. [[EPIC-16]] in turn
  imposes the service-token auth seam recorded in the decisions below.
- **Developer-time tests** — vitest, UATs, the capability matrix — are a different
  thing entirely and are not in scope. BFM runs against production, on a schedule,
  forever. The word "test" is doing double duty and the two must not share
  machinery.

## Gaps worth naming

**The gutter is a cross-epic obligation with no owner but this ticket.** Six epics
each need a `synthetic` column and a default-excluding read. If the rule lives only
here, the first surface built elsewhere will not have it, and the retrofit is the
expensive case this epic exists to avoid. Whatever REQ lands first under this epic
should be the gutter primitive itself, not a probe.

**A deploy gate that runs after the deploy is not a gate.** `bin/smoke` today runs
once the bytes are already live. Turning post-deploy probing into something that can
actually *stop* a bad deploy needs a rollback story that does not exist — and it is
the automated-deployment epic's to write, not this one's. What this epic owes it is
§3's two-phase finding and the warning about staging.

**There is no second environment**, only the `--env` seam that would accept one.
Until there is, every probe runs against production, which is survivable precisely
because the canary tier is designed for it — but it means a probe bug has no
rehearsal space, and §1's kill switch is the only thing standing between a bad loop
and every customer.

**Probe cost at N customers is unmodelled.** Per-business daily probes across DNS,
HTTP, TLS, Stripe and one email send is a per-customer recurring cost in requests,
sends and third-party API calls. Fine at ten customers; a line item at a thousand.

**A probe is a write path with production credentials on a schedule.** That is a
serious piece of machinery to leave unattended: a bug in a loop is a bug that runs
every five minutes against every customer forever. Rate limits, blast-radius caps
and a global kill switch are requirements, not polish.

**Flapping.** [[EPIC-7]] already asks this about DNS; it is worse for flows, where
a single failed probe may be a transient ESP hiccup. How many consecutive failures
before a verdict changes, and does a customer-visible verdict use a different
threshold from an operator-visible one.

**Erasure has to reach synthetic records too** ([[DOC-37]]), and their short TTL is
not a substitute for it. §1's storage subsection names the two bloat vectors that
are not row count — R2 message bodies and the embedding corpus — and both of them
are also erasure surface.

## Open questions

1. **Does [[EPIC-7]] absorb the three checker requirements, or does BFM stand
   alone?** Recommendation: absorb, and land the requirements before [[EPIC-7]]
   builds. This blocks nothing today and blocks everything if it is asked late.
2. **Where does the external runner live** — a third-party synthetic service, a
   small VPS, GitHub Actions, or a second cloud? Recommendation in §4: buy the
   outermost layer, build the rest here.
3. ~~**Is `synthetic` on the contact, on the event, or on both?**~~ **Resolved: both**,
   so the read filter is never a join — a column is paid once at write, a join on every
   page load. With it, two further gutter decisions ([[DOC-54]] §6): `Scope` grows the
   visibility field, and the run id is `newId('run')` riding inside the signed marker.
4. **What is the TTL, and does it differ by tier?** Canary rows can be reaped
   aggressively because nobody reads them; a per-business row may be worth keeping
   long enough to answer *"show me the probe that failed on Tuesday"*. The verdict is
   kept either way, so this is only about the scaffolding.
5. **Which mechanism makes the payments path synthetic** — test credentials in
   production, live-key auth-and-void, or both against different tiers. Owned by
   [[EPIC-9]]; this epic supplies the requirement and the constraint.
6. **How is a human-confirmed verdict modelled?** [[EPIC-13]]'s forwarding test
   returns pass / fail / *no answer yet*, and the third is not a failure. Whether
   that tri-state generalises to every probe or stays special to human-confirmed ones
   decides the verdict schema, and [[EPIC-7]] is the one that stores it.
7. **Who hears about a per-business probe failure?** [[EPIC-7]]'s routing rule says
   most verdicts are for us. *"Your contact form is broken"* may be the exception —
   it is actionable, it is urgent, and finding out from a customer is worse.
8. **How many canary sites, and who keeps them current?** A canary that stopped
   exercising a feature is a probe that passes for the wrong reason.
9. **Does the customer see their own uptime evidence**, and at what granularity?
   This is what makes the promise in §2 real, and it is an [[EPIC-8]] decision this
   epic supplies the data for.
10. **Does a staging environment change the tier model?** Recommendation in §3: it
   adds a fast pre-deploy phase and replaces nothing, because the canary tier's value
   is that it is real. Worth settling with the deployment epic rather than in
   parallel with it.
11. **Retention for probe history and for synthetic records** — different answers,
   both needed.

## Children

**[[DOC-54]] — "The test gutter: how manufactured traffic is marked, hidden, and
collected"** (`doc_kind: architecture`). The design document for everything in §1,
§1a and §6: five requirements with their falsifiers, the architecture against the code
as it stands, what each caller and each epic on deck inherits, and the sequencing.
Scoped deliberately to the gutter — §2–§5 of this epic are named there as explicitly
out of scope, because the gutter is the only part that gets more expensive by being
deferred.

The first *code* child should be the gutter primitive, and it is **four** things rather
than three: the signed marker, the `synthetic` column, the default-excluding read
handle, **and the reaper**. The fourth is not a follow-up — a marker without a TTL is
a slow leak with a clean conscience, and shipping the first three alone is how the
bloat the client asked about actually arrives.

It comes first because every other epic is waiting on the contract rather than on a
probe. And per §1, the canary tier needs only the marker and the reaper; the column
and the read handle exist for the thin per-business tier and for platform-level
aggregates that cross tenants.

**§6 sizes it against the code as it stands** — one migration, one RPC field, five
write paths, five read paths, and five assertions — and records the two places the
mechanism already exists in shape, so the first child is smaller than this epic's
framing suggests. [[DOC-54]] carries that sizing as a specification.

**And it does not start until [[EPIC-10]] is working and tested.** The capture chain
landed recently and is unverified; a failure after the gutter is threaded through it
would be ambiguous between the two. The baseline is what makes the gutter's assertions
mean anything, because only then is it known that the records *would* have appeared.
