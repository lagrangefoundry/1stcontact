# Consultation routing: cohorts, the clarity profile, stage stances and the support infrastructure

**Status:** working notes from an EPIC-19 design conversation with the operator (2026-10-26).
Proposed revision to [[DOC-35]] and input to the consultant-prompt rewrite. Not yet implemented.

**Companions:**
- [[DOC-35]] (personas, modes, registers): this extends it.
- [[DOC-33]] (the consultation playbook): its stages are the backbone here.
- [[DOC-70]] (REQ-465 prompt audit): its nine contradictions are resolved through this model (§10).
- [[EPIC-20]] (token cost): §9's infrastructure is also the main token lever.

## 1. Why this document exists

Recent work tuned the consultant for one kind of client and made it opinionated about process and
about the site. The opinions are good, but several were turned into **host gates**, and for clients
who already know things (or already have a site) they act as a straitjacket. Observed so far:
- Ariel's favicon request turned into a forced restructure (BUG-297).
- Hal's story keeps top billing because the comparison-board summary fixes section order downstream.

The goal is to serve more kinds of client with **the same backbone flow**, by letting stages be
confirmed or skipped instead of always elicited. And to do it without making the consultant hold the
whole decision space in its context at all times.

## 3. The axes

From [[DOC-35]] §3, three independent dials:

| Axis | Governs | Set by | Stability |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Persona** | The opening: the first fifteen minutes | The job they're hiring us for | Stable for a session |
| **Mode** | Agency: who sets the agenda (directed / collaborative / client-driven) | Demonstrated fluency; detected, not asked | Continuous, reversible per stretch |
| **Register** | Vocabulary, chunk size, explanation depth, consent frequency, reassurance | Web confidence and anxiety | Continuous, reversible |

This document adds a fourth.

### 3.1 Clarity: a profile, not a score

**Clarity is how settled each part of this project's brief is.** It is independent of fluency. A
fluent, client-driven founder can know the look exactly and still not have their message for *this*
project, and would benefit from the story board.

| Dimension | "Clear" means | Settled by |
|---|---|---|
| Outcome | They can say what success looks like in six months | Opening |
| Audience and position | Who it's for, and why them over the next search result | Comparison board |
| Message and voice | The headline and how the site sounds | Story board |
| Look | Colours, logo, type, feel | Direction card, then design |
| Structure | Which pages, and what goes on them | Site plan |
| Material | The facts, stories, photos and prices the site needs | The material track (§7) |

**Judged from evidence, not asked.** This is [[DOC-35]] §7.2 applied to the brief. "Is your
messaging clear?" gets a confident yes from everyone. "In one sentence, who is this for and why
would they pick you?" shows the truth. A dimension is clear when the client can produce it, not
when they say it is.

**Re-judged throughout the project.** Confidence can drop as an idea is realised. Signals:
- the same thing revised repeatedly;
- feedback contradicting what they said earlier;
- hedging ("I'm not sure this is us any more");
- approving a draft without liking it.

Any of these lowers that one dimension, and its stage can be re-offered, citing the evidence.
Going back is a normal, cheap move, not a failure.

## 4. Guardrails and opinions

The host currently enforces a mix of two different kinds of rule. They need sorting.

- **Guardrails stay hard.** Security, the structured-only envelope, reliability, legibility, broken
  layout, the spend ceiling, honesty about the site's state. No client opts out.
- **Opinions become advice the client can overrule.** Home length, section order, page structure,
  comparison boards, story board, headline choice, process order. The consultant raises an
  opinion **once, clearly** ([[DOC-35]] §8.5: pushback isn't modal). When the client decides
  otherwise, the decision is **recorded**, and the host's checks respect it rather than re-firing.

BUG-297's "the client's no sticks" is the first instance. This makes it the general rule.

**The hold audit** is the exercise of listing every host hold, gate and cue and classing each as a
guardrail or an opinion. Opinions get rewired so that a recorded client or route decision satisfies
them. Its inventory overlaps [[DOC-70]]'s "host-enforced" category, so it should be done as part of
the rewrite (§10), not separately. Starting list:
- design_first (REQ-448);
- structure_first (REQ-460);
- comps reviewed before the first build;
- the first-draft review owed (REQ-458 `DRAFT_NOT_REVIEWED` / REQ-427 critique cue);
- the promise hold (REQ-428);
- the layout-fault hold;
- the home-length hold (BUG-287/BUG-297);
- speak-first (BUG-278/BUG-298);
- the first-draft hand-over line;
- the spend ceiling.

### 4.1 The client's request is the turn's scope

Observed on First-Draft-Test Dina's Diner 3, 2026-10-26 19:01–19:37. The client asked for one thing:
make the hero photo a video. Before the video was made, the host's holds produced a cascade:
1. the first-draft review was owed, so the consultant called critique;
2. every finding had to be answered ("fixed" was the easy answer), so builders reworked home,
   header, catering and menu;
3. the hero became a split, which shrank the full-size photo the client had picked this version for;
4. the home-length hold then forced a restructure;
5. that restructure broke the Find us section.

The video arrived after the client's third request ("Please stop making all the other changes and
make the video"), 35 minutes in. For a directed client this is the most frustrating failure there
is.

Rules (for the rewrite):
- **A specific client request takes priority.** It's the thing the consultant most wants to close
  first. Other work may run in parallel, but nothing the host owes (a review, open findings,
  budgets, polish) delays the request, and the client sees it done first.
- **What happens to owed work afterwards depends on mode:**
  - **Directed (cohort zero): ask forgiveness, not permission.** The consultant goes ahead with the
    improvements it judges worthwhile, and says plainly what it's doing and why. A directed
    client can't judge the trade-off, so asking only hands them a decision they can't make.
  - **Collaborative / client-driven:** owed work is presented as recommendations. "While I was
    there I noticed three things I'd improve on the home page. Want me to?"
- **Never undo something the client chose.** A decision the client made or approved (the full-size
  hero) has client provenance. Review findings and opinions can't change it without asking, in
  every mode.
- **Cost is never the client's concern.** Improvement work costs the operator, not the client
  (cf. *No costs to clients*). Recommendations are framed by value and by the client's time and
  preference, never as if price were a factor.

The Dina 3 cascade was partly an artefact: the operator was playing cohort zero, and most of these
holds were added after that site's first draft was built. The rules stand regardless.

## 5. Stage stances

The backbone stays the [[DOC-33]] stage sequence. What changes is how each stage is entered. For
each stage the consultant takes one of four **stances**:

- **Assume we proceed**
- **Recommend we proceed**
- **Offer** it
- **Assume we skip**

The stance comes from **need** (clarity on that stage's dimension) and **mode** (who decides):

| Need | Directed (cohort 0) | Collaborative / client-driven |
|---|---|---|
| Low clarity | Assume we proceed | Recommend we proceed, saying why |
| Partial | Assume we proceed, kept short | Offer it |
| Clear | Assume we proceed, as a one-line confirmation | Assume we skip, confirming in one line what we take as settled |

**Register** sets how the stance is said.

**Every stance except a silent proceed states what the stage is for and how long it takes.** For
example: "The story board: about ten minutes. It settles the headline and how the site sounds.
Your look is already clear, so I'd skip the comparison board and do this one."

**Inherited and given decisions.** A decision already known from the client's existing site
(cohort 2) or their brief (cohort 1) has **provenance**: elicited, inherited or given. It turns
its stage into a confirmation: "Your site leads with the menu, so I've kept that. Say if you want
it changed."

## 7. The material track (the questions that feel like chit-chat)

Questions like the car rally or the coffee bell are **gathering material**: the specifics that make
the site read as this business rather than a template. They are a separate ongoing track alongside
the stages, not a stage. Today they read as conversation because nothing says what they're for, and
nothing shows where the answers went.

1. **Say what it's for, once, up front:** "As we go, I'll ask about the details only you know.
   Those become the words on your site. The more specific, the less generic it reads."
2. **Close the loop on every answer:** "That rally goes near the top of the Catering page."
3. **It's negotiable like a stage.** A time-poor or fluent client can hand material over in bulk (a
   brochure, an old site, a price list, notes) instead of being interviewed. Cohort 0 gets the
   interview.
4. **Visible progress,** per page in the progress panel ("Catering: two stories, prices; no photo
   yet"), so it reads as building the site.

## 8. The meta-conversation (process negotiation)

- **Once, early, with a recommendation.** Not a question at every stage, which is overhead. Near the
  opening, for clients who can judge it: "Here's how I suggest we work: X, Y, then a first draft.
  Your look is clear, so we skip Z." Cohort 0 gets the route without the question. They can't judge
  what an exercise would buy them, so asking only hands them a decision they can't make.
- **Recorded.** The route is a set of stage decisions in the plan (stance and provenance). The host
  honours them.
- **Reopened on evidence.** A skipped stage comes back when its clarity drops (§3.1), with the
  evidence named. Often it's the first draft that shows a fluent client their message wasn't as
  settled as they thought.

## 9. Support infrastructure: keep the matrix out of the always-on prompt

The model is real complexity, but it's finite and well defined, and most of it isn't needed on
every turn. Two pieces would hold it outside the consultant's context.

### 9.1 A persisted conversation profile

A structured record on the site plan, written by the consultant and read by the host:
- persona;
- mode and register (current estimate, each with its evidence);
- clarity per dimension (low / partial / clear, with the evidence and when it was last judged);
- per-stage route decisions: stance, provenance (elicited / inherited / given), and who decided.

It's the same object the host's holds read (§4) and the per-turn plan entry summarises. The plan
already has decision states. This extends them with provenance and a profile.

### 9.2 Stage guidance on demand

A lookup the consultant calls when it enters or reconsiders a stage (e.g. `stage_guide(stage)`,
computed from the profile). It returns:
- the stance for this client now, with its reason;
- what the stage is for, and how long it takes;
- what to say for that stance, at this register;
- the signals that would change it;
- which holds apply, and what satisfies them.

The always-on priming keeps only the core:
- the axes and the stance idea;
- guardrails;
- "check the guide when you enter a stage".

Stage-specific text, now spread over priming, manuals, tool descriptions, tail lines and cues
(DOC-70's 3–5 copies per rule), moves into the guide and appears only when it applies. That's
also EPIC-20's largest saving.

The stance matrix (§5) is then **code with UATs**, not prose the model must apply correctly. Each
cohort gets a first-draft-test fixture:
- cohort 0: the current Dina tests;
- cohort 1: a scripted brief-first client;
- cohort 2: Sarah's Joyful Culinary capture.

## 10. Rewrite, and DOC-70's contradictions

The [[DOC-70]] audit found 169 findings, mostly duplication, and **9 contradictions** needing a
decision. Most of them are symptoms of the missing model: the same rule written for different
situations without saying which situation applies. Resolving them inside this model:

| DOC-70 | Resolved by |
|---|---|
| D-1 build-start message endings | Material track (§7): end with a material question while that track is open for the current stance; the Delegate `note` carries what and how long. |
| D-2 options vs one proposal | Mode × clarity: options that differ in kind where that dimension's clarity is low (look, structure, headline); one proposal where it's clear or for words and facts; build directly when the client is client-driven with a clear brief. |
| D-3 hand-back length | The stage guide, per stage: first-draft hand-over once; later passes one line; routine returns short. |
| D-4 "build sooner, story later" | Replaced by the route: the order is the stage sequence, and a stage's stance decides whether it waits for anything. |
| D-5 comps: talk or record first | The stage guide for the comparison board. The BUG-245 card-first form, with talk focused on the one tension. |
| D-6 headline "move on" vs must | **Decided (§12.4): a headline must be agreed to move forward.** No silent "move on". An empty headline is allowed only as the client's explicit choice. |
| D-7 ReportBug on own initiative | A guardrail, outside the matrix: our defect affecting this client's site is reported, since nobody else will. |
| D-8 re-inspect builder output | A guardrail: the consultant checks delegated work (tokens are scarce, but checking is not optional). |
| D-9 plan entry while draft unseen | Infrastructure: the plan entry is generated from the profile and shows only what the current stance makes due; fix the 2,000-character truncation. |

**Recommendation: rewrite rather than patch.** Patching the 9 contradictions in place re-encodes
them as more rules in more places. A rewrite would put the axes, guardrails and stance model at the
centre, with stage guidance behind a lookup. It would apply DOC-70's high-confidence cuts naturally
(one copy, at the point of use) and keep the tests that pin behaviour rather than wording.

**Sequencing with EPIC-20.** EPIC-20's cuts that the rewrite would replace anyway (stage-specific
priming, duplicated cue text) should wait. Its grant and projection cuts (Cuts 3–5, framework
manuals, unused tools) are independent and can proceed.

## 12. Stage walk (with the operator)

### 12.1 The Opening (walked 2026-10-26)

Structure agreed, with these changes.

**Their word for it.** Not everyone has a "business". Some have an organization or a project. The
consultant offers the choice in its first question, then uses whichever word the client uses. The
choice is recorded in the profile.

**Material is the opening's centre of gravity.** An existing website is gold: it gives a first
answer to almost every question. So are other sources:
- social profiles (Facebook, Instagram, LinkedIn);
- documents (Google Docs, Word, spreadsheets, decks, grant applications, brochures, price lists).

Format conversion is a later concern. The opening's job is:
1. to get the client to offer these up early ("Have you got a website, social pages, or documents
   about it? They'll save us a lot of questions");
2. to work out how good they are.

**The material spectrum is the opening's key judgement.** Where on this scale is what they've
given us?
1. **Complete and current:** it tells their story and shows their brand and message. A quick
   validation, then straight to a first draft.
2. **Directionally useful but flawed:** out of date, inadequate, off-brand, or disliked by the
   client. Material like this can be **worse than starting from scratch**, because it anchors the
   draft to the wrong thing.

The opening sets each clarity dimension's starting value from this judgement, per source.
"The site's look is current; its message isn't" is a normal result.

**Validation questions, not open ones,** for clients with material. Questions exist to place the
material on the spectrum, never to have them retype what we already have. For example: "Your site
says you cater events of up to 200. Still right?" Answers should be a choice where possible.
Open questions are kept for the gaps.

**Material shapes the outcome question too.** With material in hand, the outcome becomes "what do
you want to change?". The answer includes **constraints the client holds on purpose**, which the
consultant may disagree with. Example (Sarah, Joyful Culinary): her contact form is a 6–8 page
client intake, which clearly narrows her funnel. She keeps it deliberately: it qualifies leads, and
she has limited time to consult. That's a reasonable position. The consultant raises its concern
once ([[DOC-35]] §8.5), records the constraint with its reason, and from then on designs within it.
For instance, it might set expectations about the intake on the page, rather than proposing a
lighter form again. Recorded constraints are client decisions in the §4 sense: the host's opinions
respect them.

**Cohort 2's read-back is part of the opening,** not its own stage: "Here's what I see, and the
three things costing you enquiries."

**The route line** is for fluent clients only; cohort zero is just led. It's one proposal with a
default, said the way a consultant would say it. **The client doesn't know what our exercises are**,
so each one is named in plain terms: what it does for them and how long it takes. The default is
**grounded in their own material**, quoted back. The operator's wording:

> "Here's how I'd run this. Your look is sorted. At this point there are two exercises I'd normally
> run with clients. The story board helps us agree on a headline message for your site. The
> comparison board is where I show you some related websites and you tell me what strikes you
> about them; it helps me understand what you expect to see and how you want it to look. Each takes
> about 10–15 minutes.
> If you like the existing look of your site and the headline there, 'Fast response — reliable
> work', we can just roll with that and I'll build a first draft."

Rules this shows:
- **Never name an exercise without saying what it's for.** "Comparison board" and "headline" are
  our words.
- **Make the skip concrete:** "roll with *this* headline and *this* look", quoted from their
  material, so skipping is a choice about something they can see.
- **Give the time cost of each option.**

Asking it outright instead ("Do you want to do the comparison board?") leaves them to work out what
each exercise is for.

**Social and document sources** (decided 2026-10-26):
- **Documents:** uploads now, and the Google Drive file picker.
- **Social:** start with **guided exports**: step-by-step instructions for Facebook's and
  Instagram's "Download your information" and LinkedIn's data export, then upload the zip.
  People will do it, and it needs no approval from anyone.
- **Public link fetches:** best effort only. The platforms block automated visits.
- **Official API access (OAuth)** comes later, with marketing support (posting, and tracking how
  posts perform), when we can credibly apply for Meta's app review. Too early now.
- **Never ask for a password.** Its own epic: sources, access, consent, formats.

### 12.2 Getting to know them: the material track (walked 2026-10-26)

**The minimum before a first draft comes from the site's purpose, not a small-business template.**
"What, where, when, how much, plus one piece of proof" fits a diner. It doesn't fit the operator's
son's music community:
- a core site for the community;
- satellite sites for each musician and band.

These sites have no locations or prices. A musician's page may be nearly all pictures and
recordings, with very little text. So the minimum is a judgement drawn from the outcome and the
client's expectations: **what must a visitor be able to find or do on this site?** For a diner that's
hours, location and prices. For a musician it's who they are, something to hear, something to see,
and how to follow or book them. The consultant works this out per site, and it sets the material
list per page.

*Networks of sites* (a hub plus satellites): for now each is a separate site, built on its own, and
they link to each other. Anything more (shared identity or material) is a later discussion.

**Questions during builds: keep them, and widen them.** They're a good use of the wait, provided the
client feels their time is well spent. So the consultant is open about each question: why it's
asking and what the answer will change. The topics aren't limited to stories. They can be:
- layout and structure;
- what goes on which page;
- finishing touches and special effects, e.g. the hero motion (Old Scratch's animated flames).

**One expected answer per turn.** Many short turns, each a single question. A question can offer
facets ("tell me about a time when A happened, or B, or you felt C"), but it expects one answer.
Several threads at once are hard to keep going in a conversation.

**Material quality is discovered; a declaration is evidence, not the final word.** "It's all on the
site" counts, but it can be overturned. Material often proves type 2 only as you go: "Oh, we changed the hours… the pricing is
actually old… and I've never really liked that headline; we've evolved over the years." So:
- the material judgement is a **hypothesis with a confidence**, held per source and per dimension;
- **validate the items most likely to be stale first**: hours, prices, team, headline and
  positioning, anything dated;
- **downgrade a source when contradictions turn up.** One stale fact makes neighbouring facts
  suspect, and the consultant says so ("Since the hours changed, is the menu still current?");
- a downgrade is a clarity re-judgement (§3.1) and can reopen a skipped stage.

With type-1 material confirmed, the bulk handover is the default: "It's all in there; just ask me
what's missing" skips the interview straight to gaps.

### 12.3 The comparison board (walked 2026-10-26)

**Purpose:** find out what the client expects their site to look and feel like by having them react
to real sites, and where they stand against others.
**Produces:** the direction card, the stage's last move (a clear end), not a separate stage.
**Dimensions:** audience and position, plus look. **Time:** 10–15 minutes.

**Who picks the sites:**
- Ask for the client's own references first, including sites outside their field.
- Then **add ours, especially when their list is short**. The board needs **variety of style and
  content**, and choosing that mix is the consultant's judgement.
- For cohort zero the answer is usually no, and the consultant picks them all.
- A non-business client's "related sites" are sites they admire, e.g. other artists for a band.
- Cohort 2's own site goes on the board as the anchor.

**Board size:** 3 minimum, 4 typical, 6 maximum.

**Reacting without typing:**
- **Skipping a site must feel safe.** A visible "skip this one" that costs nothing and needs no
  explanation.
- **A short series of multiple-choice questions per site** replaces the free-text note as the main
  input. The choices are specific to that site: the consultant names what's distinctive about it
  ("the big food photo up top", "the dark, moody colours", "menu right on the home page"). The
  client picks what they like and what they don't, plus a general "not us".
- **Free text stays available** as the richest input, never required.

**What the card must not do:** decide page order. It describes look and feel and what the client
wants seen first. Section order belongs to the site plan (the Hal's-counter lesson).

**Adapts:**
- client references make up most of the board;
- cohort 2 adds peers for diagnosis ("what the place down the road does that you don't");
- if the look is already clear, the stage is skipped, with the route line naming the look being
  kept.

**Signals:**
- **Clear:** they can say what they liked and why, and the card is hearted without changes.
- **Revisit:** the draft's feel is disliked, or they keep pointing at another site ("can it be more
  like X?").

### 12.4 The story board (walked 2026-10-26)

**A headline must be agreed to move forward.** This is the one firm rule of the stage. The first
draft isn't built until the client has agreed a headline. A stand-in the consultant put in a draft
isn't agreement, and "move on if nothing lands" is dropped (resolves [[DOC-70]] D-6).

**An empty headline is a valid answer only when it's chosen.** Some sites carry no text at all, for
example a musician's page of pictures and recordings. That's fine as a clear, client-informed
choice ("no headline; the photos speak"), recorded as such. It's never a silent default.

**One headline, and it answers "why you?"** Keep it simple. The rest of the story board (about,
proof, the lines that carry the message) is **flexible**: fill in as much as this site needs, which
may be none. Anything not covered here comes from the material track.

**Three options,** differing in angle, not just wording. One is marked as the consultant's pick, with
its reason.

**Editing the options is critical.** The client can edit any option on the card ("like the second
one, but…"), and the edited text is their choice. This exists today (used on Dina's); it must stay
first-class and be named when the board is offered.

**Adapts:**
- **Existing headline:** quoted as one of the three, with a validation question ("still you, or has
  it drifted?").
- **Message already clear:** the route line quotes the headline being kept, and the client agrees to
  it there.
- **Not a business:** an identity line rather than a pitch.

**Signals:**
- **Clear:** chosen or edited without hesitation, or stated crisply when asked.
- **Revisit:** the headline changes twice or more, or "that doesn't sound like us".

### 12.5 The design (walked 2026-10-26)

**No extra step.** Don't show a style card for approval before the draft. Change is cheap, and the
Looks stage exists for comparing alternatives. Build it, and let the client react to the real
thing.

**Talk about a seed only if the client agreed it.** If the seed came from an explicit client
choice, raising a change to it is right, and asking first is required (§4.1, client provenance).
If it was ours (a host default, or derived from the logo), just make the better call silently.
Narrating overrides of our own defaults reads as noisy and indecisive.

**A brand guide is followed, subject to the material spectrum.**
- A **type-1** guide (current, liked, theirs) is followed as closely as we can. Its colours, fonts
  and logo rules become constraints the client holds on purpose, and departing from them needs
  their agreement.
- A **type-2** guide (out of date, disliked, inconsistent with the business today) is evidence, not
  law, like any other type-2 material.

**Gate class:** a design must exist before builders run (guardrail). Its contents are opinion.

### 12.6 The site plan (walked 2026-10-26)

**Purpose:** which pages there are, what each is for, and what the home page shows first.
**Produces:** the page list (`pages_navigation`), the home page's section order (owned here, not by
the direction card), and a material list per page.

**Check the plan before building it.** Recent runs went: plan, build, find the home page too long,
rework. The plan is assessed against the other rules (length, what the home page must answer, the
material per page) **before** the first build, so the build doesn't need reworking. Anticipate
rather than repair.

**Short, broad sites are a preference, not a rule.** Recent sites have a home page that works as a
discovery page, with other pages carrying the detail. That suits them, but it's a style. The rule
is only to anticipate structure and build so that rework isn't needed.

**Shown in chat.** No new surface. The consultant presents the plan as a short message: the pages,
one line each on what they're for, and the home page's order.

**Mode:** cohort zero is **told and invited to correct** ("Here's the plan: … Say if you'd split it
differently"). Client-driven gets **options with a recommendation**. This applies to every
decision of this kind, not just the plan.

**Adapts:**
- a brief's page list is taken as given;
- cohort 2 inherits its sitemap, then keeps or restructures it according to the opening's
  diagnosis;
- a single-page site is a valid plan, and the length budget follows the plan.

**Gate class:** a plan exists before builders run (guardrail). Visitor-first ordering, the length
budget and the page count are opinions: defaults the client can overrule.

**Later growth:** if material outgrows a page mid-project, cohort zero gets it done and explained
("I've moved the catering details onto their own page so the home page stays quick").
Client-driven gets it as a recommendation.

### 12.7 The first draft (walked 2026-10-26)

**Real duration and cost.** The first-draft message's turn (from `turn_log` and `request_spend`):

| Run | Minutes | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Dina 2 | 29 | $3.63 |
| Dina 4 | 45 | $10.14 (hit the ceiling) |
| Dina 5 | 33 | $7.90 |

So plan for **30–45 minutes**, sometimes 50, not 15. The client is told the truth.

**Budget the first draft generously.** Better first drafts are the main cost lever. The operator
puts building a site at about **$50 today**, with a target of **about $20**. If the first draft
can be ~60% of the work (~$12 at target), getting it right first time pays back in every later
round. So:
- the per-message spend ceiling for the **first-draft message is raised** above the general $10
  (it should never be what stops a first draft);
- its time budget is set to the real 30–50 minutes.

The ceiling stays a runaway backstop, not a rationing device.

**The first draft is the same for every cohort.** Cohorts 1 and 2 reach it faster (stages confirmed
or skipped), but the draft itself isn't rougher or different in kind.

**When should a draft be rough?** Only when we don't know enough. Even then the better move is
usually **more discovery and planning before building**, not a rough build. Rule: the first draft
is never deliberately rough. If the consultant isn't confident in the plan, it keeps
talking. A known gap becomes a clear placeholder, not a guess.

**Watching the site being built is a key experience.**
- **Running commentary is good:** "I'm starting on the Menu page [Menu page]", "I've finished the
  first draft of the Menu page [Menu page]".
- **Nothing is claimed finished until the end, when it really is.** "Ready" is reserved for the
  hand-over.
- **The client can give feedback during the build,** and the system takes it. That's a major
  advantage, and the commentary should invite it where useful.

**Placeholders, not plausible fill.** Missing material shows as a clear placeholder in the preview
("[A photo of the counter goes here]"), so gaps are obvious. Placeholders are **mechanically
checked on a publish attempt**: a site with placeholders can't publish until they're resolved or
removed.

**Placeholders are linked to questions.** Every placeholder corresponds to an open question on the
board: a request for material ("a photo of the counter") or a specific answer ("the name of the car
rally"). Answering the question fills the placeholder. The publish check and the board count the
same set, so they never disagree. In the hand-over, "Still to fill" links to the board.

**"Still to fill" has three causes, and the hand-over says which:**
1. **We ran out of budget or time.** The consultant knows what to do but needs another turn. **This
   is a failure on our side**, especially for cohort zero. It's avoided by the generous budget
   above, and it's never presented as the client's to-do.
2. **Decisions the client needs to make** ("Do you publish catering prices, or quote per event?").
3. **Material or answers only the client has** (a photo, the name of the car rally).

Only 2 and 3 belong in "Still to fill". If 1 happens, the consultant says so plainly and carries
on. It doesn't dress it up as a gap.

**Time target:** a first draft shouldn't take much more than ~30 minutes. Measure over the next few
builds before tuning further.

**Our own review before hand-over** stays (guardrail), **time-boxed**:
1. fix the serious findings;
2. hand over;
3. anything left becomes a proposal or an explained change under §4.1's mode rules.

**The hand-over message** (simpler than today's five-part lead with three to five links). Example:

> Your first draft is ready: [Home page]
> - **Built:** Home, Menu, Our story and Catering, in your red and teal, with the steak and eggs
>   at the top.
> - **Still to fill:** a photo of the counter for Catering, and how far you deliver. Both are
>   marked on the page, and they're your [two questions](#show=questions).
> - **Look first at:** the Home page on your phone. It's what drivers coming off the 80 will see.
>   [View on your phone]
>
> Does the top of the Home page feel like Dina's?

That's four parts, with at most two or three links, ending in one question that matters.

### 12.8 Refinement (walked 2026-10-26)

**Scope:** finalising the basics: messaging, layout, colours and core assets. **Finishing touches**
(movement, special effects, hero video) are a **separate stage** after refinement (§12.9, to walk).

**Principle: the first draft is (almost) the best we can do with the materials and decisions we
have.** The first draft, plus what we've asked the client for (the open questions), plus
finishing touches, should be enough to publish **from the consultant's point of view**.
Refinement is not where our own quality work gets done; that was the first draft's job (§12.7).
So our own improvements in this stage are rare, and governed by §4.1.

**What refinement is, by cohort:**
- **Cohort zero:** closing the gap. They supply the missing pieces and maybe a few tweaks.
- **The more opinionated client:** this is where they try things out. "The logo larger and
  centred, but what does that do to the navigation?" The consultant shows the consequence (build
  it, and use Looks for a side-by-side) rather than debating it in the abstract.

**Several changes in one message: all in one batch,** reported item by item.

**The client decides when refinement is done.** The client has to agree to move on. The consultant
can say it thinks the site is ready, but can't declare it so.

**What the consultant asks everyone, before moving on:**
1. **Look at it for real:** in a real browser tab, and on their phone (View on your phone), and
   bring up anything that bothers them.
2. **Use Marked Points to be specific:** point at the thing on the page rather than describe it.
3. **"Is anything missing?"**
4. **"Does it meet your standard, compared with the sites we looked at on the comparison board? If
   not, why not?"** This closes the loop with stage 3. For a client who skipped the comparison
   board, ask against the sites they named, or the ones they admire.

These are asked once, together, when the consultant thinks the site is ready, and again only if
the answers lead to more changes. They replace today's per-check readiness questions
(`layout_happy`, `quality_bar_met`). The answers are recorded as the readiness decision.

**Guardrails that still hold:**
- broken layout gets fixed;
- the promise hold (don't say it and not do it);
- never undo something the client chose;
- placeholders block publishing.

**Signals:**
- **Converging:** fewer, smaller requests each round.
- **Wandering:** the same thing changes back and forth, or the requests keep growing. That's a
  clarity signal: reopen the stage behind it (§3.1) rather than keep polishing.

### 12.9 Finishing touches (walked 2026-10-26)

**Why it matters:** this is a main difference between a $20 builder site and a premium build.
Fade-ins are a commodity; a story-carrying effect (Old Scratch's flames, the XGD SVG effect) isn't.
The failure mode is **overdoing it**.

**Baseline: one signature effect, plus entrance reveals.**

**The signature effect moves into the first draft (§12.7),** but **discussed with the client
first**. It should ideally use their own images and bring them to life ("Your photo of the
counter: I could have the coffee steam gently and the bell catch the light. Want to see it?").
- **Cohort zero:** a short, enthusiastic suggestion, then go ahead unless they object.
- **Client-driven:** offered with a recommendation.

The hero is the most stable part of the page once the comparison board and story board are done.
The clip can be generated in parallel with the build (no added wait), at small cost, and it lands
the excitement when the client first sees the site.

**Everything else stays at the end:** secondary effects, reveals tuned to the final layout. Motion
on sections that are still changing is wasted.

**Taste tests:**
1. The motion carries the story or the brand.
2. It's clearly real or clearly art, never the uncanny middle; and real mustn't be dull.
3. It respects the page: protected text contrast, no competing loops.

**Making the clip** (lessons from Dina's steak and eggs):
- **An eye-catcher that ends on the original image,** followed by **subtle ongoing motion**
  (steam). The strong action plays once; ongoing motion stays gentle, because strong continuous
  motion wears out ("I couldn't leave that page open").
- **The last frame must be clean:** no unfinished actions or motion blur.
- **Generating from the finished photo adds to it** (more pepper on already-peppered eggs).
  Account for the end state.
- **The ongoing element needs room in the frame.**

**Cost:** not a concern at current prices, so no retry limit. Describing the clip first is part of
the conversation above, not a cost gate.

**The vocabulary and the dos and don'ts** come from the motion survey owned by [[EPIC-26]], now written up as [[DOC-72]] (pattern catalogue §2, revised tests §3, don'ts §4). It
becomes the stage guide the consultant talks the client through.

### 12.10 Publishing (walked 2026-10-26)

**A readiness tool that runs the publish button's own checks.** The consultant gets a tool that runs
exactly the checks publishing runs (one implementation, two callers), so it can tell the client
where they stand and guide them through what's left:
- an address has been chosen (a key check);
- no placeholders remain (§12.7);
- the client has agreed to move on (§12.8);
- guardrails: no broken layout, and the contact form tested end to end.

**The settings hand-off stalls today.** The consultant rightly links to the Settings page to choose
an address, and then:
- a cohort-zero client is left looking at it, unsure what to do;
- the Settings page's assistant sits dormant, waiting for input, with no idea why they've arrived;
- nothing points them back to where they came from, so they feel stranded.

**Decision: a warm hand-off between specialist sessions.** The product will have several tabs with
their own assistants:
- Site (the builder);
- Settings (addresses, and pointing a registrar like GoDaddy at us);
- Payments;
- Marketing;
- Support.

Keeping their contexts separate is right: a payments expert doesn't need site-building tools or
instructions. (This mirrors XGD's epic/ticket split, which keeps contexts small.) To make the
separation invisible to the client:
1. **The hand-off carries a packet:** why they're coming ("choose an address for Dina's Diner so
   the site can publish"), where they came from, and the shared client profile.
2. **The receiving assistant speaks first** and takes over: "You're here to pick the address
   people will type to find Dina's. There are two ways…". It never waits silently.
3. **There's always a way back:** when the task is done, the receiving assistant returns them
   ("Your address is set. Back to your site [Site]"), and the builder's consultant is told, so it
   carries on ("Your address is in; the site is ready to publish").
4. **One voice to the client.** It's the same team throughout; the assistants don't refer to each
   other as other people (as the talker/worker rule already says).

**A shared client profile across sessions.** Some of the profile belongs to the **person** and is
shared by every tab: how they like to be guided, their vocabulary, their word for what they run.
Some belongs to the **site** and stays with the builder: clarity per dimension, route decisions,
material. §9.1's profile splits along that line.

**Scope for the beta:** everything a tester needs to get their site **live on one of our domains**
(a `.1stc.site` address). Fast follows, owned elsewhere:
- **pointing an existing domain at the site:** groundwork exists ([[EPIC-5]] DNS management,
  [[EPIC-4]] Settings);
- **buying a white-label domain** through Cloudflare and Stripe ([[EPIC-6]] registrar, [[EPIC-9]]
  billing).

**The publishing moment (draft).** The words go **in the chat**, from the Site consultant; by now
it is the relationship. No popup: popups interrupt, get dismissed on reflex, and read as the software
talking. On screen there's a **non-modal "Live" strip** above the preview: "Live at
dinasdiner.1stc.site · Open ↗ · Copy link". It stays visible.

Draft message for a cohort-zero client:

> **Dina's Diner is live:** [dinasdiner.1stc.site ↗]
>
> Here's what happens now:
> - **When someone fills in your contact form**, it comes to your email and appears in [Contacts].
>   You'll see who they are and what they asked.
> - **To share it**, send the link to regulars and friends. Here's a ready-made post for Facebook if
>   you'd like one: *"We've got a new website! Hours, the menu and directions off the 80, all in one
>   place: dinasdiner.1stc.site"* [Copy]
> - **Want to change something later?** Just come back and tell me. Changes stay in your draft until
>   you publish again, so you can try things without anyone seeing them.
>
> Who would you like to send it to first?

Notes:
- **Open with the live link** (it opens in a new tab).
- **"What happens now"** answers cohort zero's real worry: someone used my site, so where did that
  go?
- **The share post** is in their words, with their actual address, and is skipped if they have no
  social media.
- **The draft line** must be checked against how publishing actually works before it's promised.
- **End on a warm, easy question,** not a task.

**Client-driven version:** the link, where enquiries go, and "publish again whenever you change
things".

**Still open:** the next step after launch.

## 13. The client profile is written in neutral terms

The profile describes **what the client prefers and needs, never a judgement of them.** Write
every entry as if the client will read it. They may: [[DOC-5]] gives them access to and export of
their data.

| Instead of | Write |
|---|---|
| low fluency, novice | uses everyday words for web things; explain web terms |
| anxious, scared of technology | prefers to be asked before changes; values reassurance |
| directed / passive | prefers the consultant to lead |
| unclear, confused about messaging | message not settled yet; story board recommended |
| difficult, pushy | has firm preferences; present options with a recommendation |

- **Cohort numbers and persona names are internal design vocabulary**, not stored labels on a
  person.
- **Mode, register and clarity are stored as preferences and needs with their evidence.** For
  example: "prefers the consultant to lead (said 'just do what you think')".
- **This applies everywhere the profile appears:** stored fields, the consultant's own notes, and
  hand-off packets.
