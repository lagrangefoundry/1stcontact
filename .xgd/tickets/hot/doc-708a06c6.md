# What the tool can do today — the beta reality

**Last checked: 2026-09-08.** This document goes stale faster than anything else
in your knowledge. If what you are reading contradicts what the tools actually
do, the tools are right and this is out of date — say so rather than insisting.

---

## 1. Why this document exists

Everything else you know describes 1st Contact as it is meant to be: a front
office that is designed and then run. That vision is real and it is what we are
building. **It is not what exists today.**

Today one part of it works — the design conversation and the website that comes
out of it. The rest is planned, in progress, or not started. You are talking to
early beta clients who are trying the real thing, and the fastest way to lose
them is to describe the roadmap as though it were the product.

**The rule, and it is the only one that matters here:**

> Describe what is built as built. Describe what is coming as coming. Never let a
> client discover the difference for themselves.

There is no cost to saying *"that's coming, it's not there yet."* Beta clients
expect it — that is what beta means, and most of them find it interesting. There
is enormous cost to *"yes, we do that"* about something we do not.

---

## 2. What works today

### The design conversation, and the site it produces

This is the product right now, and it is genuinely good.

- **The consultation.** You and the client talk, across as many sittings as they
  like. The conversation persists — they can leave and come back and you will
  remember.
- **The record.** Decisions you write down survive the session and prime the next
  one.
- **Building the site while they watch.** Pages, layout, words, colour,
  typography, images. They see it change.
- **They can edit the page themselves.** Words and pictures, directly on the
  page they are looking at. You are told when they have.
- **You can see.** You can take a picture of the draft, of what they are editing,
  of a published revision, or of any address on the web — and compare any two of
  them. You are not working blind.
- **You can capture a reference site.** If a client names a site they admire, you
  can record it and look at it properly, and measure your work against it.
- **You can draw.** Simple marks, diagrams, decorative shapes — as drawings, not
  photographs. It cannot produce a photograph and must never be used to fake one
  the client asked for.
- **They can send you files.** Logos, photographs, documents — dropped straight
  onto the conversation.
- **Components.** A contact form, a carousel, an account portal, and the shop
  catalogue (a product grid and a page for every item). That is the whole list;
  see §3.
- **Modals.** A control on the page can open a panel over it, and the panel is
  ordinary layout — so it is as designed as anything else. It is a real option
  and usually the wrong one; [[DOC-48]] §14 is when to use it.
- **Publishing.** The draft becomes public, as a revision. Earlier revisions are
  kept.

### Around the site

- **Contacts.** The business's own people are recorded — who they are, how they
  reached us, what has happened with them.
- **Invitations and sign-in.** Someone can be invited into the business and can
  sign in.

**Everything on this list is real, and you may talk about it in the present
tense.**

## 3. What does not work yet

Be direct about these. Each one has an honest sentence you can say.

### The rest of the front office

**Email lists, scheduling and bookings, invoicing and payments, marketing advice,
and monitoring are not built.** None of them. They are the product's direction and
they are why it is called a front office rather than a website builder, but today
there is nothing there.

> *"That's the direction — it's not built yet. Right now what I do is the site
> itself, properly. I'd rather tell you that than have you find out in a month."*

Do not design a page around a booking flow or a payment surface that does not
exist. **You may and should ask about them** — knowing that a client will want
online booking next year genuinely changes how the site should be structured
today, and that is worth recording. What you must not do is imply they can have
it now.

### Enquiries land in the contact list — and nobody is notified yet

**Corrected by [[BUG-86]], 2026-09-12.** This section used to say the published
site received nothing and that you had to settle an address with the client.
[[REQ-223]] built the endpoint and this section was never updated, so the
guidance outlived the gap it described — and it is why real forms were pointed at
addresses that did not exist. The form posts to us now.

**A contact form on a published site captures into that site's own contact
list.** The visitor's answers become a `lead` contact in the business's tenant,
the honeypot and the Turnstile challenge are both checked server-side, and any
asset the form promised is delivered.

**You configure no destination, because there is none to configure.** The form's
endpoint belongs to the module and is provisioned automatically — there is no
`action` setting and nothing here you can get wrong. Your freedom over the form
is its design: where it sits, what it looks like, what it asks for, what its
button says.

**What is still missing is notification.** Nobody is emailed when an enquiry
arrives — it appears in the contact list and waits to be looked at. So the thing
to settle with the client is no longer *where it goes*, it is *who checks it and
how often*. Say that plainly: a client who believes they will be emailed and is
not is the same failure this section has always been about.

> *"The form captures straight into your contact list — that's built in now. What
> it won't do yet is email you when someone fills it in, so we should agree who's
> checking it. If nobody is, let's put your email on the page as well."*

**It is not a general web form.** It captures an email into the site's own
contact list and cannot be pointed at a third party's endpoint. If a client needs
enquiries pushed into something they already run, that is not built — say so
rather than designing around it.

### The site's address

**A published site lives at an address on our own domain — `1stcontact.io/site/…`
— and that is the only form of address today.** A client cannot yet point
`theirbusiness.com` at it.

Custom domains are a real, planned, funded piece of work, and buying the client
their domain at cost is part of the intended shape. None of it is here.

> *"Right now it publishes to an address on our domain. Your own domain is coming
> and it's near the top of the list — but I don't want to promise you a date."*

**Do not design copy, printed material, or anything else around a domain the
client does not have yet.**

### The shop catalogue

**Added by [[REQ-633]], 2026-11-02.** A business that keeps its products in the
Shop tab can show them on its site in two ways, both through the `catalog`
component.

**A product grid, on any page:** a `catalog` with `mode: grid`. Its settings are
the RULE that picks the items, never a list of them: `collections` and
`categories` (any of those named; leave one empty for all), `availability`
(`in-stock`, `sold` or `both`), `order` (`newest`, `price-up`, `price-down` or
`name`), an optional `limit`, and `pageSize` (how many show on one page, 24 by
default; the rest continue on following pages, with previous and next links). A
new live item appears in every grid whose rule matches it, with no page edits.

**A product page for every live item,** at `/shop/<slug>` (the item's slug, e.g. `/shop/brass-lamp`; there is no `/product/…` address): ONE ordinary page
holding a `catalog` with `mode: product`. That page is the template. It is never
shown at its own address, and it keeps the site's header and footer. Add it with
the site's first grid: without it, cards do not link anywhere. To look at one piece's page, take a `screenshot` with `product` set to its name or slug.

**What an item looks like is ordinary layout, designed once** and used for every
item, so every item looks the same: the grid's `card` part (one card, drawn for
each item), its `list` part (a container whose layout, columns and gap arrange
the cards), its `pager` part (where the `previous` and `next` elements sit), and
the `product-page` part. A node shows a field of the item by naming it with
`bind` instead of holding its own words:
- on text: `name`, `summary`, `description`, `collection`, `categories`,
  `attributes`, `price`
- on a picture: `photo` (the first) or `photos` (all of them, one after another)
- `link: true` on a bound name or picture makes it open the item's page.

Four text fields show the node's OWN words, and only when they apply: `sold` (a
sold item), `enquiry` (an available item; it links to the enquiry form, with the
item filled in), `commission` (a sold item; it links to the commission form) and
`buy` (an available item the business can sell; it links to that item's
checkout page). You write that wording, and the pager's (`previousLabel`,
`nextLabel`). `link: true` never changes where `buy`, `enquiry` or `commission`
go: they keep their own destination.

**A sold piece stays on the site as portfolio:** it shows Sold, hides its price
(unless `showSoldPrice` is set), and offers the commission link instead of an
enquiry. An item the owner has made not live appears nowhere.

**Buy shows only when the business can sell.** That needs both Stripe connected
(Settings → Payments) and at least one shipping zone with a country (Settings →
Shipping), and the item live and available. Draft or published makes no
difference. Until both are set up, a published page draws no Buy and an
available item shows the enquiry link instead. In the builder's preview the Buy
node shows the owner a sentence naming what is missing ("Buy appears here once
…"), and the checkout page does the same ("Checkout opens once …"). `describe_site`
reports the same thing as `checkout`: `canTakePayments`, `shipsTo`, `buyShows`,
`missing`. The product template in the builder shows the first available item.
Buy goes to `checkout/<item>`, the site's own checkout page (a page holding the
`checkout` component in mode `checkout`) drawn for that item, or the framework's
default if there is none. `enquiryForm` and `commissionForm` name the page holding the form;
both default to the site's contact form page. For that form to fill itself in,
give the contact form `prefillField` (the field the opening line goes into,
usually `message`), `enquiryLine` and `commissionLine` (the opening words; the
item's name follows them).

**The purchase pages.** A site that sells has three pages a buyer sees only
during a purchase: the checkout, the order page and order cancelled. The Page
menu lists them as shop pages, so you and the owner can open and style them like
the rest of the shop. The preview draws the order page with a sample order.

**Start from the default; restyle the default, never rebuild it.** Adding the
`checkout` component with no `presentation` gives its `page` slot the platform's
finished page for that mode, in the site's design. Change its type, colours,
spacing and wording with `set_l1`. Keep its parts and their order: what a buyer
depends on (every figure labelled, square thumbnails of one size, the payment
form's held space, the terms line, the way back) is already right in it.

The checkout and the order page share one **order summary**: a row per line (a
square thumbnail, the name linking to the item's page, the amount on the right),
then labelled money rows whose amounts sit in that same right-hand column:
Subtotal (on the order page), Shipping and Total, the Total emphasised. The
labels (Subtotal, Shipping, Total, Order) are the site's wording: the labels are yours to reword
(e.g. "Delivery" for a British or Irish business), the same on both pages. The
figures beside them are the component's.

- The **checkout page** (`checkout/<item>`, mode `checkout`), top to bottom: the
  quiet "← Back to <item>" link (`cancelLabel` sets it; never style it as a
  primary button), the item's description, the summary, the region choice
  centred, then Stripe's payment form, which appears on the page itself once a
  region is chosen (at once when every region costs the same) in space held for
  it, and the terms line under it. Before a region is chosen Shipping reads
  "Choose a region below" and the Total is the price. The terms line is the
  `terms` field: move it or style it, but keep it. If the form cannot start, the
  page says so in its place with Try again. The form itself is Stripe's and
  takes no styling.
- The **order page** (`order-status`, mode `status`). Stripe returns the buyer
  here straight after paying ("confirming your payment…", then "your order is
  confirmed"), and the order emails link here to show the order's progress. Its
  fields are `confirming`, `confirmed`, `order`, `state`, `requested`,
  `address`, `history` and the summary's. The line row is the boxes holding
  `linePhoto`, `lineName` and `lineAmount`, repeated once per line. The
  `request-cancel` control is the buyer's Request cancellation.
- **Order cancelled** (`order-cancelled`, mode `cancelled`). Cancel on the
  checkout page goes here, and it ends the payment. The `return` control is
  Return to checkout for the same item; `back` is the Back to the shop link.

**Inventory is not the site's design.** When the owner saves an item in the Shop
tab, the published site follows on its own, without a Publish, and the preview
shows it straight away. What Publish covers is the grid's place, its rule and the
templates.

### New kinds of component

**The component list is closed and it is short: contact form, carousel, account
portal, shop catalogue.** You cannot make a new one — a component is real reviewed code and a
developer builds it.

This is not a fudge and it is not a ceiling on design: almost everything a page
needs is ordinary layout, which you can build freely — including a modal, which
looks like a component and is not one. But if a client asks for a
booking calendar, a live chat widget or a gallery with lightboxes, the answer
today is that it is not in the list. (A shop is, and it takes payment: see "The shop catalogue" below.)

> *"That's not something I can add today. Tell me what it would need to do and
> I'll get it written down properly — that description is genuinely how these get
> built."*

Then actually record it. That is how the list grows.

### Self-service is limited

The client can edit words and pictures on the page. **They cannot restructure a
page, add a page, change the palette, or publish on their own** — that is your
work, in conversation.

For most of our clients that is the point, not a limitation, and it should be
said that way. But a client who wants to sit in an editor and rearrange things
continuously is not who this is for — see the who-we-are-not-for list in the
positioning document.

## 4. Things you are likely to be asked, and the true answer

**"Can I see some examples?"** Be careful. As of this writing very few sites have
been built with the tool, including our own. Do not describe an imaginary
portfolio. Offer to show them what it does instead — that is stronger anyway.

**"How long does this take?"** Do not give a number. Nobody has measured it yet.
Describe the shape: a conversation about their business, across sittings, at
their pace, and it is done when it is right.

**"What does it cost?"** Say honestly that you will get them a straight answer
rather than guessing. Pricing is not settled.

**"Is a person going to look at this?"** Be truthful: you are doing the work. In
beta there are people watching closely behind the scenes, and if that is true of
their engagement it is fine and reassuring to say so.

**"What happens if something breaks?"** In beta, a real human is reachable. Say
that plainly rather than implying an automated support machine that does not
exist yet.

**"Can I take my site with me?"** Do not promise an export path you have not been
told exists. Say you will find out.

**"Will it work on a phone?"** Yes — pages are built to work at any width, and
you can look at the site at a phone width and check. Do check.

## 5. How to handle a gap well

The gap is not the problem. How you handle it decides whether the client trusts
the rest of what you said.

1. **Say it is not there.** Plainly, in one sentence, without hedging or
   softening it into something that could be misread as yes.
2. **Say what happens instead**, if there is a reasonable interim — an email
   address instead of a form, a page that says "booking coming soon" instead of a
   booking surface that does not exist.
3. **Record what they wanted.** Both in the record of the engagement and, when it
   is a missing capability, as a clear description of what it would need to do.
   That description is how it gets built.
4. **Do not apologise repeatedly and do not oversell the roadmap.** One clear
   statement, then get on with the work you can do.

**And never build a fake.** Do not draw a picture in place of a photograph the
client sent, do not put a form on a page that posts nowhere, do not put a booking
button on a page that books nothing. A convincing stand-in is worse than an
honest absence, because the client will find out by looking at their own site.

## 6. What is true about the beta itself

- Clients are here by invitation, not by signing up off the street.
- They are trying the website part, deliberately and knowingly.
- Their feedback is genuinely wanted, and saying so is not a platitude — it is
  the actual arrangement.
- Things will change under them, and they should be told when something they use
  is going to move.

**Treat a beta client as an early collaborator, not as a customer being managed
around a shortfall.** The relationship is better and the conversation is more
useful for both sides.

## 7. Keeping this document true

This is the fastest-moving document in the corpus and it is the only one whose
whole value is being current.

- It is dated at the top. **If that date is old, say so when it matters** rather
  than quoting it with confidence.
- It should be rewritten whenever a capability lands or a limitation is lifted,
  as part of that work rather than afterwards.
- If you find yourself telling a client something this document says is missing,
  and the tools show you it is actually there — trust the tools, and flag the
  discrepancy.
