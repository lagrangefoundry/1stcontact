# Bob's Salon: the story

The full backstory, for reference, followed by the same story as Bob
would actually tell it. It's consistent with `README.md` and `services.md`.

---

## The story

Bob Kearney grew up on Old Harbor Street in South Boston, the youngest of
five. His mother did her friends' hair in the kitchen on Saturday mornings,
and Bob, aged nine, handed her the rollers and listened to all the gossip.

At sixteen Bob grew his hair down past his shoulders. He was in a band, and
it was 1994. His father didn't speak to him for a month. Every barber in the
neighbourhood either wanted it off or did a hack job. A woman's salon on
Broadway took him in, mostly as a joke, and the stylist, an older woman
named Dolores, cut it beautifully. She told him, "Long hair is long hair. It
doesn't know if you're a boy."

After high school he went to cosmetology school in Boston, mostly because of
Dolores. The band broke up and the hair stayed. He spent eleven years
behind the chair at salons downtown and on Newbury Street. He was good at
it and hated the atmosphere: the upsell, the price list with "men's" and
"women's" columns, and clients made to feel scruffy for walking in in work
clothes. Downtown he became the guy people were sent to for long hair,
men especially, because nobody else wanted them.

In 2012 he took the second floor above a bakery on East Broadway, a former
insurance office with one sink. He opened with two chairs and one rule:
price by the hair, not the person. His mother did the reception the first
year. The old Southie ladies came because they knew his mother. The long-hair
guys came because they'd been looking for him for years.

Marisol joined in 2015 from a salon in JP, and brought colour and curls.
Twee rented the nail station in 2017. She was meant to stay a year and is
still there. Aoife arrived from Galway in 2021 and turned the old storage
room into a facial room. Her "come here, you've got dry skin" pitch to men
waiting for haircuts became a whole part of the business.

The free Wigs for Kids cuts started in 2013, after Bob's niece was treated
for leukaemia at Children's. The wall of photos by the front desk has over
three hundred kids on it now.

Bob is 48. His hair is still long, now grey and in a ponytail. His mother is
84, still gets her hair done every Friday at 11, and still tells everyone
in the chair what Bob was like at nine.

## As Bob would tell it (paste-ready)

**Why long hair**
> I had hair to my shoulders at sixteen. I was in a band, it was the
> nineties. No barber in Southie would touch it without trying to talk me
> into a crew cut. Dolores on Broadway cut it, and she said "long hair
> is long hair, it doesn't know if you're a boy". I've basically built a
> business on that sentence.

**His mother**
> My ma did hair in our kitchen every Saturday. I was the kid handing her
> rollers. She did reception for me the first year we opened. Still
> comes in every Friday at eleven. Don't book a Friday at eleven, that's
> her chair.

**Why he went independent**
> I did eleven years downtown and on Newbury. Good money, but I hated it.
> Men's price, women's price, upsell, upsell. Somebody comes in in work
> clothes and they get the look. I wanted a place where a guy from the
> Gillette plant and a lawyer from the Seaport sit next to each other and
> nobody blinks.

**Opening**
> 2012. Second floor over the bakery, one sink, two chairs. I hardly had
> any money. The smell of bread every morning's the best thing about it,
> still.

**The team**
> Marisol came in 2015 and she's the reason we're known for colour. Twee came
> to rent the nail station "for a year". That was 2017. Aoife came from Galway
> in 2021, cleared out my storage room and made it a facial room. Best decision
> I never made.

**Wigs for Kids**
> My niece had leukaemia when she was six. She's fine now, she's at
> college. While she was going through it I said, right, any kid who wants
> to donate their hair, it's free here. There are three hundred photos on
> that wall.

**What it means to him**
> Every week someone sits down and says "I've been scared to let anyone
> touch my hair." When they get up, they're not scared anymore. That's the
> job.
