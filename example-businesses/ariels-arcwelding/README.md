A made-up welding, fabrication and repair shop in Oakland, CA, run by a
metal sculptor. It's the third role-play business, after Charlie's
Plumbing and Bob's Salon. The operator plays Ariel.

## Chat fragments: things Ariel can say

Ariel is direct and dry, and she lights up when talking about metal. She
knows her trade cold and has no patience for marketing language. Paste
these as they are or mix them.

### What we do

> Anything metal that's broken, or doesn't exist yet. Somebody's boat
> rail snapped, somebody's restaurant needs a hood bracket nobody makes
> anymore, a guy at the airport needs a fixture to hold an engine cowling.
> If you can draw it on a napkin, we can make it. If you can't draw it,
> bring me the broken one.

> About half the work is repair and half is making things. Repair pays the
> bills. Making things is why I get up in the morning.

### Who our customers are

> Boat people, a lot of them. Jack London Square, the Alameda marinas,
> Grand Street. Aluminum hulls with cracks, stainless rails, davits, a
> fuel tank that's started weeping. They're in a hurry because the boat's
> in the water and it's costing them money.

> Car people: hot rods, a lot of old trucks, exhaust, the occasional roll
> cage. Contractors who need a gate, a stair stringer or a beam plate made
> to fit something that isn't square. Restaurants. Other artists who can't
> weld but have an idea. And a couple of hangar guys at Oakland and Hayward
> who need tooling and ground equipment.

> I like the jobs where somebody else said "can't be done". That's half my
> work.

### Aircraft (keep it straight)

> I don't weld anything that holds a plane up. That's FAA-certified repair
> station work and I'm not one. What I make is the stuff around the plane:
> engine stands, tow bars, fixtures, brackets for the hangar. Gus machines
> parts to print when there's an A&P mechanic signing off on them. I'm
> careful about that line. People trust you with their lives on aircraft
> work.

### What we're best at

> Aluminum and stainless. Most shops can stick steel together. Not many do
> nice aluminum TIG on a boat that's bobbing up and down at the dock. That's
> where I make my money.

> Making a part that doesn't exist anymore. You bring me the broken bit of
> a 1950s mixer or a boat fitting from a company that went bust in 1985.
> I'll weld it if it can be welded, and if it can't, Gus will make a new one
> from scratch.

### What I did last week

> Monday was a 38-foot sailboat in Alameda with a crack in the aluminum
> stern pulpit. I did it at the dock. My dad would have loved that one. I
> had to time the welds between the wakes from the ferry.

> Wednesday a restaurant on Telegraph needed a stainless prep table with a
> weird corner cut out to go around a column. Built it in a day. They're
> putting it in tonight.

> Thursday Gus made a bushing for a 1962 Ford truck. You can't buy one
> anywhere in the world. He made it from a drawing he did on a paper towel.

> Friday a guy brought in his grandmother's cast-iron garden bench, cracked
> in three places. Cast iron's a pain to weld. It's in one piece now, and he
> cried a bit. You'd be surprised how often that happens in a welding shop.

### The sculptures

> The yard is where I put my weekends. Some of them are huge. There's a
> 25-foot heron made from old boat props and rebar. Some of them you have to
> get up close to. I've got hummingbirds made of forks and bees with spark
> plug bodies. People stop their cars on Kennedy Street to look.

> Old Scratch is the big one, the devil. Thirty feet, built for Burning Man
> in 2022. It's plumbed for oil, so fire comes out of the horns and the
> mouth. It took me eleven months of weekends. We light him up twice a
> year now, at Halloween and the Jingletown open studios, with the fire
> department's blessing.

> The art is the best advertisement I have. People see the yard and think,
> if she can do that, she can fix my gate.

### Gus and Mika

> Gus is sixty-seven and he's the best machinist I've ever met. He did
> forty years, the last twenty in aerospace. He retired for six weeks,
> drove his wife crazy, and walked in here asking if I needed anyone. He
> doesn't do email. He does tolerances to a ten-thousandth of an inch.

> Mika came to my kids' class when she was thirteen and never really left.
> Now she's in the welding programme at Laney and works here three days a
> week. Her TIG beads are better than mine were at her age, and I tell her
> so, rarely.

### Kids' classes

> Saturday mornings I teach kids, ten to sixteen. The little ones do cold
> work: bending, riveting, cutting with hand tools, making robots out of
> bolts. From twelve they can weld with me standing right there. Everyone
> leaves with something they made and a healthy respect for hot metal.

> My dad taught me in his shop when I was eight. Most kids never get to
> touch a real tool. That's what the class is for.

### Things Ariel says when asked web or design questions

> I make things out of metal. You make the website. Just don't make it look
> like a stock-photo welder in a clean shirt. Nobody welds in a clean shirt.

> People need to send me a photo of the broken thing. That's 90% of a
> quote. If they can do that from their phone, I'm happy.

> Put the sculptures on there. That's what people remember.
