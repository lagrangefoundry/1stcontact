# Ariel's Arc Welding: the story

The full backstory, for reference, followed by the same story as Ariel
would actually tell it. It's consistent with `README.md` and `services.md`.

---

## The story

Tomás Sanchez came to San Diego from Ensenada at nineteen and got work at
the shipyards on the bay, welding steel on Navy supply ships. He did fifteen
years there, a lot of it in the bottom of hulls in the summer. When the
yards laid people off in the early nineties, he took his savings and opened
a muffler and fabrication shop in Chula Vista: Sanchez Muffler & Welding.

Ariel was born in 1985, the second of three, and the only one who wanted to
be in the shop. At eight she was sweeping up and sorting bolts. At ten Tomás
let her strike her first arc with his hand over hers. At fourteen she was
doing exhaust hangers on customers' cars, though the customers didn't know.
Tomás taught her the shipyard rule: "If you wouldn't stand under it, don't
weld it."

She was also always drawing. Her mother wanted her to go to college, so she
went, to an art school in Oakland, where she studied sculpture and spent every
night in the metal shop. The professors said her work was "too
industrial". She thought that was a compliment.

After school she worked for six years at a ship-repair yard on the
Alameda side of the estuary, the only woman on her crew. That's where she
learned aluminum and got her structural certifications. She was building
sculptures out of the yard's scrap pile on her lunch breaks, with
permission, mostly.

In 2014 she rented half of an old cannery warehouse in Jingletown, on the
Oakland side of the estuary, just across the water from the yard where
she'd worked. She bought a used Miller TIG welder, a lathe from a retiring
machinist's estate sale, and built the forge herself out of a brake drum and
a hair dryer. Within two years she had the whole building. The first jobs
were boat people from the yard who followed her.

Tomás died in 2019. She has his old stick welder on a shelf in the office.
It still works and she still uses it for some things.

Gus Petrakis walked in in 2018. He'd retired from an aerospace machine shop
in Hayward and lasted six weeks at home. Mika Tan came to the kids' class
in 2019, aged thirteen, and now works three days a week while she studies
welding at Laney.

The sculptures started taking over the yard around 2016. In 2022 she spent
eleven months of weekends building Old Scratch, a 30-foot steel devil plumbed
for oil-fed fire, for Burning Man. It took two flatbeds and a crane to get
it to the playa and back. It came home to the yard and now it's a
neighbourhood landmark. Kids in the class call her "the devil lady", and
she doesn't mind.

The Saturday kids' classes began in 2017, because a neighbour's son kept
hanging over the fence watching her work. Her dad taught her, and she figures
somebody should do the same for the next lot.

## As Ariel would tell it (paste-ready)

**Her dad**
> My dad welded ships in San Diego for fifteen years. Navy supply ships,
> down in the hull, in August. When the yards laid everyone off he opened a
> muffler shop in Chula Vista. I grew up in that shop. First arc at ten, his
> hand on mine.

> He had a rule: "If you wouldn't stand under it, don't weld it." I still
> say it to the kids every Saturday.

**Art school**
> I went to art school in Oakland for sculpture, because my mom wanted me to
> go to college. They said my work was too industrial. I took that as a
> compliment.

**The shipyard**
> After school I worked six years at a ship-repair yard in Alameda, the only
> woman on the crew. I learned aluminum there. I learned a lot of things
> there, actually, including how to not take any nonsense.

**Starting the shop**
> 2014. Half an old cannery in Jingletown, a used TIG welder, a lathe from a
> dead man's garage and a forge I built out of a brake drum. The first
> customers were boat owners from the yard who followed me across the
> water.

**Dad's welder**
> My dad passed in 2019. His old stick welder is on the shelf in the office.
> It still works. Some jobs, I use it on purpose.

**Old Scratch**
> Eleven months of weekends. Thirty feet. Two flatbeds and a crane to get him
> to the playa. When we lit him up out there, that was the best night of my
> life. Now he lives in the yard and scares the delivery drivers.

**Why the kids' classes**
> A neighbour's kid used to hang on the fence watching me weld, every single
> day. One day I just said "come in, put these on." That was the first class.
> My dad did that for me. Somebody has to do it for them.

**What it all means**
> People think welding is just sticking metal together. It's taking
> something broken and making it stronger than it was. That's true of the
> boats, and it was true of my dad's shop, and it's true of me.
